## Background Information

The authors describe meditation as holding attention on immediate experience and away from distractions such as self-referential thinking and :term{id="mind_wandering" case="lower"}. A set of brain regions known as the default mode network (:term{id="dmn"}) is most active when people are left to think to themselves undisturbed or are thinking about themselves, and less active during tasks that take mental effort. Earlier studies, the paper notes, had linked mind wandering to activity in this network, and a meta-analysis had found its activity consistently lower during meditation, in studies of both :term{id="focused_attention" case="lower"} and the repetition of phrases. In a previous study, the same Yale group found that experienced meditators had lower default mode activity than non-meditators across three standard :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditations, and another study found that reduced default mode activity during meditation went with better sustained attention outside the scanner.

Most of those studies compared meditation with rest, and the authors saw a problem with that. Meditators had also been found to differ from non-meditators in the network's activity at rest, measured as :term{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"}. If a meditator lying in the scanner drifts toward meditating whenever asked to rest, rest stops being a neutral comparison:

> These findings introduce a potential confound to studies of meditators comparing meditation to rest, as meditation may transform the resting state into a more meditative state.

Rest also varies a great deal from person to person. So this study compared meditation with an active task as well. Any effortful task lowers default mode activity, so the question was whether meditation lowers it further, beyond the usual dip that comes with paying attention to a task. The team also wanted to repeat their earlier finding in a larger group, since meditation imaging studies had averaged about 12 participants (range 4–31).

## What They Did

The researchers scanned 20 experienced meditators and 26 non-meditators with functional MRI (:term{id="fmri"}). The meditators, recruited through advertisements and word of mouth, all practiced in the Insight tradition of :term{id="theravada"} Buddhism. They reported an average of 9,676 hours of practice over about 14 years, including daily practice and retreats. The controls had never meditated. The groups were matched on sex, race, age and years of education: everyone was White (non-Hispanic), each group was a little over half men, and the average ages were 45.6 and 42.2 years. Six of the meditators and three of the controls had also taken part in the group's earlier study; the authors report that the results look similar if those participants are left out.

Just before scanning, everyone was taught three meditations and practiced them outside the scanner:

- **Concentration:** attending to the physical sensation of the breath, and bringing attention back to it when it wandered.
- **Loving kindness:** recalling a time they genuinely wished someone well, then silently repeating phrases such as "May all beings be happy."
- **Choiceless awareness:** following whatever came into awareness, a thought, emotion or body sensation, until something else arose.

Each of six scanner runs followed the same sequence: 30 seconds of rest with eyes open, 90 seconds of an active task, 30 seconds of rest with eyes closed, a recorded meditation instruction, 3 minutes of meditation and a final 20 seconds of rest. In the active task, adjectives appeared on a screen one at a time, and people pressed a button to answer either "Does the word describe you?" or "Is the word in uppercase letters?" Each meditation was done twice. After each run, participants rated from 0 to 10 how much their mind had wandered during the meditation and how well they could follow the instructions.

For the analysis, all the rest periods were combined into a baseline. The three meditations were pooled into one "meditation" condition, and both kinds of word judgment into one "active task" condition. The key test was a group-by-task interaction: did the difference between meditation and the word task differ between meditators and controls? It was run across the whole brain, with a correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}, and restricted to spots where the interaction was not simply driven by the groups differing overall.

## One Big Result: Only in Meditators Did Two Default Mode Hubs Drop During Meditation and Rise During the Word Task

The group-by-task interaction turned up in several regions: the middle temporal gyrus on both sides, the left :term{id="fusiform_gyrus" case="lower"} and neighboring hippocampal gyrus, the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"} and the :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"}. For the two midline regions, the anterior cingulate and precuneus, the paper describes how each group's activity behaved compared with rest:

> Plots of the parameter estimates for the anterior cingulate cortex and precuneus demonstrate that activity in these brain regions decreases during meditation and increases during the active control task in meditators, whereas controls did not show this dissociation.

In other words, the contrast between meditating and doing the word task was larger in meditators. Part of it came from the word task itself: in meditators, precuneus activity during the word judgments rose above the rest baseline, which the authors suggest may reflect more self-related thinking during the task than during rest. In controls, activity in the precuneus and :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"} was lower than at rest during both meditation and the word task, which the authors read as both tasks drawing people away from the self-related thinking and mind wandering of rest.

The authors conclude that in experienced meditators, meditation lowers default mode activity "beyond that expected by general task-based deactivation," and that for meditators the results fit the idea that meditation may reduce self-related thinking and mind wandering more than another active task. The paper's title says meditation "leads to" this reduction. The design can't show cause and effect, though: the meditators were compared with non-meditators once, so the differences may reflect who chooses to spend years meditating as well as the practice itself. The brain results also cover the three meditations pooled together, so they don't show whether each practice had the effect on its own.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Comparison With Rest Was Repeated in a Larger Group

The study also repeated the group's earlier comparison with rest, this time with 20 meditators and 26 controls instead of 12 and 12. During meditation, compared with rest, meditators had lower activity than controls in the anterior cingulate cortex and in two parts of the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex.

### One Change Showed Up in Everyone

Across both groups together, meditation raised activity compared with rest in the orbitofrontal cortex and nearby rectal gyrus, on the underside of the frontal lobes. The same regions became less active than at rest during the word task. The paper reports this without interpreting it.

### Meditators Reported Less Mind Wandering

Across the three meditations, meditators rated their minds as wandering less than controls did (F(1,44) = 7.57, p = .009). The paper gives the averages for each practice, shown below, but tests only the overall group difference. Both groups said they could follow the instructions well, with average ratings between 8.5 and 9.0 out of 10 for every practice. Neither mind wandering nor instruction-following changed between the first and the last run.

### Both Groups Handled the Word Task Similarly

The comparison with an active task assumes that both groups did it about equally well, and they mostly did. Reaction times were almost identical (1.25 seconds for meditators and 1.26 for controls). Meditators made fewer errors on the uppercase judgments (1.7% vs 3.5%), while errors on the "describes you" judgments did not differ (1.7% vs 1.3%). The groups also moved their heads in the scanner a similar amount, which matters because movement can distort fMRI results.

### Limits the Authors Acknowledge

The authors list several weaknesses of the design. The meditation blocks (3 minutes) were twice as long as the word-task blocks (90 seconds), which can make brain responses harder to estimate. Pooling the "describes you" and "uppercase" judgments may have mixed trials that raise and lower default mode activity, and pooling the three meditations means they couldn't be compared with each other. The order was also fixed:

> A related limitation is that the meditation and active tasks were not counterbalanced; the active task always preceded the meditation task.

The authors chose the fixed order so that a meditative state would not carry over into the word task, but note that it did not account for possible trait-based effects. And meditating in a scanner strips the practice of its usual context, such as intentions, beliefs and a community.

Other limits follow from the design. Mind wandering was rated by the participants themselves, after each run. Everyone in the study was White, and the meditators came from a single tradition. One small inconsistency appears in the paper's thresholds: the Methods give the threshold for forming clusters of active voxels as p < .01, while the note to the interaction table gives it as p < .005.

### The Authors' Clinical Argument

In their discussion, the authors connect the findings to mental health. They cite studies linking greater default mode activity to poorer task performance and to depression, anxiety and addiction, and note that mind wandering and self-related thinking feed rumination and have been linked to lower well-being, citing [Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010)](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/). Meditation, they note, has been shown to improve attention and :term{id="working_memory" case="lower"} performance. They also point to mindfulness training's benefits for pain, anxiety and depression, and for addiction, citing among others their own [trial of mindfulness training for smoking](/mindfulness-smoking-cessation-trial/). From this they suggest that reducing default mode activity may be one way meditation brings clinical benefits. That is a hypothesis: this study measured brain activity during meditation, not any change in symptoms or well-being.

## Final Reflection

This study's main contribution is methodological. By comparing meditation with an effortful task rather than only with rest, it tried to separate any default mode change specific to meditation from the ordinary drop that comes with focusing on something. In 20 experienced Insight meditators, two hubs of the network, the anterior cingulate cortex and the precuneus, were less active during meditation and more active during a word task than at rest, a split that 26 non-meditators did not show. The meditators also reported less mind wandering while they meditated.

The result fits other work on this site. In Zen meditators, the [default mode response to a word's meaning ended sooner](/zen-meditation-default-mode-words/) than in non-meditators, and simply [repeating a word silently](/silent-word-repetition-brain-deactivation/) lowered activity across much of the network in people who had never meditated. A study of [mindful attention to stressful scenarios](/decentering-imagined-stress-fmri/), by contrast, found no default mode difference between two strategies in novices.

The study's limits matter for how far it goes. It compares two groups at one point in time, so it can't show that meditation produced the difference; the word task always came first; and the three practices were pooled. The paper's own title and abstract speak more causally than the design allows. It is best read as good evidence that, in experienced meditators, the default mode network behaves differently during meditation than during a demanding task, and as a model for using an active comparison task, rather than as proof that meditation itself caused the change.
