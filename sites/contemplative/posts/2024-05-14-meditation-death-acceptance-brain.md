## Background Information

Humans are probably the only animals that know they will die. Hundreds of studies, mostly within :term{id="terror_management_theory"}, describe automatic mental processes that buffer people against that knowledge, usually without their noticing. Fear of death has also been proposed as a hidden thread running through many mental health problems.

The Israeli team behind this paper wanted to know how early in the brain this protection begins. Their approach draws on :term{id="predictive_coding" case="lower"}, the idea that the brain is constantly predicting what it will sense next and reacting when its predictions are wrong. Show someone the same face several times and the brain comes to expect it. Then show a slightly different face, and the visual system produces a small "surprise" signal about a quarter of a second later, a visual cousin of the :term{id="mismatch_negativity" case="lower"}. That signal shows the brain had built a prediction in the first place.

In a [2019 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116080) of people who didn't meditate, the team recorded this signal with :term{id="meg"} while faces appeared under a word that was either related to death or merely negative. The surprise signal showed up under negative words, whether the face was the person's own or a stranger's, and under death words when the face was a stranger's. But in one combination it disappeared:

> Crucially, the presentation of self-images displayed under a death-related word (DS condition) resulted in a sharp attenuation of this basic predictive perceptual mechanism as indicated by a lack of a detectable vMMR effect.

The authors' reading was that the brain holds a deep, automatic assumption that "death concerns others, but not our self." Faced with a run of one's own face paired with death, it declines to form the prediction at all. A behavioral experiment in the same study found that death words biased how people judged blended self–other faces, tilting them toward "other," and the gap between the stranger and self responses under death words, which they called a death-denial index, predicted an implicit fear of death.

Buddhist traditions treat the opposite, a direct recognition that one's own body is impermanent, as a central goal. The Satipatthana Sutta, a foundation of modern :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} practice, includes contemplating a body at various stages of decay with the reflection that one's own body will go the same way. Tibetan traditions go further, with practices for the moment of death and the belief that some masters remain in meditation afterward, in :term{id="tukdam"} (a claim one [EEG study](/thukdam-eeg-after-death/) has tested), and with practitioners said to enter [near-death experiences](/meditation-near-death-experiences/) at will. Earlier questionnaire studies had linked mindfulness to less self-reported :term{id="death_anxiety" case="lower"}. This study asked whether meditation also changes the brain's automatic defense.

## What They Did

The participants were 38 meditators (20 women, aged 26 to 72, average 40) from Tovana, the Israel Insight Meditation Society, which teaches mindfulness in the :term{id="theravada"} :term{id="vipassana"} tradition. All had practiced daily for at least a year, 15 to 120 minutes a day, and attended at least one residential retreat of a week or more. Lifetime practice ranged from 115 to 24,837 hours, averaging about 4,100. None had been on a retreat focused on death.

They came from the team's earlier project on dissolving the sense of self. After specially designed training, these meditators had [alternated between keeping and dissolving the felt boundary between themselves and the world](/self-boundary-dissolution-letting-go/) in the MEG scanner and then described the experience in long interviews. Researchers turned those descriptions into scores, including how deep the dissolution went and how pleasant or unpleasant it felt. Shortly after dissolving the boundary in the lab, they had also filled in a questionnaire on altered states that includes an "anxious ego dissolution" scale. Those interviews took place between November 2018 and December 2019; the death study was recorded between November 2019 and July 2020.

In the new session, each meditator lay in the MEG scanner and watched faces. Each trial began with a word, either death-related or merely negative, which stayed on screen. Below it, either the person's own face or a stranger's face appeared three to six times, a quarter of a second each, followed by an odd one out: a 50/50 blend of the two faces. To keep attention up, people pressed a button whenever a face wore sunglasses. That gave four conditions: death–self, negative–self, death–other and negative–other.

They also rated their general well-being and trait anxiety, and placed themselves on a new scale from −10 (certain that consciousness ends at death) through 0 ("I don't know") to +10 (certain that it continues).

The main hypothesis, that meditators would show a surprise response in the death–self condition, was preregistered, along with the sample size and the MEG analysis, which followed the 2019 study. The links with well-being and with the earlier meditation experiences were labeled exploratory. No new comparison group was recruited.

## One Big Result: Death Words Didn't Mute the Brain's Response to Meditators' Own Faces

First, the basic signal replicated. Across all conditions, the odd face produced a surprise response between 247 and 288 milliseconds after it appeared, over the back left of the head, just as in the 2019 study.

Then the key test. In the death–self condition, where non-meditators had shown no response, the meditators showed a clear one, a medium-sized :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} (Cohen's d = 0.59). In fact, it was their largest response of the four conditions.

To compare the groups formally, the team went back to the 2019 data. After putting each group's results on a common scale, they found that the groups differed specifically in how death words and one's own face combined. The death–self response was larger in meditators (d = 0.69, 95% :term{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} 0.14 to 1.24), while the other three conditions did not differ significantly between the groups.

Within the meditators, death words and merely negative words made no measurable difference. What mattered was whose face it was: the meditators' brains responded to their own face under either kind of word (more weakly, just reaching significance, under negative words), but not significantly to the stranger's face. Brain-source estimates placed the self-face response mainly in right-side visual and face-recognition areas, extending into the :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"}, and the death–self and negative–self responses looked alike. In other words, for these meditators death was just another negative word, not a special threat to be kept away from the self. The authors were careful in how they put it:

> These results lead us to cautiously suggest that meditators’ brains signal an acceptance of death rather than its denial.

That caution is warranted. The study measured a split-second response to blended faces, not how anyone felt about dying, and it included no questionnaire on death anxiety. Calling the response "acceptance" rests on the 2019 interpretation of its absence as denial. And the comparison with non-meditators is weaker than a two-group study would be. The non-meditators were tested separately, for an earlier study, and the paper doesn't say they were matched to the meditators in age, sex or beliefs. The authors acknowledge "the lack of a proper control group" and call the group comparison exploratory, while arguing that the meditators' pattern was different in kind, not merely in degree.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### More Practice Didn't Mean More Acceptance

The team had predicted that meditators with more lifetime practice would show less denial. They didn't: the death acceptance measure (the death–self response minus the death–stranger response) was unrelated to hours of practice (Spearman correlation −0.07). The authors grant that it is therefore unclear whether meditation itself is what made the difference. They suggest two possibilities: a few hundred hours might be enough, so the effect levels off early, or what matters is the felt understanding of impermanence that this style of practice cultivates, rather than hours on the cushion. A third possibility is simpler. People who take up meditation, or who stick with it, may already relate to death differently. A :term{id="cross_sectional_study" case="lower"} can't tell these apart; the authors call for longitudinal studies.

### Not Explained by Belief in an Afterlife

Belief in literal life after death is known to soften people's defenses against mortality, so the team checked it. Most of these meditators were quite sure consciousness continues after death. Only one leaned slightly toward annihilation (−2), five said "I don't know" (0), and the rest scored positive, with a median of 8.5 out of 10. As predicted, the strength of this belief was unrelated to the death acceptance measure. With so few doubters, though, the sample could not really test whether belief matters, and the authors call for samples more balanced in their beliefs.

### The More "Accepting" Brains Had Gentler Experiences of Self-Dissolution

The most striking finding links the brain measure to what happened in the earlier project, when these meditators deliberately dissolved their sense of a boundary between self and world. Some found that blissful; [a few found it frightening](/self-boundary-dissolution-letting-go/). How deep people went had not predicted how it felt. Meditators with higher death acceptance had described their dissolution experiences as more pleasant (Spearman correlation 0.52) and had scored lower on the anxious ego dissolution scale (−0.42). Depth of dissolution and the other altered-state scales were unrelated to death acceptance.

The authors see this as a first, data-based answer to why :term{id="ego_dissolution" case="lower"} is wonderful for some people and terrifying for others, with implications for [psychedelic therapy](/meditation-psychedelics-self-loss/), where such states are often induced deliberately:

> Facing one’s fear, denial and anxiety of death prior to engaging in practices or therapeutics which may induce states of self/ego-death might reduce the risks involved and yield better clinical outcomes.

It is an intriguing hypothesis, but the evidence is a pair of :term[correlations]{id="correlation"} in 38 people, among several tests with no reported correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}, and the experiences were recorded in an earlier phase of the project (2018–2019) than the brain responses (2019–2020). It can't show which way, if either, the influence runs.

### Less Self-Favoritism Went With More Well-Being

Brains usually respond more strongly to one's own face than to other faces, and so did these meditators'. Those with a smaller gap between their self and stranger responses reported higher well-being (a moderate negative correlation, r = −0.39), though not lower trait anxiety. That echoes [an EEG study](/loving-kindness-self-bias/) in which loving-kindness meditators showed less of a brain bias toward their own face. Note, though, that this was the self-versus-stranger gap, not the death acceptance measure. The abstract's statement that the death-related responses corresponded "to increased self-reported well-being" runs the two together a little, and the correlation itself was modest and exploratory.

## Final Reflection

This is an imaginative study. It takes a big question, whether meditation changes how we face death, and pins it to a specific, measurable process: whether the brain will let "death" and "me" sit together long enough to form a prediction. The main hypothesis was preregistered, the basic brain signal replicated cleanly, and the result went in the predicted direction with a respectable effect.

How far it reaches depends on things the design can't settle. The non-meditators came from a separate, earlier study, the brain measure didn't track how much people had practiced, and "acceptance" is an interpretation of a quarter-second visual response rather than something the meditators reported. The links with well-being and with the feel of self-dissolution are exploratory correlations in a modest sample.

Still, the pattern is suggestive. It hints that one of the brain's most basic defenses against mortality may not be fixed, and that people whose brains don't flinch from it may also find letting go of the self less frightening. A longitudinal study, following people as they learn to meditate, with matched controls and direct measures of death anxiety, would show whether meditation can teach the brain to stop turning away.
