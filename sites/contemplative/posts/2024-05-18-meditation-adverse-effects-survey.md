## Background Information

Buddhist-derived meditation is now widely promoted for health, through programs such as mindfulness-based interventions for stress, pain and mood. The authors of this paper point out that Buddhist sources describe a much wider range of effects than better health, including periods of difficulty. Tibetan traditions speak of *nyams*, "meditation experiences" that range from bliss and visions to intense body pain, paranoia and fear. :term{id="zen"} traditions speak of *makyō*, largely perceptual "side-effects" that arise in practice, and of a prolonged illness-like "Zen sickness". :term[Theravāda]{id="theravada"} traditions describe "corruptions of insight", blissful states that meditators can mistake for real progress, and present some stages of insight as particularly challenging.

Scientific research has paid these effects much less attention. The authors note that more than 75% of meditation studies do not actively ask about adverse effects, relying instead on participants to mention problems, which people are unlikely to do without being asked. Passive monitoring of this kind is thought to underestimate adverse effects more than 20-fold. Meditation-induced :term{id="psychosis" case="lower"}, :term{id="depersonalization" case="lower"}, mania and seizures have been reported in the clinical and medical literature, but there had been little systematic study of what challenging meditation experiences are like, what causes them, and how they can be managed.

The Varieties of Contemplative Experience study set out to fill that gap. The team, led by religious studies scholar Jared Lindahl and clinical psychologist and neuroscientist Willoughby Britton at Brown University, asked what range of experiences Western Buddhist meditators report, which of them people find challenging, what they think causes them, and what helps.

## What They Did

The researchers deliberately went looking for people who had had difficulties, rather than a random sample of meditators. They first recruited experienced teachers from well-respected meditation centers. These teachers passed the study's contact details to students who had had notable or challenging experiences, and many of those students passed them on to other meditators. To take part, practitioners had to practice in a Buddhist tradition and be able to describe a meditation-related experience that was challenging or difficult, or that brought significant psychological or physical changes. People were excluded if they had had similar experiences before learning meditation (for example from drug use or mental illness), if much of their practice came from non-Buddhist traditions, or if something else, such as a medical illness, could explain everything they reported.

Of 73 practitioners interviewed, 13 were excluded: 5 for mixed practice histories, 4 because they reported no challenging experiences and 4 because another cause could account for their experiences. That left 60, with 20 each from the Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan traditions. They were 43% women, with an average age of 48.9 (range 18–76). Most were from the United States (85%), 94% were white, and the group was highly educated: 42% had a master's degree and 25% a doctorate. Sixty percent were meditation teachers themselves, and 43% had more than 10,000 hours of lifetime practice.

The interviews took place between December 2010 and March 2016, by phone, video or in person, and usually lasted 45 to 120 minutes. They were semi-structured and open-ended. Practitioners were asked about their experiences ("Have you had any significant experiences that were unexpected, challenging, or difficult?"), how they and others interpreted them, why they linked them to meditation, and what helped or didn't. The team also interviewed 32 experts: 13 Theravādin, 8 Zen and 6 Tibetan teachers and 5 clinicians. Eleven of the experts had also been interviewed as practitioners.

The transcripts were coded category by category, with the categories built mostly from the participants' own descriptions rather than set in advance. On a 15% subset coded twice, the two coders' agreement averaged 99.5% across categories (:term[inter-rater reliability]{id="inter_rater_reliability"} kappa .70).

After all interviews were done, practitioners were sent a questionnaire on their background, how long their difficulties and impairment lasted and how severe they were. Fifty-three (88%) completed it; for the other 7, the researchers took the information from the interview transcripts. The questionnaire also scored six criteria for whether an experience was caused by meditation, borrowed from those that regulators use to judge whether a treatment caused an adverse event. These included whether the practitioner attributed it to meditation, whether it happened during or after practice, whether it eased when practice was reduced and whether it came back when practice resumed. A score of 2 or more counted as "possibly related". All 60 practitioners met that bar, meeting on average 4.2 of the six criteria.

## One Big Result: 59 Kinds of Experience Across Seven Domains, Pleasant and Unpleasant

The team sorted what practitioners described into 59 categories of meditation-related experience, grouped into seven domains. Each domain was reported by at least three-quarters of the 60 practitioners.

![Share of the 60 practitioners who reported at least one experience in each of the seven domains, with the number of kinds of experience in each: affective 100% (13 kinds), cognitive 93% (10), social 90% (5), somatic 88% (15), conative 82% (3), perceptual 78% (7) and sense of self 75% (6). Drawn from the values in Table 4 of Lindahl et al., 2017.](/assets/article_images/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/experience-domains.jpg)

- **Cognitive** (10 categories): changes in worldview (48%), delusional, irrational or paranormal beliefs (47%), mental stillness, vivid imagery, problems with concentration or memory, "mind racing", and, more rarely, a breakdown of conceptual meaning and obsessive thoughts about ethics (scrupulosity, 3%).
- **Perceptual** (7): hallucinations, visions or illusions (42%), visual lights (33%), hypersensitivity to light, sound or touch, distortions of time and space, the dissolving of perceived objects, and :term{id="derealization" case="lower"} (7%), where the world seems dreamlike or unreal.
- **Affective** (13): fear, anxiety, panic or paranoia (82%, the most common category overall), positive affect such as bliss (75%), depression or grief (57%), re-living traumatic memories (43%), anger, emotional swings, suicidality (18%) and emotional flattening (17%).
- **Somatic** (15): surges of "energy" through the body (63%), sleep changes (62%), pain (47%), pressure or tension, changes in appetite, heat or cold, involuntary movements, and changes in the heart, breathing and digestion.
- **Conative** (motivation; 3): changes in motivation or goals (78%), changes in effort or striving (42%), and loss of pleasure and drive (18%).
- **Sense of self** (6): changes in the boundary between self and world or self and others (53%), loss of the :term{id="sense_of_agency" case="lower"} (25%), loss of the sense of a basic self (25%), changes in embodiment and in one's life story, and loss of the :term{id="sense_of_ownership" case="lower"} of thoughts, emotions and body sensations (18%).
- **Social** (5): social impairment (50%), difficulty re-entering ordinary life after a retreat (47%), changing relationships with the meditation community (45%), problems at work (42%) and, less often, increased sociability (7%).

Practitioners described a lot. On average each reported 19.6 categories (range 7–40) across 6.1 of the 7 domains, and 43% reported experiences in all seven. As a group, the 32 experts described 56 of the 59 categories in their students or patients.

The catalogue is not a list of harms. It includes positive affect, mental stillness, clarity and increased sociability, and the authors found little agreement on which experiences are "positive" or "negative". The same kind of experience could be welcome for one person and frightening for another. A dissolving boundary between self and world, for instance, drew responses "from neutral curiosity, to bliss and joy, to fear and terror". In the authors' words:

> This range of experiences and impacts suggests that very few of the experiences were universally appraised as adverse.

The authors also note that neither "mania" nor "psychosis" was a category. What practitioners called "mania" was typically a combination of positive affect and racing thoughts, sometimes with delusions.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Symptoms Often Lasted Years

Most practitioners (88%) said their challenging experiences spilled over into daily life, beyond the meditation session or retreat. For the experiences they found challenging or impairing, which the paper calls "symptoms", the median duration was 1–3 years, ranging from a few days to more than 10 years. Impairment tended to last about as long as the symptoms. Ten percent reported minimal impairment, but 73% reported moderate to severe impairment in at least one area of life, 17% reported suicidality and 17% needed inpatient hospital care.

![How long participants' symptoms (dark bars) and the impairment linked to them (light bars) lasted, as a percentage of the sample, from none or under a week to more than 10 years. The median symptom duration was 1–3 years. Fig 1 from Lindahl et al., 2017, PLOS ONE (CC BY 4.0).](/assets/article_images/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/symptom-impairment-durations.jpg)

These figures describe a group chosen for having had difficulties, so they can't be read as the odds for meditators in general.

### Not Only Beginners, Not Only Retreats

Practitioners had been meditating for anywhere from 1 day to more than 25 years (7.1 years on average) when their challenges began. A little over a quarter (29%) ran into them in their first year, 45% after 1–10 years and 25% after more than 10 years. Seven (12%) had challenges within their first 10 days of practice.

Most challenges (72%) arose during or just after a retreat, but 28% arose in daily practice. At the time, 41% of the 56 who answered were meditating 10 or more hours a day and 25% less than an hour. About three-quarters were practicing with a community (72%) or a teacher (75%). At onset, 32% had a psychiatric history and 43% a trauma history, so most had no psychiatric history and most had no trauma history.

The authors argue that this challenges the assumption that meditation difficulties only happen to people with pre-existing conditions, on long or intensive retreats, poorly supervised or practicing incorrectly. They add that this is not to say such factors play no role.

### 26 Factors, Each Cutting Both Ways

The team also catalogued 26 categories of "influencing factors" that practitioners and experts thought shaped the experiences, grouped into four domains:

- **Practitioner:** worldview or explanatory framework (cited by 97% of practitioners), intentions and goals, personality, psychiatric, trauma and medical history, and identity.
- **Practice:** amount, intensity or consistency of practice (93%), practice approach (93%), type of practice, how one responds to an experience, and stage of practice.
- **Relationships:** relationship to the teacher (97%), relationships inside and outside the practice community, surroundings, sociocultural context and early-life relationships.
- **Health behaviors:** psychotherapy or medical treatment, diet, medication, grounding activities, sleep, recreational drugs, exercise and bodywork.

Many factors were described as both a risk and a remedy. Teachers and communities were reported as risk factors when they were absent, unhelpful or unsympathetic, and as remedies when they were supportive and understanding. The silence and social isolation of retreats were commonly described as a risk, and changing environment was sometimes needed to ease a difficulty. Many remedies that practitioners tried, or were told to try, were described as ineffective or harmful. The authors stress that this list reflects what their participants believed, not tested risk factors.

### Interpretation Shapes the Experience

Interpretation came up again and again. Practitioners often met several, sometimes conflicting, ways of making sense of the same experience, from teachers, texts, fellow practitioners, doctors and therapists. What counts as "progress" or as "pathology" can differ between traditions, lineages and even teachers. Traditional explanations may only be available to people working closely with a teacher, and may not suit Western meditators who came to practice for therapeutic reasons. The authors suggest that some "adverse" responses may come from a mismatch between a practitioner's goals and expectations and the tradition's own framework for self-transformation. A much older paper on [depersonalization in meditators](/depersonalization-and-meditation/) makes a related argument about how interpretation shapes distress.

### Links to Other Work From the Project

Two of the perceptual experiences, a brightening of the visual field and seeing lights, were analyzed in [an earlier paper from this project](/meditation-light-experiences/). The authors also report that a questionnaire based on this catalogue was being pilot-tested in a randomized trial of :term[mindfulness-based cognitive therapy]{id="mbct"}. A later paper from that trial used structured interviews to [estimate how often such effects occur in a mindfulness course](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/).

### What the Study Can't Say

The authors are direct about the limits of their design:

> Thus the 100% frequency of challenging experiences is an artifact of sampling and not a reflection of the actual frequency among Western Buddhist meditators.

The sample was highly educated, mostly white and mostly American, and the authors expect it had lower-than-average rates of mental illness and trauma. The findings can't be generalized to other groups of meditators, to children, or to mindfulness-based programs, which were not studied. Although some participants had difficulties in conditions resembling those programs (daily practice, under an hour a day, or within the first 50 hours of practice), the authors only argue that program teachers should know about such challenges.

Everything was self-reported and recalled, sometimes long after the events. Practitioners described what they chose to mention in answer to open questions, so how often each category was reported reflects how memorable and easy to describe it was as well as how often it happened. The authors call these frequencies preliminary. The causality criteria make it less likely that all the experiences were unrelated to meditation, but they are not proof. The authors note that a randomized trial with multi-year follow-up would be needed for that.

## Final Reflection

This study maps what can happen when meditation becomes difficult. It does not count how often that happens. By recruiting people who had struggled, the researchers collected a wide range of experiences, from bliss and clarity to panic, hospitalization and changes in the sense of self, and found that the same experience could be welcomed by one person and feared by another. Many participants were experienced practitioners, and many had no psychiatric or trauma history.

The authors present their results as a starting point:

> Finally, given that this is one of the first studies of this scope on this topic, these results should not be interpreted as conclusive; rather, they should be taken as sufficient evidence to warrant further investigation.

Later studies on this site take up the question of frequency, including surveys of [regular meditators](/regular-meditators-unpleasant-experiences/) and of [a broad sample of US meditators](/meditation-adverse-effects-prevalence/).
