## Background Information

Some meditation traditions have long held that practice can cut the need for sleep, with stories of yogis who sleep only a couple of hours a night. The authors of this 2010 paper, researchers at the University of Kentucky, noted that :term{id="eeg"} studies of meditation had rarely touched the question:

> Surprisingly, given the number of EEG studies on meditation, essentially no one has addressed whether meditation might provide a restorative function similar to non-REM sleep despite centuries of anecdotal claims by certain practitioners and followers that meditation does reduce the need for sleep…

Their reasoning started from brain waves. During meditation, synchronized :term[alpha waves]{id="alpha"} dominate much of the cortex, more strongly than in ordinary eyes-closed rest. That bears a superficial resemblance to deep :term{id="nrem_sleep"}, when even slower, synchronized :term[delta waves]{id="delta"} take over. The two rhythms come from very different brain systems, but both reflect many neurons firing together, and the authors wondered whether that might give the brain a chance to reset.

There's no direct measure of how much sleep a person needs, so the team used an indirect one: the :term[psychomotor vigilance task (PVT)]{id="psychomotor_vigilance_task"}. For ten minutes, a person watches a small display and presses a button as fast as possible whenever a counter starts, at random intervals. Well-rested people typically respond in about 180 to 300 milliseconds and almost never miss. As sleep debt builds up, responses slow down and lapses become more common.

## What They Did

The paper reports two studies.

**Study 1: Beginners.** Ten University of Kentucky students (7 men and 3 women, aged 19 to 23) with no previous meditation experience were screened for medical, psychiatric and sleep problems. They kept a regular sleep schedule, checked with a wrist activity monitor (:term{id="actigraphy" case="lower"}), and averaged about 7.5 hours of sleep before each test day. On test days they avoided caffeine, nicotine, alcohol and other drugs.

Each student had two one-hour lessons, in pairs, in a simple eyes-closed meditation focused on the breath, a form of :term[concentrative meditation]{id="focused_attention"}. They knelt on a meditation bench and breathed from the abdomen, keeping their attention on its movement.

In this :term{id="within_subject_design" case="lower"}, every student then went through three conditions, twice each, on six separate days. Each day's activity was assigned at random, and the students didn't know it in advance:

- **Meditation:** 40 minutes of the breath-focused practice.
- **Nap:** 40 minutes lying in bed, trying to sleep.
- **Control:** 40 minutes in a desk chair with soft music, light reading or conversation, eyes open.

The session ran from 3:10 to 3:50 pm. The students took the 10-minute PVT at 3:00, again at 4:00 (ten minutes after the session) and a third time at 4:50, after 40 minutes of the control activities.

Later, each student stayed up through a weekend night. After about 32 hours without sleep, they took the PVT, meditated for 40 minutes and took it again. There was no sleep-deprived nap or control session for comparison.

**Study 2: Long-term meditators.** Seven experienced meditators in the Delhi region of India (3 women and 4 men, aged 24 to 48) had practiced for at least three years, two hours or more on most days, in traditional yogic styles focused on the breath. For 15 to 30 days they kept sleep journals, and for 15 to 22 days they wore activity monitors, pressing a marker button each time they began to meditate. Smaller subsets had an EEG (3 people), or took the PVT and a :term[Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT)]{id="multiple_sleep_latency_test"}, a standard measure of daytime sleepiness (4 people). Their average sleep was compared with that of 23 non-meditators in India matched for age and sex.

## One Big Result: Every Student Reacted Faster After Meditating, While Most Got Slower After a Nap

In all ten students, reaction times on the PVT were faster after 40 minutes of meditation than before it. Averaged across the ten students in the paper's Table 1, responses went from about 253 to 237 milliseconds, roughly 17 milliseconds quicker, which the authors describe as about a 7% improvement. The direction and size of the change matched on both meditation days for 9 of the 10 students.

The nap went the other way. Reaction times slowed for 9 of 10 students, from about 263 to 296 milliseconds on average. The authors put this down to :term{id="sleep_inertia" case="lower"}, the grogginess that follows waking. Most students seemed to fall asleep within ten minutes, so they had about half an hour of sleep, probably enough for some to reach deep sleep, which makes waking up harder.

Perhaps more surprising, the control activity also left people slower, from about 250 to 266 milliseconds; 9 of 10 slowed and one was essentially unchanged. The authors suggest the mid-afternoon dip in alertness, the students' missing their usual afternoon caffeine, or flagging motivation. Whatever the cause, it changes how the meditation result reads:

> In light of the results following naps and control activities, the improvement post-meditation appears even greater, since the improvement occurs against the normal decline.

Measured against the control days rather than the pre-test, the authors estimate the meditation advantage at closer to 14%. In their statistical comparison, the two meditation conditions differed significantly from the nap and control conditions, while the nap and control conditions didn't differ significantly from each other.

The boost didn't last long. An hour later, many students were back to their baseline, with no significant difference from the control days, though the paper doesn't show these data.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Meditation Helped Even After a Sleepless Night

After about 32 hours awake, the students started slower, at 289 milliseconds against 255 on rested days. Meditation still sped them up significantly: 9 of the 10 were faster afterward, by about 27 milliseconds on average, though this wasn't significantly different from the gain on rested days. Lapses, responses slower than half a second or errors, showed up after the sleepless night (they were extremely rare on other days), and they dropped after meditation. Without a sleep-deprived day spent napping or doing the control activity, though, there's no way to tell how much of this change was due to meditation itself.

### Yogis Slept About Two and a Half Hours Less

The seven long-term meditators slept an average of 5.2 hours a day, against 7.8 hours for the 23 non-meditators, a statistically significant difference, and also less than published norms. Each of the seven slept between four and six hours, and, as the authors note, their daily meditation, between 1.5 and 3.5 hours, was roughly equal to the "lost" sleep. (The paper describes the meditators as practicing two hours or more on most days; in the paper's Table 2, two of them average 1.5 and 1.75 hours a day.)

They didn't seem short of sleep. In the four who took the MSLT, it took an average of 18.25 minutes to fall asleep during the day, which the authors describe as above population norms, a sign of little daytime sleepiness. Their PVT scores showed no sign of sleepiness either, though those data aren't shown. In some of them, EEG, video and direct observation during meditation found very little sleep and no head-nodding, so the yogis weren't simply napping while they meditated.

That led the authors to suggest meditation might replace some sleep, roughly hour for hour. They also offer an alternative: meditators might sleep more deeply, getting sleep's benefits in less time. And they found a clear limit:

> When we challenged Yogis with a total night of sleep deprivation, they were generally too tired to meditate successfully, further discounting the idea that extensive meditation could totally or largely replace sleep.

This part of the study is a one-time comparison of seven people with a separate group, so it can't show that meditation caused the shorter sleep. It can't rule out, for example, that people who need less sleep are the ones drawn to hours of daily practice, or that the groups differ in other ways. The paper also doesn't say how the comparison group's sleep was measured. A later study on this site found a smaller gap: in one night in a sleep lab, [long-term meditators slept about 36 minutes less](/meditators-sleep-gamma/) than non-meditators.

### What the Design Can and Can't Rule Out

The authors acknowledge that ten participants is "admittedly quite small," and that results from college students new to meditation may not apply to anyone else. Students knew whether they were meditating or napping, so expectations could have played a part (see :term{id="demand_characteristics" case="lower"}). The authors think this unlikely: several students said they expected the nap to help too, yet they got worse after it, and the PVT is a simple test with no learning curve that would be hard to underperform on purpose.

The three conditions also differed in more than the activity. Meditating, the students knelt with eyes closed and attention on the breath; in the control condition they sat in a chair with eyes open, reading, talking or listening to music. So the results can't separate meditation from simply resting quietly with eyes closed, a possibility the authors raise themselves when they ask whether "meditation (or even simple eyes-closed resting)" might pay off sleep debt.

### Not the Same as a Short Nap

The authors suggest that brief meditation could give a quick lift without the grogginess that follows a longer nap. They also speculate that "power naps" of 10 to 15 minutes, which often involve no actual sleep, may work more like meditation than like sleep. That idea wasn't tested here.

## Final Reflection

This small, early study made a neat point with a simple test. Forty minutes of breath-focused meditation, practiced by people with just two lessons, left all of them responding faster on a basic alertness task, while a 40-minute nap left nearly all of them slower. Because performance fell in the control condition, meditation seemed to work against the afternoon slump rather than just adding a small gain.

It's also a reminder for studies that test beginners [right after a session](/brief-mindfulness-training-cognition/): part of any gain may come from the session just finished rather than from the training. Here, the lift had largely faded within an hour.

The bolder claim in the title, that meditation "may decrease sleep need," rests on seven yogis compared with a separate group, and the authors frame it carefully as preliminary. It's an intriguing observation, not evidence that meditation can stand in for sleep, and their own sleep-deprivation test suggests it can't replace much of it. Larger studies with sleep measured the same way in both groups, and tracking people before and after they take up long daily practice, would be needed to tell.
