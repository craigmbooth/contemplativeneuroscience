## Background Information

Earlier brain imaging studies had found a set of regions that are more active when people rest than when they do almost any demanding task. This :term{id="dmn"} includes areas along the brain's midline and the :term{id="angular_gyrus" case="lower"}, and it has been linked to the stream of thoughts, memories and associations that runs on its own whenever we aren't busy, the raw material of :term{id="mind_wandering" case="lower"}.

That stream is exactly what many forms of meditation work with. :term{id="zen"} meditation in particular aims at a state of full awareness with little conceptual content, while staying alert, eyes open, rather than withdrawing into a dreamy calm. The authors quote the classic instructions for zazen (seated meditation) by Dōgen, the patriarch of the Japanese Sōto Zen school:

> Think of neither good nor evil and judge not right or wrong. Stop the operation of the mind, and consciousness; bring to an end all desires, all concepts and judgments […] After the bodily position is in order, regulate your breathing. If a thought arises, take note of it and then dismiss it.

The researchers wanted to see the "take note of it and then dismiss it" part in the brain. Their idea was that years of noticing distracting thoughts and returning to the breath should make meditators better at cutting short the cascade of associations that a single word sets off. If so, the default mode network's response to a meaningful word should be shorter in meditators than in people who had never meditated.

## What They Did

The study compared 12 Zen meditators, each with more than 3 years of daily practice and recruited from the local community and meditation centers, with 12 people who had never meditated. The groups were matched for sex (10 and 9 men), age (averages of 37.3 and 35.3 years) and education (17.8 and 17.6 years of schooling on average). All were native English speakers. The paper gives no further detail of the meditators' practice, such as total hours.

Everyone, meditator or not, did the same thing in the scanner: lie still for about 20 minutes, look at a cross on the screen, attend to their breathing and return to it whenever thoughts, memories or sensations pulled them away. Giving the non-meditators the same simplified meditation, rather than letting them simply rest, was meant to make the two groups' tasks as similar as possible.

At random moments, a string of letters appeared on the screen. Participants pressed one button if it was a real English word and another if it wasn't, then went straight back to their breath. This :term{id="lexical_decision_task" case="lower"} used 50 real words and 50 nonwords: strings of letters that could be pronounced but meant nothing, matched to the words for sound and spelling. The gaps between them varied from 1.4 to 72.9 seconds with a median of 8.2 seconds.

The trick lies in the comparison. Both kinds of letter strings interrupt the meditation, need to be read and need a button press. Only real words carry meaning, and recognizing a word automatically calls up associations with it, even though the task never asked anyone to think about the meaning. The authors chose this to mimic spontaneous, unbidden thoughts, using the words as "seeds" that set off a small cascade of associations at moments the researchers controlled. Subtracting the brain's response to nonwords from its response to words should leave the part due to meaning, which the authors call conceptual processing.

The analysis had two steps. First, pooling both groups, the researchers found the brain areas that responded more to words than to nonwords in the :term{id="fmri"} scans. Then, within those areas, they traced how the response to meaning unfolded over the 14 seconds after each word. fMRI tracks blood flow, which rises and falls a few seconds behind the neurons (the :term[hemodynamic response]{id="hemodynamic_response"}), so a lingering train of thought should show up as a long tail. Their main test added up the response to meaning from 6 to 14 seconds after the word, after the point where a standard, brief response would have peaked, and compared the groups.

## One Big Result: In Meditators, the Brain's Response to a Word's Meaning Faded Sooner

Eight clusters responded more to words than to nonwords, all in the left hemisphere, as expected for a language task, and they largely overlapped with the default mode network. They included two parts of the angular gyrus, two parts of the superior frontal gyrus, the :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"}, the rostral :term[anterior cingulate]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"}, part of the inferior temporal lobe and the middle cingulate cortex.

In these regions, the response to meaning lasted longer in the non-meditators. Summed from 6 to 14 seconds after the word, it was larger in non-meditators than in meditators across the eight regions (F(1,22) = 12.3, p = 0.002), and the size of the group difference didn't differ significantly from region to region. Tested one by one, with a correction for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}, six of the eight regions showed the difference. The two that didn't were the middle cingulate cortex, which the authors note isn't usually counted as part of the default mode network, and the posterior cingulate cortex. The difference was clearest in the left angular gyrus and the left superior frontal gyrus.

In the authors' words:

> In regions of the default network, meditators displayed a BOLD response related to semantic processing that was characterized by a reduced post-stimulus tail compared to control subjects.

The authors' suggested explanation is that the meditators were better at the task they had both been given: once they had answered, they returned to the breath more effectively, cutting short the chain of associations the word had started. They suggest that meditation training may build the ability to regulate the flow of spontaneous thought, and that this line of research could be relevant to conditions marked by excessive rumination, such as depression, anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder. The study tested only the brain response, though, not participants' thoughts or any clinical outcome.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Meditators Were No Quicker or More Accurate

The two groups answered equally fast. Both took longer for nonwords than words, but the group difference was far from significant (p = 0.62). Non-meditators averaged 988 milliseconds for words and 1,094 for nonwords; meditators, 969 and 1,008. Errors and missed answers were rare in both groups, about one of each per 50 items. The authors had thought meditators might respond faster, and suggest that switching from inward attention on the breath to an outside stimulus took time for everyone. Whatever the reason, the brain difference wasn't accompanied by a difference in behavior.

### A Standard Analysis Found No Group Difference

The finding depended on how the brain response was modeled. Standard fMRI analyses assume the response to a brief event has a fixed, short shape. Analyzed that way, the words-minus-nonwords response did not differ between meditators and non-meditators in any region, and neither did a search across the whole brain. The authors explain that the standard shape catches the first rise in the signal but only partly fits a response that lasts longer, which was what their hypothesis was about. So they modeled the response more flexibly and found the difference in how long it lasted, rather than in how strong it was at first.

### The Difference Wasn't About Difficulty

Nonwords took longer to judge than words, so the words-minus-nonwords difference could, in principle, reflect how hard the items were rather than their meaning. The authors checked: across participants, the brain difference in each region was unrelated to the difference in response times (median :term{id="correlation" case="lower"} r = 0.04, none significant).

### Every Interruption Quieted These Regions

Both words and nonwords generally lowered activity in these regions compared with the meditation baseline, in both groups. The authors suggest two reasons: the meditation itself likely involves some spontaneous thinking that registers in these regions, and every letter string forced a switch from inward attention to reading and pressing a button. Words simply lowered it less than nonwords did, and that difference is the response to meaning measured above.

### In Meditators, Activity Dipped Below the Baseline

In a few regions, especially part of the left angular gyrus, a region the authors call central to elaborating concepts, the meditators' response to meaning dropped below the baseline after the word. The authors suggest that refocusing on the breath was so effective in meditators that conceptual processing briefly fell below its usual level during meditation. This is their interpretation of the curves, not something the study tested directly.

### What the Design Can't Show

The authors are candid about the limits:

> the employed cross-sectional experimental design cannot rule out the possibility of a selection bias where the observed effect is not due to the difference in meditative experience between the two groups but to some pre-existing hidden variable

They add that the study had no behavioral measure of what people were thinking after each word, so the shorter tail is inferred from the brain signal alone, and that a larger sample would help. Two further points follow from the setup. The meditation in the scanner was a simplified breath focus done lying down, chosen so the non-meditators could do it too, not seated zazen with its posture and open awareness. And the conceptual processing was triggered by single words, chosen to mimic spontaneous thoughts; the authors suggest emotional words and full sentences as a next step.

## Final Reflection

The paper's title captures its approach: rather than ask meditators to stop thinking, the researchers planted a thought and watched how long its trace lasted. In 12 Zen meditators, the default mode network's response to a word's meaning ended sooner than in 12 people who had never meditated, while both groups judged the words equally well.

The result fits with later work on this site showing [lower default mode activity in experienced meditators during meditation than during an active task](/reduced-dmn-active-task/), and with :term{id="eeg"} studies of meditators [aiming for a mind empty of thought](/emptiness-meditation-eeg/). It adds a twist: the difference here was not in how strongly the brain responded to a word, but in how quickly the response passed.

Still, this was a small, one-time comparison, and the difference appeared only when the response was modeled in detail. It can't show that Zen practice caused the shorter response, and it didn't measure thoughts directly. It is best read as an early, inventive demonstration of a way to study letting go of thoughts in the brain, one that needed larger, longitudinal studies to confirm.
