## Background Information

How much something hurts isn't a direct readout of what the body is sending the brain. Attention, mood and expectation all shape the experience, and pain researchers usually ask about two parts of it separately: how *intense* the sensation is, and how *unpleasant* or bothersome it feels.

By 2010, a few studies had suggested that :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation can lower pain ratings, including earlier work by the same first author, but very little was known about how it works. Which brain areas does meditation recruit, and do they change the way the brain processes the pain signal itself?

This study set out to answer that in beginners, using the most basic form of mindfulness practice, which the authors call :term{id="focused_attention" case="lower"} or :term{id="shamatha"}: keeping attention on the changing sensations of the breath, noticing thoughts and feelings as they come up, letting them go without reacting, and returning to the breath. The authors predicted that meditation would engage areas involved in controlling attention and emotion, such as the prefrontal cortex, the :term[anterior cingulate cortex]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} and the anterior :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, and that this activity would track how much pain relief each person got.

To scan people while they meditated, the team used a less common kind of :term{id="fmri"} called :term[arterial spin labeling]{id="arterial_spin_labeling"}. It gives a fully quantitative measure of blood flow in the brain, and it is less prone than standard fMRI to slow drifts in the signal, which suits steady states like meditation that last for minutes at a time.

## What They Did

Eighteen healthy volunteers were recruited. One was dropped for being barely sensitive to the heat, one for being oversensitive to it, and one for falling asleep during meditation training, leaving 15: six men and nine women, aged 22 to 35 (average 26), none of whom had meditated before.

Each person first came for a practice session to get used to the heat and the rating scale. The heat came from a small probe on the back of the right calf, which alternated every 12 seconds between 49°C, which is painful, and a neutral 35°C, over runs lasting just under 6 minutes. Some runs used only neutral warmth for comparison. After each run, people rated the run as a whole on two 15-centimeter sliding scales, one for intensity ("no pain sensation" to "most intense imaginable") and one for unpleasantness ("not at all unpleasant" to "most unpleasant imaginable").

There were two scanning sessions, one before training and one after:

- **Before training**, people first lay with their eyes closed through a heat run and a neutral run (rest). They were then asked to "meditate by focusing on the changing sensations of the breath" through another heat run and neutral run. Since they hadn't been trained yet, the researchers called this condition "attention to breath" and used it as their comparison: the same instruction, without the meditation skills.
- **Training** came next: four 20-minute sessions on separate days, in groups of one to three, led by a teacher with more than ten years of experience. The lessons built from sitting with attention on the breath at the nostrils, to following the rise and fall of the chest and belly, to practicing with a recording of scanner noise, and finally to meditating lying down with that recording.
- **After training**, people went through the same routine: rest runs first, then the same instruction to meditate on the breath, with heat and neutral runs in each.

That adds up to 80 minutes of instruction in all, and unlike most mindfulness programs, there was no homework:

> Contrary to traditional mindfulness-based training programs, subjects were not required to practice outside of training.

A short mindfulness questionnaire (the Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory) was filled in at the start and at the end. Breathing and heart rate were recorded in the scanner.

## One Big Result: Meditating After Training Lowered Both How Intense and How Unpleasant the Heat Felt

Before training, attending to the breath didn't significantly change pain ratings compared with resting, for intensity (p = .35) or for unpleasantness, which came close (p = .06).

After training, it did. Meditating cut pain intensity ratings by 40% and unpleasantness ratings by 57% compared with resting in the same session. In both cases the drop after training was significantly larger than the change before training, and the authors report that every participant's ratings fell:

> In the present investigation, meditation reduced all subjects’ pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings with decreases ranging from 11 to 70% and from 20 to 93%, respectively.

Scores on the mindfulness questionnaire also rose, from an average of 45.3 before training to 51.8 after it, a 14% increase.

The brain scans showed meditation changing how the pain was processed, through more than one route:

- **Less activity where the pain signal arrives.** During painful heat, meditation reduced activity in the part of the :term[primary somatosensory cortex]{id="somatosensory_cortex"} that maps the leg, where the heat was applied. That change didn't track how much any one person's ratings fell, though, so the authors suggest it happens at an early stage, before the signal becomes a felt experience of pain.
- **More activity in control areas tracked who got the most relief.** People whose intensity ratings fell the most showed the biggest increases in the right anterior insula and the anterior cingulate cortex while meditating. People whose unpleasantness ratings fell the most showed the most activity in the :term[orbitofrontal cortex]{id="orbitofrontal_cortex"}, an area involved in judging how good or bad something is, and the biggest *drop* in activity in the :term{id="thalamus" case="lower"}, the brain's relay station for incoming sensory signals.
- **Different from simply attending to the breath.** Compared with attending to the breath before training, meditating after training engaged more of the middle anterior cingulate, the orbitofrontal cortex on both sides and the right anterior insula.

The authors read the thalamus result as a possible gate: frontal areas may adjust what the thalamus passes on, filtering the pain signal before it reaches the parts of the brain involved in conscious experience. That is an interpretation. The study measured blood flow across large areas, not the gating circuit itself.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Meditation Quieted the Default Mode Network

Compared with resting, meditating after training lowered activity in the :term{id="pcc"} and :term{id="mpfc"}, core parts of the :term{id="dmn"}, which is most active when the mind wanders. It also activated areas that represent sensations from the nose and throat, consistent with people attending closely to their breathing. Even before training, attending to the breath lowered activity in the medial prefrontal cortex compared with rest, which the authors take as a sign that people were actively doing the task.

### Breathing Slowed Sharply, but Didn't Explain the Relief

People breathed more slowly in the second scanning session than in the first (about 14 versus 20 breaths a minute on average), and slowest of all while meditating during the heat, at 9.5 breaths a minute. Earlier studies had linked slower breathing to lower pain ratings, so the team checked whether it accounted for the drop. In the 14 people with usable breathing data from the second session, it didn't significantly track the reductions in intensity or unpleasantness. With so few people, though, a modest link could easily be missed (the :term[correlations]{id="correlation"} were −0.35 and −0.24). Breathing data were also lost for five people in the first session because of equipment problems.

### Focused Attention vs. Open Monitoring

This study taught focused attention, and it lowered how *intense* the pain felt as well as how unpleasant. The authors contrast this with :term{id="open_monitoring" case="lower"}, a style in which meditators let the sensation in fully, without judging it. Studies of that style had found it reduced unpleasantness but not intensity, and a later study found the same pattern in [expert meditators](/activation-during-anticipation-pain/), who rated painful heat as just as intense as novices did but less unpleasant. The authors suggest the two styles may work differently:

> Thus, focused attention may attenuate pain by altering the elaboration of nociceptive information to pain, whereas open monitoring promotes a non-evaluative stance to a fully experienced sensory event.

That is a hypothesis. Neither this study nor the expert study compared the two styles directly.

### Why the Missing Control Group Matters

Every comparison here is within the same 15 people, and there was no group that went through a sham or different program. The authors acknowledge this. Ratings during rest were similar in both sessions, which they take as evidence that getting used to the heat, the scale or the scanner can't explain the results. And the first session works as a partial control: the same people, given the same instruction to focus on the breath in the same order (rest first), didn't get significant relief before training.

What it can't control for is expectation. After four days of lessons, people knew they had been trained to meditate and were being asked to meditate during pain. They may have expected it to help, or wanted to show that it did. As the authors put it, a sham meditation group "would have allowed better assessment of :term[demand characteristics]{id="demand_characteristics"} related to meditation's reputation." They argue that the links between pain relief and activity in control areas point to more than biased reporting, but expectations themselves work through some of those same areas. The authors themselves note that :term{id="placebo" case="lower"} pain relief has also been linked to orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate activity, though they argue that meditation differs because it is a deliberately produced mental state. The same lab later ran that test, comparing mindfulness with a sham meditation and a placebo cream ([Zeidan et al., 2015](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/)).

The relationships between brain activity and pain relief also rest on differences among just 15 people, which are easy to overstate in samples this small. And the participants were healthy young adults rating a few minutes of lab heat. The authors suggest meditation "may serve as an effective adjunct therapy in clinical settings," but this study didn't test anyone with :term{id="chronic_pain" case="lower"}.

## Final Reflection

This study came at a time when little was known about how meditation changes pain in the brain, and its main result is striking: four lessons totaling 80 minutes, with no practice at home, were enough for beginners to rate painful heat as much less intense and less unpleasant while meditating, and the brain scans gave a first picture of how. Meditation seemed to work at more than one level, damping the incoming pain signal, recruiting areas that regulate attention and emotion, and possibly gating what the thalamus passes on.

It also fits a broader pattern on this site. In an earlier study led by the same first author, [four 20-minute mindfulness sessions](/brief-mindfulness-training-cognition/) improved some measures of attention and :term{id="working_memory" case="lower"}. The authors cite that result in arguing that meditation eases pain partly by strengthening cognitive control, through pathways it may share with other mental pain-relief strategies such as attentional control, expectation and placebo.

The study's design leaves its biggest question open, though. Without a group trained in something that looked like meditation but wasn't, it can't separate mindfulness from the expectation that mindfulness will help. It also measured meditation during a few minutes of lab heat, not lasting relief. For people living with chronic pain, trials of longer programs such as :term{id="mbsr"} have found more modest benefits: [slightly more pain relief than matched programs](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/), and in one trial [less pain and opioid misuse than a support group](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/). This study is best read as a promising, small demonstration of what a trained mind can do with pain in the moment, and a map of where in the brain to look next.
