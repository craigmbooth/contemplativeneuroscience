## Background Information

By the early 2010s, a string of MRI studies had reported that experienced meditators' brains differed in structure from those of non-meditators: a thicker cortex in parts of the prefrontal cortex and the right :term{id="insula" case="lower"} in insight meditators, a thicker :term[anterior cingulate]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} in :term{id="zen"} practitioners, and more :term{id="gray_matter" case="lower"} in various regions in other traditions. Most of this research, the authors of this paper point out, had looked at practices that train attention. If training the mind can change the brain regions it uses, a practice built around emotion might be expected to change different ones.

:term{id="loving_kindness"} (LKM) is such a practice. The authors describe it this way:

> LKM practitioners explicitly cultivate positive feelings, generating an emotional state that is full of unconditional love, compassion and empathy toward the self and others, without any discursive thoughts

At the time, no one had looked at brain structure in loving-kindness meditators. A few :term{id="fmri"} studies by Antoine Lutz and colleagues had found that, while meditating on compassion and hearing emotional human sounds, experienced practitioners activated the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} and the right :term[temporoparietal junction (TPJ)]{id="tpj"} more than novices did. The TPJ is a hub for working out what other people think and feel. So Mei-Kei Leung, Tatia Lee and their colleagues in Hong Kong predicted that loving-kindness experts would have more gray matter not only in regions earlier studies had linked to attention or :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"}, but also in the right TPJ and the amygdala.

## What They Did

The study compared 25 healthy, right-handed Chinese men. Ten were loving-kindness experts, recruited from a Buddhist meditation network in Hong Kong, who had practiced in the :term{id="theravada"} tradition for at least five years. They reported an average of 6,456 hours of practice, though this varied enormously, from 588 to 14,600 hours.

The other 15 were novices who were interested in meditation. To make the two groups more alike in their motivation to meditate, the novices had done a total of 7 hours of meditation practice at home, following written instructions in concentration, calming and kindness-cultivation skills prepared by one of the study's authors, the Venerable Jing Yin, whom the paper describes as an expert in meditation. The groups were similar in age (about 50 and 48 on average) and did not differ significantly in years of education (14.0 for the experts and 16.9 for the novices). Only men were recruited, the authors explain, to avoid possible sex differences :term{id="confounding" case="lower"} a first exploratory study.

Each man had one structural MRI scan of the brain at 3 tesla. The team analyzed the scans with :term{id="voxel_based_morphometry" case="lower"}, using a method designed to line up small structures across brains more precisely, and compared gray matter volume between the groups at every point across the whole brain.

Testing every point in the brain raises the problem of :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}. The team counted a difference only when a cluster of points each passed p < 0.01 and the cluster was larger than 530 voxels. Their simulations put that combination as equivalent to p < 0.05 after correction. They also reported weaker "trend-level" results, equivalent to a corrected p < 0.1, which required clusters of more than 463 voxels.

Finally, in the 10 experts, they checked whether practice hours tracked gray matter volume in the regions that differed. The paper reports no measures of empathy, emotion or mood; apart from the scans, its only data are age, education and practice hours.

## One Big Result: Meditators Had More Gray Matter in the Right Angular Gyrus and Right Posterior Parahippocampal Gyrus

Two regions passed the main threshold, both on the right side of the brain, and in both the experts had more gray matter than the novices:

- **The right :term{id="angular_gyrus" case="lower"}** (corrected p = 0.015, a cluster of 655 voxels). It lies at the back of the :term{id="inferior_parietal_lobule" case="lower"} and is one of the main parts of the TPJ, the region the authors had predicted.
- **The right posterior :term{id="parahippocampal_gyrus" case="lower"}** (corrected p = 0.049, 531 voxels), on the inner surface of the temporal lobe next to the :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}. This cluster only just cleared the 530-voxel cut-off.

No region had more gray matter in the novices than in the experts. Both differences held after accounting for age and education, and the paper reports that they also held when the experts were compared with a different group of people who had never meditated at all, though the details of that comparison are only in the paper's supplementary data.

The authors note that no earlier structural study of meditators had reported a difference in the right angular gyrus. Given its role in understanding other people's minds and in cognitive empathy, they consider it "unique to LKM." They link both regions to emotion, citing earlier reports that loving-kindness practice reduces anxiety and negative feelings, and offer this, labeled as speculation:

> Taking these findings together, we speculate that the enlarged right angular and right parahippocampal gyri (and the left temporal lobe at a trend level) in LKM experts may play a role in counteracting anxiety and negative affect.

Since the study measured neither anxiety nor emotion, that link is a hypothesis for future work rather than something the data show. The paper's abstract says these regions "are important for affective regulation associated with empathic response, anxiety and mood"; that is a statement about what other research has found these regions do, not about what this study tested.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### More Practice Went With Less Gray Matter, Not More

If long practice built up these regions, the experts with the most hours might be expected to have the most gray matter there. The data pointed the other way. Among the 10 experts, more practice hours went with *less* gray matter in the right angular gyrus (:term[r]{id="correlation"} = −0.618, p = 0.057), a trend that reached significance once age and education were taken into account (r = −0.761, p = 0.028). There was a similar trend in the left middle temporal gyrus (r = −0.593, p = 0.071) and no relationship in the parahippocampal gyrus (r = −0.079).

The authors point to fMRI findings that activity in some regions rises with meditation experience and then falls again in the most experienced practitioners, an :term[inverted U]{id="inverted_u"}, and speculate that gray matter might rise in earlier practice (under roughly 2,000–5,000 hours) before falling. That idea wasn't tested here, and these correlations come from just 10 men.

### The Left Temporal Lobe Showed a Weaker Difference

At the more lenient trend-level threshold, the experts also had more gray matter in a cluster spanning the left inferior and middle temporal gyri (corrected p = 0.091, 478 voxels). The authors note that earlier studies of insight meditation and of meditators from several traditions had reported similar differences in the left :term{id="inferior_temporal_gyrus" case="lower"}, also partly at lenient thresholds, so they read this as a possible general effect of meditation rather than one specific to loving-kindness, and they urge caution because it was only a trend.

### Some Predicted Regions Showed No Difference

Not every predicted region turned up. The amygdala, in particular, did not differ, and the authors state that some hypothesized regions showed no difference "no matter how lenient a threshold was used." They suggest the mechanisms behind structural and functional brain changes may differ, or that the small sample lacked the power to detect differences in emotion-related regions such as the medial :term{id="orbitofrontal_cortex" case="lower"} and the insula, or that some changes happen only at particular stages of practice.

### The Limits the Authors Acknowledge

The authors list three limitations themselves: only 10 experts, because they were hard to recruit; results that may not apply to women; and the design:

> Second, like other cross-sectional studies comparing brain structure between experts and novices, we cannot definitively say that long-term LKM caused the gray matter differences observed in this study or that people with these brain differences are more inclined to practice LKM.

Two other points are worth keeping in mind. The experts' practice ranged more than twentyfold, from 588 to 14,600 hours, so "expert" covers very different levels of experience. And, as the authors note, meditators usually start with attention or mindfulness practice before moving on to other forms, which is why they expected differences in attention-related regions too. Any differences in the experts may reflect years of meditation in general, not loving-kindness alone.

## Final Reflection

This was the first study to look at brain structure in loving-kindness meditators, and it found what its authors hoped to see: more gray matter in a region tied to understanding others, the right angular gyrus, and in a region linked to emotion, the right posterior parahippocampal gyrus. Neither had been reported in earlier studies of attention-based practices.

The evidence is thin, though. Ten men were compared with fifteen at a single point in time, one of the two clusters only just passed the threshold, and the experts with the most practice had *less* angular gyrus gray matter, not more. Without measures of empathy or emotion, the links to those abilities are the authors' interpretation.

Later work on this site puts the finding in context. A [meta-analysis of 21 structural studies](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/), which included loving-kindness practitioners, found that meditators' brains differed only modestly, in a handful of regions, and flagged small studies and :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"} as reasons the differences may be overstated. An [influential small study of an eight-week mindfulness course](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/) reported denser gray matter near the left temporoparietal junction, but a [much larger pair of trials](/mindfulness-brain-structure/) found no structural changes after the same course. And a [nine-month training study](/social-brain-plasticity/) that followed 332 adults over time found that compassion-based and perspective-taking training were each linked to cortical thickening in different regions, the kind of design needed to show cause and effect. Like [another small comparison of loving-kindness meditators](/loving-kindness-self-bias/), this study is best read as an early, intriguing clue rather than evidence that loving-kindness practice reshapes the brain.
