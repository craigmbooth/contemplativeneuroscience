## Background Information

Repeating a word or phrase, a :term{id="mantra" case="lower"}, is one of the oldest and most widespread meditation practices. The authors open by describing a mantra as "a sound, word, or group of words whose repetition can exert calm, or mental quiescence, without the need for intense concentrative efforts." But mantra meditation is rarely just repetition. It is usually practiced within a tradition, with training and spiritual aims, and with a word selected for its spiritual or physiological qualities.

This study, from the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel, set out to strip all of that away and ask what the bare act of silent repetition does to the brain. The researchers scanned people who had never meditated while they silently repeated an ordinary word, "one," with no spiritual framing at all. Unlike a traditional mantra, the word was not chosen for any special qualities.

Their focus was the :term[default mode network]{id="dmn"} (DMN), a set of brain regions that tends to be active when the mind is turned inward: thinking about oneself, planning, or :term[mind wandering]{id="mind_wandering"}. Many contemplative traditions treat mind wandering as a distraction, and the authors cite research linking such thinking to emotional stress, including the [Killingsworth and Gilbert experience-sampling study](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/). Their hypothesis was that silent repetition might switch off widespread brain activity, including the DMN, and that this could be part of why mantra practice is described as calming.

The first author, Aviva Berkovich-Ohana, also led a study on this site of [long-term meditators trying to step outside time and space](/timelessness-meditation-meg/).

## What They Did

Twenty-three healthy, right-handed adults (average age 40, eight women), none of whom had ever practiced meditation, lay in an :term{id="fmri"} scanner with their eyes closed. Brief spoken cues through headphones told them what to do. Two parts of a longer scanning session are reported here:

- **Repetitive speech.** "Silently say 'one' in a comfortable speed," using the Hebrew word *echad*. Five 21-second blocks were mixed in with two other tasks (a tone-discrimination task and a thought-control task), and every block was followed by 12 seconds of rest ("rest as best as you can"). The run took about 8 minutes.
- **Verbal fluency.** Silently producing as many new words as possible starting with a cued letter, a standard :term[verbal fluency task]{id="verbal_fluency_test"}. Again five 21-second blocks, mixed in with three other tasks, in an 11-minute run.

The main comparison was each task against rest. To make that baseline steadier, the researchers averaged all the rest periods in a run (15 in the repetition run), not just the ones next to the task. The scanner measures the :term[BOLD signal]{id="bold_signal"}, an indirect marker of activity: a drop relative to rest is called a deactivation. A separate visual task with eyes open let the team map the default mode network in the same participants for comparison.

The team also checked two things that could fake a widespread drop in the signal. For 12 of the 23 participants, they recorded breathing during the scan. And they reran the analysis with the rest periods from both runs pooled into one baseline, in case activity from the tasks had been "leaking" into the rests.

After each run, participants briefly described their experience. Only later, after seeing the brain results, did the team run a separate questionnaire study with 30 different people.

## One Big Result: Silent Repetition Turned Activity Down Across the Cortex Without Turning Anything Up

Compared with rest, silently repeating "one" lowered activity across a large part of the cortex. The drops included the :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"} and :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"} on both sides, the :term[anterior cingulate cortex]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"}, the right :term[inferior parietal lobule]{id="inferior_parietal_lobule"}, parts of the frontal lobes and the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}. These areas overlapped substantially with the default mode network mapped independently in the same participants. Not every part of the network went down: the :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, a memory region, did not, which the authors attribute to the task asking little of memory.

The striking part was what did *not* happen. Most tasks that quiet the default mode network also switch on other areas that do the work, and the two tend to rise and fall in opposition. Here, no cortical or subcortical area was significantly more active during repetition than at rest. The authors put it this way:

> Our fMRI results show that a surprising level of widespread negative responses can emerge in the human cortex when participants perform a repetition of a single word.

Word generation, the comparison task, behaved like a typical task. It strongly activated frontal language areas, including :term[Broca's area]{id="brocas_area"} and the :term[dorsolateral prefrontal cortex]{id="dlpfc"}, while also lowering activity in regions that largely matched the default mode network. The depth of the drops looked roughly similar in the two tasks. In other words, repeating a word didn't quiet the default mode network more than generating words did. What set it apart was that the quieting came without a matching rise elsewhere.

The team checked this with a count of every cortical voxel (a small cube of brain tissue) in each person. During repetition there were significantly more voxels with a significant drop than with a significant rise (p = 0.014); during word generation the two counts were similar (p = 0.8). An index of how lopsided the counts were differed sharply between the tasks (p < 0.0005). In a region of Broca's area defined in each person, repetition produced only a marginal rise (t = 2.6, p = 0.06 after correction), against a strong rise for word generation (t = 11.0).

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Breathing and the Baseline Didn't Explain It Away

A change in breathing can shift the fMRI signal across the whole brain, and a monotonous task might plausibly slow it. In the 12 participants whose breathing was recorded, none of five measures (pace, inhale length, depth and two others) differed significantly between repetition and rest. When the analysis used the pooled rest periods from both runs, the difference between the tasks in how lopsided their voxel counts were remained significant, but more weakly (p < 0.05). The authors acknowledge that differences in the rest baseline "might account partly" for the effect.

### What It Felt Like

The participants' comments after the scan were brief and varied. Some examples: "it was easy to do, but monotonous and boring"; "it was similar to the rest, just deeper, there were no thoughts"; and "it was more relaxed and easy compared to the rest period."

### Fewer Thoughts, but Also Fewer Sensations

To put numbers on the experience, the team asked 30 research students (average age 26, 17 women) to sit with their eyes closed for one minute of resting and one minute of silently repeating "one," in counterbalanced order. The paper doesn't say whether they had meditated. After each minute they rated 27 questions, written by the first author, of the form "To what extent did you experience…", from 1 (low) to 5 (high). Eleven questions were later judged ambiguous and dropped, and four of the authors grouped the rest into "Thoughts" (seven questions) and "Sensations" (three).

Thoughts were rated as less frequent during repetition than during rest (p < 0.0001), with the clearest drops for thoughts about oneself and about the future. Sensations, such as bodily and sensory awareness, also dropped (p < 0.0005). Control and concentration went the other way, higher during repetition. During rest, people who reported more thoughts tended to report fewer sensations (r = −0.24); during repetition that tradeoff all but disappeared (r = −0.04).

The paper's abstract summarizes this study as showing "a significant reduction in intrinsic thought processes," but the ratings show a broader dampening: outward-facing sensations dropped too. The form did include an item on physical relaxation, and the paper's Figure 6 marks no significant difference on it between repetition and rest. The authors flag the bigger limitation themselves:

> Importantly, it should be cautioned that our inference about the cognitive manifestations of the fMRI results was limited by having the detailed behavioral reports obtained in a separate experiment.

### A "Gate" Rather Than a Seesaw

The usual account of default mode deactivation is a seesaw: task areas switch on and push inward-focused areas down. Because repetition lowered activity without raising any, the authors propose a different idea, a gate. Small activity in one key bottleneck region might shut down every competing process that depends on it, much as, in the :term{id="gate_control_theory" case="lower"} of pain, a light touch can block pain signals. They suggest Broca's area or the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex as candidate gatekeepers, but this study couldn't identify one, and they say further research is needed.

### Why Earlier Mantra Studies Hadn't Seen This

Previous brain-imaging studies of mantra meditation mostly reported increases in activity, and a meta-analysis the authors cite found no significant deactivations. The authors offer an explanation: those studies usually compared mantra meditation with other language tasks, such as repeating pseudo-words or generating words, rather than with rest. As their own word-generation task showed, such tasks also lower default mode activity, so subtracting one from the other could hide drops that both produce. They also note a study in which a looser, nondirective form of mantra meditation, which allows the mind to wander, raised default mode activity compared with a concentrated form. "Mantra meditation" covers practices that may not act alike.

## Final Reflection

This is a neat, focused experiment. By removing the tradition, the teacher and the meaningful word, it showed that the plain act of silently repeating a word, done by people who had never meditated, was enough to lower activity across large parts of the cortex, centered on the default mode network, without switching on any other region to a significant degree. That fits other work on this site: in experienced Insight meditators, [default mode activity was lower during meditation than during an active task](/reduced-dmn-active-task/).

How far it speaks to mantra meditation is a separate question, and the authors' own answer varies. In the discussion they are careful:

> This intentionally omitted the wider context and spiritual orientations of commonly practiced Mantra practices. Hence, the comparison with commonly practiced Mantra studies should be done with caution.

Their conclusions go further, saying the study "suggests a neuronal mechanism that may account for the uniquely calming effect of Mantra meditative practice, explaining its wide use across cultural and historical boundaries." The data are thinner than that. Calm and relaxation were never measured in the scanner, the questionnaire's relaxation item showed no significant change, and the repetition lasted 21 seconds at a time, against the many minutes of real practice. The authors raise the block length themselves, noting that 30-second blocks have been used in other mantra studies. The scanned group was 23 people, breathing was checked in only about half of them, and the experience ratings came from a different group after a single minute of each condition.

The question mark in the title is the right note to end on. The study offers a plausible piece of the puzzle: repetition alone seems to quiet the brain's inward-focused systems in an unusually one-sided way. Whether that is what makes mantra practice calming, in trained meditators practicing for real, is something this study raises rather than settles.
