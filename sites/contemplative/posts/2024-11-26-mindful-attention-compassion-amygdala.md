## Background Information

The :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} is a pair of almond-shaped structures deep in the brain that help flag what is emotionally important, from threats to pleasant surprises. How strongly it reacts to emotional images varies from person to person and has been linked to personality traits, to habitual ways of regulating emotion, and to conditions such as depression and anxiety.

By 2012, a few studies had found that the amygdala responds less to emotional material while people meditate, both in beginners after a short course and in some experienced meditators. But those studies scanned people *in* a meditative state. The bigger question is whether training changes how the brain reacts in everyday life, when nobody is meditating. The authors explain why they designed their study around that question:

> Since we were interested in putative changes in affective trait, study participants were not instructed to enter a meditative state, so that changes in brain activity would reflect uncontrived emotional responses without being influenced by a purposeful manipulation of brain state.

They also wanted to compare two quite different kinds of meditation. Mindful-attention practice trains steady, clear awareness and might be expected to dampen emotional reactions. Compassion practice deliberately cultivates feeling for other people's suffering, and an earlier study had found that when experienced meditators entered a compassionate meditative state, their amygdala responded *more* strongly to emotional sounds of human voices, such as a woman screaming. The two kinds of training might therefore push the amygdala in opposite directions.

## What They Did

The participants were part of a larger study at Emory University in Atlanta, the Compassion and Attention Longitudinal Meditation (CALM) study. They were healthy, medication-free adults aged 25 to 55 with no meditation experience. In this :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, people were randomly assigned to one of three eight-week programs, each with 2 hours of class a week:

- **Mindful Attention Training (MAT).** A simplified version of the :term{id="shamatha"} training taught at three-month retreats such as the Shamatha Project (the source of [a study of perception and vigilance on retreat](/shamatha-retreat-perception-vigilance/)), with its compassion and ethics content removed. It moved from :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} of breathing, to watching thoughts and emotions come and go, to "awareness of awareness," combining :term[focused attention]{id="focused_attention"} and :term[open monitoring]{id="open_monitoring"}.
- **Cognitively-Based Compassion Training (CBCT).** A secular program based on Tibetan Buddhist :term{id="lojong"}, designed by one of the authors, Lobsang Tenzin Negi. It started with attention training and then worked through compassion for oneself, equanimity toward friends, enemies and strangers, appreciation and empathy, ending in "active compassion" for others. An earlier version of this program, taught to Emory students, is the subject of [a trial on stress and inflammation](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/) by several of the same researchers.
- **Health discussion (control).** An :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"} adapted from a university health education course, covering topics such as diet, exercise, sleep, relationships and stress, with talks, small-group discussions and role-play. It matched the class time; instead of meditating at home, participants reported each week which of the previous week's health tips they had started using.

Both meditation groups were asked to practice for an average of 20 minutes a day at home and to log it.

Fifty-one volunteers (average age 34) agreed to be scanned, and they had their first scan before being randomized. A second scan followed within three weeks after the program ended. Five people dropped out, and 10 fell asleep or moved too much in the scanner in at least one session, leaving 12 people in each group for the analysis. The scientists running the scans didn't know who was in which group until all the scans were done.

In the :term{id="fmri"} scanner, people saw photos of people from the International Affective Picture System, a database of emotional images used in research: a third positive, a third negative (typically people suffering) and a third neutral, each shown for 5 seconds. They were simply told to press a button whenever a picture appeared and to "let yourself react to them naturally." The word "meditation" was avoided throughout, and at the end everyone said they had not been meditating during the pictures.

The analysis focused on two :term[regions of interest]{id="region_of_interest"}, the left and right amygdala, traced on each person's own brain scan. The researchers also gave everyone the Beck Depression Inventory and the :term[Beck Anxiety Inventory]{id="beck_anxiety_inventory"}, standard questionnaires, before and after the programs.

## One Big Result: Mindful-Attention Training Lowered the Right Amygdala's Response to Emotional Photos

Before training, the three groups' amygdala responses did not differ significantly. The control group looked lower in the figure below, and the authors suggest this may reflect the larger share of men in that group.

After training, there was a significant difference between groups in how the right amygdala's response to all the photos changed (p < 0.035). Follow-up comparisons showed the change in the mindful-attention group was significantly larger than in the control group, while the compassion group did not differ significantly from either.

Looking within each group:

- **Mindful-attention group:** the right amygdala's response fell for all images taken together (p = 0.012) and for positive images (p = 0.011). Its response to negative images also fell, but not significantly, and its response to neutral images didn't change.
- **Compassion group:** the response to positive images fell by about as much as in the mindful-attention group on average, but this wasn't statistically significant (p = 0.085). Its response to negative images rose slightly, also not significantly.
- **Control group:** no significant changes or trends.

The left amygdala showed no effects or trends, and a whole-brain search found no significant before-and-after differences in any group.

The authors read this as evidence that the training itself caused the change, since the design was controlled and the control group didn't change. Two details call for some care. The positive-image result comes from a within-group test, so it shows that the mindful-attention group changed, not that it changed more than the controls; the one between-group difference reported is for all images together. And the control group's response, which started lower, did not fall. The authors suggest two possible explanations: that mindful-attention training let people give the images more attention, or that it raised their everyday positive mood, though they note they didn't measure mood directly.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Compassion Training: A Rise to Images of Suffering That Went With Lower Depression Scores

The compassion group's small, non-significant rise in amygdala response to negative images was larger in people who had practiced more, and those who practiced least showed a small drop, but this relationship also fell short of significance (r = 0.46, p = 0.13).

The researchers then compared each compassion trainee's change in amygdala response to negative images with their change in depression score. Those with a bigger rise in amygdala response tended to have a bigger drop in depression score (a :term{id="correlation" case="lower"} of 0.58, p = 0.048, across 12 people). In the mindful-attention group there was no such link (r = 0.06), and neither group showed a link with anxiety scores.

This runs against the usual picture, in which depression goes with a stronger amygdala response to negative images. The authors note that earlier studies typically used faces showing sadness or anger, whereas their negative photos showed people in difficult situations, which may be more likely to evoke compassion. They offer a tentative interpretation:

> One might speculate that these images of suffering inspired more compassion in the participants after compassion training, which may itself be related to an increase in amygdala activation, as seen in experts (Lutz et al., 2008a).

That is worth holding loosely. The link rests on 12 people, it was found while following up a non-significant trend, and its p-value only just crosses the usual threshold.

### Depression and Anxiety Scores Didn't Change Differently Between Groups

Questionnaire results are reported for 41 people who completed the scanning study and the questionnaires (a few more than the 36 whose scans were analyzed). Scores in these healthy volunteers were low to begin with: group averages of about 3 to 6 for depression and 3 to 5 for anxiety. Anxiety and depression scores fell over time across everyone, but the groups didn't change differently (p = 0.10 for both). In an exploratory analysis, only the compassion group's depression scores fell significantly on their own, by about 3 points. In the meditation groups, how much people practiced was not related to their questionnaire scores or to changes in them.

### Practice Fell Short of 20 Minutes a Day

The mindful-attention group logged an average of 645 minutes of home practice over the eight weeks (range 210 to 1,491), and the compassion group 454 minutes (range 190 to 905), a difference that wasn't significant. That works out to roughly 11 and 7.5 hours, well under the 20 minutes a day participants were asked for.

### Why the Compassion Results Were Unclear

The authors give two reasons the compassion group's rise in response to images of suffering might not have reached significance. One is the small sample:

> This raises the possibility that our study simply did not have sufficient statistical power, with our relatively small sample size of twelve subjects per group.

The other is that CBCT begins with attention training much like MAT. If attention training lowers the amygdala's response and compassion training raises it, the two could cancel out. They point to the low-practice compassion trainees, who looked more like the mindful-attention group, as a hint that the compassion-specific effect only appeared with more practice. Both explanations are possibilities the study couldn't test.

### A Small Inconsistency About Men and Women

Before training, women's right amygdala responded more strongly to the photos than men's. The results section reports this difference as significant (p = 0.038), while the discussion calls it "a trend which was almost statistically significant (p = 0.059)." Either way, the authors say the study was too small and unbalanced to examine gender, and they suggest it as a question for future work.

## Final Reflection

This was among the first studies to look for changes in how the brain reacts to emotional material when people who had trained in meditation weren't meditating, which is closer to what matters in daily life. Its main finding is that after eight weeks of mindful-attention training, the right amygdala responded less to emotional photos of people, and this change was larger than in a health-class control group. The compassion results were more tantalizing than solid: a trend toward a stronger response to images of suffering, which in a dozen people went with falling depression scores.

The design has real strengths: random assignment, a first scan taken before randomization, an active control matched for class time, and scanner staff who didn't know the groups. But with 12 people per group, a single between-group result and several findings resting on within-group tests or trends, it is best read as an early, suggestive study. Other work on this site has looked at the amygdala's structure rather than its activity: an [MBSR study](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/) linked bigger drops in stress to lower :term{id="gray_matter" case="lower"} density in the right amygdala, while a [larger analysis of two randomized trials](/mindfulness-brain-structure/) found no structural change. Whether training reliably changes the amygdala's day-to-day reactions needed larger trials to settle.
