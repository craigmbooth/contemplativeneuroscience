## Background Information

People who are depressed or anxious tend to process emotional information in a lopsided way. Their attention and memory lean toward negative things and away from positive ones, while healthy, happy people tend to have a mild bias toward the positive. Losing that positive tilt, or gaining a negative one, predicts later episodes of depression and anxiety. Treatments that shift these biases, from :term[cognitive behavioral therapy]{id="cbt"} to antidepressants to :term[cognitive bias modification]{id="cognitive_bias_modification"} (computer exercises that train attention toward positive or away from negative material), tend to ease symptoms as they do so.

Douglas Roberts-Wolfe, Willoughby Britton and colleagues at Brown University wondered whether meditation might work partly the same way. A central feature of meditation is training attention, and the authors suggest it may strengthen the brain's top-down control over emotional reactions. If so, meditators might come to process emotional information differently, and that might help explain why meditation often improves well-being.

The few earlier studies on the question had clear gaps. One tested people only after a mindfulness course, so it couldn't show change; another used a single 12-minute breathing exercise just before the test, which says little about longer training. And their comparison groups did nothing comparable, so any effect could have come from effort, expectation or simply being in a group. This study measured people before and after 12 weeks of training and compared them with students in courses chosen to be similar.

## What They Did

The researchers recruited 58 undergraduates (28 women, average age 20) from three kinds of courses between 2008 and 2010. Thirty-five took a course that included "meditation laboratories." The other 23, an :term[active control group]{id="active_control_group"}, took either music courses with practice labs (a similar mix of lectures and hands-on practice) or religious studies or East Asian studies courses with similar content but no meditation, some taught by the same professor and some voted by students as "inspirational and transformative." The paper doesn't say how many students came from each kind of comparison course. Students chose their own courses; nobody was randomly assigned.

The meditation labs met for an hour three times a week. Each included about 30 minutes of practice from Buddhist or Taoist traditions, followed by 5 to 10 minutes of written reflection and questions. They were taught by the course professor, Harold Roth, a scholar of Buddhist and Taoist contemplative practice and a co-author, with more than 30 years of :term{id="zen"} practice. Students practiced both :term{id="shamatha"} and :term{id="vipassana"}: holding attention on one object, like the breath, or on a class of objects, like body sensations, but not open, objectless awareness. Although the paper calls this :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} training, its emphasis differed from courses such as :term{id="mbsr"}:

> Meditation instruction emphasized attention allocation rather than the acceptance that is central to Western styles of mindfulness, although it incorporated ideals of letting go of evaluation.

At the start and end of the 12-week course, everyone did an emotional memory task. They saw one list of words on a screen and heard a second list read aloud. Each list had six positive words, six negative and six neutral, plus neutral "buffer" words at the start and end, and the lists were balanced for word length, how common the words are and how strongly they stir people up. Participants rated each word for pleasantness and :term{id="arousal" case="lower"}, which made them think about it. They had been told only that they "may be asked to recall these words sometime in the future," but right after each list they were asked to recall as many words as they could, in any order (:term[free recall]{id="free_recall"}). Counts were added across both lists, so a person could recall up to 12 words of each kind. Different word lists were used before and after.

Participants also completed two questionnaires: the Scales of Psychological Well-Being, which covers self-acceptance, purpose in life, personal growth, good relationships, autonomy and mastery of daily life, and the Mood and Anxiety Symptom Questionnaire, which measures symptoms of depression and anxiety.

## One Big Result: Meditators' Recall of Positive Words Rose to Match the Comparison Group's

Meditators recalled more positive words after the course than before: on average 4.0 of 12 at the start and 4.9 at the end, a significant rise. The comparison group went slightly the other way, from 5.4 to 4.9, a change that wasn't statistically significant. The difference between the two groups' changes was significant (p = .013), with an :term[effect size]{id="effect_size"} between medium and large by the benchmarks the paper uses (partial η² = 0.11). There was no change of note in negative words, neutral words or the total recalled, so meditators weren't simply remembering more of everything.

The authors read this as a shift in emotional processing:

> This increase in positive words occurred in the context of no change in either total word or negative word recall, which suggests an increased efficiency in positive information processing.

The chart shows the important catch. The two groups didn't start level: the comparison students recalled significantly more positive words at the outset (p = .003), and by the end the groups were the same. The authors point out that when a group starts unusually low, its scores can drift up on retesting with no help from the intervention, a statistical effect called :term[regression to the mean]{id="regression_to_the_mean"}:

> These baseline differences raise the possibility that the meditators’ disproportionate increase in positive word recall may be a form of regression to the mean.

They offer two reasons the groups might have differed at the start. The music students were fairly advanced, with hundreds of hours of practice, and music training is linked to stronger :term{id="executive_function" case="lower"}. And some students may have chosen the meditation course because they were struggling emotionally and hoped it would help. Either way, because students picked their own courses (:term[self-selection]{id="self_selection_bias"}), the groups may have differed in ways the study couldn't measure.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Well-Being Rose in the Meditation Group

On the well-being questionnaire, which can range from 84 to 504, meditators' average score rose from 368.2 to 383.1, a significant gain, while the comparison group's barely moved (373.2 to 371.2). The difference in change between the groups was significant (p = .013) and of the same size as for positive words (partial η² = 0.11). Depression and anxiety symptoms moved in the expected directions, falling slightly in meditators (121.3 to 118.1) and rising in the comparison group (118.7 to 123.9), but the difference between the groups fell short of significance (p = .088), and neither group's change was significant by itself. The students' symptoms were in the "mild" range to begin with, which the authors suggest left little room for improvement.

### Positive Memory and Well-Being Moved Together

Across all 58 students, those whose recall of positive words rose more also tended to report larger gains in well-being (:term[correlation]{id="correlation"} r = 0.31) and larger drops in depression and anxiety symptoms (r = −0.29). Changes in negative or neutral word recall weren't linked to either. These are modest correlations, and with only a before and an after measurement the authors say they can't test whether better positive processing *caused* the gains in well-being (a :term[mediation analysis]{id="mediation_analysis"} would need more time points).

### More Positive, Not Less Negative

Earlier studies had found mindfulness reduced memory or attention biases toward negative or threatening material. Here, neither group changed in recall of negative words. The authors suggest that's because these were healthy students without much negative bias to lose, and that a fading positive bias may come before a growing negative one as well-being declines, so positive processing might be the more sensitive early signal. The same pattern, more positive change than negative, showed up in the questionnaires too.

### Possible Brain Pathways, Not Measured Here

The paper discusses several brain regions that could link meditation with a more positive memory bias, including the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}, prefrontal areas that help regulate it and the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, which supports :term{id="interoception" case="lower"}, the sense of the body's internal state. In another paper on this same sample, the meditators showed greater body awareness than the comparison students. But this study didn't scan anyone's brain, so these links are the authors' suggestions rather than findings.

### What a Free Recall Test Can and Can't Show

The authors argue that a memory test like this is less open than a questionnaire to :term[demand characteristics]{id="demand_characteristics"}, cues that nudge people to answer the way they think researchers expect. They also note its limit: because people only wrote down what they remembered, the test can't tell whether meditation changed what they noticed, how they stored the words or how they retrieved them.

## Final Reflection

This small study asked a sensible question: if meditation trains attention, does it change which emotional information sticks? After a semester of meditation labs, students remembered more positive words and reported more well-being, compared with students in other hands-on or similar-content courses, and the two changes went together. That fits with other work in this theme, such as a randomized trial in which a [loving-kindness workshop gradually raised people's daily positive emotions](/loving-kindness-positive-emotions-resources/) without changing negative ones.

But the headline finding is a convergence: the meditators started lower than the comparison students on positive recall and ended level with them. With self-selected groups and that starting gap, regression to the mean and differences in who chose meditation are real alternative explanations, as the authors themselves say. The practice taught also emphasized directing attention rather than the acceptance at the heart of standard mindfulness programs, and students' practice outside class wasn't recorded. The study is best read as an early, intriguing pointer that positive emotional processing might be one route by which meditation improves well-being, one that needs a randomized trial, as the authors call for, to test properly.
