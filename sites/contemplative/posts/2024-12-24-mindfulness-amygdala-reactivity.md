## Background Information

The :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}, a pair of almond-shaped structures deep in the brain, is central to how emotions are generated and regulated. One region that seems to help keep it in check is the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (:term{id="vmpfc"}), on the underside of the frontal lobes. The vmPFC is involved in *automatic* :term[emotion regulation]{id="emotion_regulation"}: the adjustments the brain makes to an emotional reaction without any deliberate effort, as when a feared thing stops seeming dangerous through :term{id="extinction" case="lower"} learning.

:term{id="mindfulness"} meditation trains people to notice thoughts and feelings as they arise and let them pass, without clinging to the pleasant or pushing away the unpleasant. Courses such as :term{id="mbsr"} have been shown to improve mood and emotional recovery, but the brain changes behind those benefits were less clear. Earlier imaging work was small, and few studies compared mindfulness with an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"} (a comparison course that is equally engaging but doesn't teach meditation).

Tammi Kral, Richard Davidson and colleagues at the University of Wisconsin–Madison asked two questions. Do people with years of mindfulness practice show a calmer amygdala response to emotional pictures, and a stronger link between the amygdala and the vmPFC? And does an eight-week MBSR course produce the same pattern, compared with a well-matched control course?

## What They Did

The study had two parts.

**Long-term meditators vs. non-meditators.** The researchers recruited 31 long-term meditators from meditation centers and mailing lists across the United States, and 127 healthy adults with no meditation experience from around Madison. The meditators had practiced at least 30 minutes of sitting meditation a day for at least three years, had experience with :term{id="vipassana"}, concentration and compassion practices, and had been on at least three intensive retreats of five days or more. They averaged 9,081 hours of lifetime practice (range 1,439 to 32,612), 86% of their daily practice being mindfulness-based (:term{id="focused_attention" case="lower"} and :term{id="open_monitoring" case="lower"}). After exclusions for brain abnormalities, a dropout, technical problems and a dental implant that distorted one meditator's scan, 30 meditators and 121 non-meditators were analyzed. The two groups did not differ in age, gender, education, socio-economic status or head movement in the scanner. People who had a psychiatric diagnosis in the past year, or took medication for anxiety or depression, were excluded.

**An eight-week randomized trial.** After their first scan, a subset of the non-meditators took part in a :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial" case="lower"}. Forty-three were assigned to MBSR and 43 to the Health Enhancement Program (HEP), a course designed to match MBSR on everything that isn't specific to mindfulness. Four people didn't complete the brain task afterward and 15 left the study for logistical reasons, so 32 MBSR and 35 HEP participants were scanned again after the course. The experimenters collecting the data didn't know who was in which group.

**The task.** In the :term{id="fmri"} scanner, everyone saw 72 pictures from a standard set of emotional images, 24 each negative, neutral and positive, for 4 seconds apiece, and pressed a button to say which kind each picture was. Nobody was asked to meditate or to regulate their feelings, so the task captured people's ordinary, automatic response.

The researchers focused on two :term[regions of interest]{id="region_of_interest"}. The left and right amygdala were defined from a separate group of 90 people who had viewed similar pictures. The vmPFC region came from a standard brain atlas. For each person, they measured how much more the amygdala responded to negative or positive pictures than to neutral ones. They also used a :term{id="psychophysiological_interaction"} analysis to measure how closely the right amygdala's activity tracked the vmPFC's during emotional pictures compared with neutral ones, a task-specific form of :term[functional connectivity]{id="functional_connectivity"}.

In the trial, they compared the two groups after the course while statistically adjusting for each person's baseline scan. Outlying data points were removed from the tests, and the paper notes where including them changed a result.

## One Big Result: Less Amygdala Response to Positive Pictures After Long-Term and Short-Term Training

Both comparisons pointed the same way, and both were specific to positive pictures and to the right amygdala.

- **Long-term meditators** had a significantly smaller right amygdala response than non-meditators to emotional pictures overall (p = 0.01). Split by type, the difference was significant for positive pictures (p = 0.001) but not for negative ones (p = 0.10).
- **After MBSR**, the combined emotional-picture response did not differ from the control group (p = 0.15). Split by type, MBSR participants had a smaller right amygdala response to positive pictures than HEP participants (p = 0.01), and the two groups hadn't differed on this before training. For negative pictures, the difference was not significant (p = 0.07).

The left amygdala showed no group differences in either comparison. A whole-brain search, corrected for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}, found no regions where the groups differed. The authors summarize:

> Meditation training was associated with less amygdala reactivity to positive pictures relative to controls, but there were no group differences in response to negative pictures.

The negative-picture results were fragile in both directions. With the outliers left in, the meditator vs. non-meditator difference for negative pictures became significant (p = 0.05), while the MBSR vs. HEP difference moved further from significance (p = 0.46).

Why would mindfulness dampen the response to *pleasant* pictures? The authors point out that a quieter amygdala doesn't mean less enjoyment. They draw on research separating "liking" something from "wanting" it, and suggest the amygdala tracks the wanting:

> Therefore, our findings of decreased amygdala activation to positive stimuli are most consistent with a decreased desire to approach (or grasp at) a stimulus rather than a change in pleasure or appreciation.

That fits mindfulness teaching, which asks practitioners not to grasp at pleasant experiences any more than they push away unpleasant ones. But the study didn't measure wanting or pleasure, so this is the authors' interpretation rather than a finding.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### MBSR Strengthened the Amygdala's Link With the vmPFC

The trial's clearest result was in connectivity. After the course, MBSR participants showed stronger coupling between the right amygdala and the vmPFC than HEP participants did, both for negative pictures (p < 0.001, partial r = 0.42) and for positive pictures (p = 0.01, partial r = 0.30), adjusting for baseline. Connectivity didn't differ significantly between the groups at baseline (p = 0.60 for negative pictures, p = 0.06 for positive). It also wasn't related to how much MBSR practice people did.

The long-term meditators did *not* show this pattern. Their amygdala–vmPFC coupling during negative pictures was significantly above zero (p = 0.04), while the non-meditators' wasn't, but the two groups didn't differ significantly from each other (p = 0.20). The authors suggest the trajectory may be curved: early in practice the prefrontal cortex is recruited to calm the amygdala, and with long experience the calmer response becomes automatic and needs less vmPFC help. They also note simpler explanations. The MBSR group all took the same course in the same city, while the meditators varied in practice styles, years of experience, location and motivation.

### Retreat Hours, Not Daily Practice, Tracked the Response to Negative Pictures

Among the long-term meditators, more lifetime hours of retreat practice went with a smaller amygdala response to negative pictures (p = 0.05), adjusting for age. In a post hoc follow-up, hours of open-monitoring (Vipassana) retreat practice, the style closest to MBSR, showed a stronger link (p = 0.02, partial r = −0.47). Lifetime hours of daily practice showed no link (p = 0.64), and no practice measure was related to the response to positive pictures. In the trial, minutes of MBSR practice weren't related to the amygdala response either.

The authors take this to mean that changing the response to negative emotion may take more intensive practice than an eight-week course provides. Two cautions apply: practice hours were self-reported, and a :term{id="correlation" case="lower"} among fewer than 30 meditators can't show that retreats caused the difference.

### Meditators Reported Being Less Reactive, but the Course Didn't Change Self-Reports

On the non-reactivity scale of the :term{id="ffmq"} (items such as "I watch my feelings without getting lost in them"), the long-term meditators scored higher than non-meditators (28.8 vs. 23.4 on average). Across both groups, people who rated themselves less reactive had a smaller amygdala response to positive pictures, and this held after accounting for the wish to present oneself well. But MBSR didn't change self-reported non-reactivity compared with HEP. The authors suggest that shifting self-reports in healthy people may take longer practice, especially against a well-matched control.

### Meditators Saw More Emotional Pictures as Neutral

In an exploratory analysis, the long-term meditators labeled more of the emotional pictures as neutral: 20% of the negative pictures (vs. 8% for non-meditators) and 37% of the positive ones (vs. 18%). Labeling of neutral pictures didn't differ. The authors treat this cautiously. A technical error meant button presses weren't recorded for 84 participants: 61 labeled the pictures online at home afterward and 23 had no data. How often a meditator labeled pictures neutral also wasn't related to their amygdala response.

### What the Study Can't Show

The authors are clear that the task measured brain responses, not emotion regulation itself:

> The current study was limited to inferring that regulation occurred, as there were no measures of participants’ emotional experience or regulation strategies during the task.

They also had no direct behavioral measure of emotion regulation and describe the effects as small to medium, possibly because generic pictures aren't very personal. Beyond that, the meditator comparison is a single snapshot, so the meditators may have differed from non-meditators in ways that predate their practice. The analysis included only the people who completed the course. The paper reports many region-of-interest tests and doesn't report correcting them for their number. And the negative-picture results depended on how outliers were handled.

### Part of a Larger Set of Wisconsin Trials

The randomized part of this study is "sample one" of the two trials later pooled by the same team in [Kral et al. 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/). That larger analysis found that MBSR produced no measurable change in brain *structure*, and cited this study's amygdala results as evidence that the course did change brain *function*. An earlier, uncontrolled study found that people whose stress dropped most during MBSR showed the largest decreases in [right amygdala gray matter density](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/). That was a structural measure in a small sample with no comparison group, so it complements rather than confirms the functional results here.

## Final Reflection

This study is unusual in testing the same question two ways, comparing seasoned meditators with newcomers and randomizing newcomers to mindfulness or a matched course. Both comparisons found a smaller amygdala response to positive pictures. Neither found a clear change for negative ones, although among experienced meditators, more retreat practice went with a smaller response to them. The eight-week course also strengthened the amygdala's coupling with a prefrontal region tied to emotion regulation, a change not seen in the long-term meditators.

Anyone expecting mindfulness mainly to calm reactions to upsetting things may find that surprising. What changed most reliably was the response to pleasant images, which the authors read as less grasping rather than less enjoyment. The effects were modest, the negative-picture results were fragile, and the study measured brain activity rather than how well people actually managed their emotions.
