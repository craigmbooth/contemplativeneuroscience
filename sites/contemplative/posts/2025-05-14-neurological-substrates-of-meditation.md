## Background Information

:term[Loving-kindness meditation]{id="loving_kindness"} (LKM) asks practitioners to focus their attention on wishing well-being to themselves and to others. The authors point out that earlier brain-imaging studies found changes in the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} and :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, two deep structures involved in emotion and memory, after continued loving-kindness or compassion practice (for example, [Leung et al., 2013](/loving-kindness-gray-matter/) and [Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/)). Scalp :term{id="eeg"} studies have also found more :term{id="gamma" case="lower"} activity during meditation ([Braboszcz et al., 2017](/gamma-three-meditation-traditions/)), including during loving-kindness meditation in experienced practitioners ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)).

What nobody had done was record the electrical activity of those deep structures directly while someone meditated. Scalp electrodes can't reach them. People with epilepsy sometimes have electrodes placed inside the brain, but usually only during a hospital stay, which the authors describe as a poor setting for calm meditation.

This study used a different group: people with drug-resistant epilepsy who live with a :term{id="responsive_neurostimulation"} device. Its electrodes stay in place long-term, often in the amygdala and hippocampus, and can record brain activity while the person goes about daily life. The team predicted that loving-kindness meditation would raise gamma activity in both structures. Because :term{id="beta" case="lower"} rhythms are linked to paying attention to the outside world, they also predicted that beta would fall as attention turned inward.

## What They Did

Eight adults with drug-resistant epilepsy took part (three women, average age about 45). All had the device implanted for their treatment, and the electrode positions were chosen for clinical reasons only. All described themselves as novice meditators.

Each person sat through one 15-minute audio recording in a quiet room at Mount Sinai designed to resemble a Japanese teahouse and garden:

* **Baseline (5 minutes):** they listened to instructions about the purpose of meditation and how to do loving-kindness practice well.
* **Meditation (10 minutes):** a guided loving-kindness meditation. They first imagined that "you are filled and overflowing with warmth and love" and then silently repeated phrases such as "may you live with ease, may you be happy, may you be free from pain", directing them first at themselves, then at loved ones, neutral people and all living beings.

For the analysis, the team compared the first 2 minutes of the instructions with 2 minutes from the middle of the meditation. Afterward, participants rated how deep their meditation had been on a 1-to-10 scale. The average was 7.43.

The device records from pairs of neighboring electrode contacts. The team had 13 recording channels they call "amygdala" (each pairs a contact in the amygdala with one in the front of the hippocampus) and 14 "hippocampus" channels. The device takes 250 samples per second, so the analysis stopped at 55 Hz, the low end of the gamma range. About 6% of the data were removed because of epileptic or noisy activity (between 2% and 16% per person), and the amount removed didn't differ between baseline and meditation.

They then looked at the signals in several ways, in five frequency bands from slow :term[delta]{id="delta" case="lower"} (2–4 Hz) to beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (30–55 Hz):

* **Background activity:** the part of the signal that isn't rhythmic, often read as a sign of the overall balance between excitation and inhibition.
* **Presence of rhythms:** how many channels showed a clear rhythm in each band.
* **Strength of rhythms (power):** first across channels, then within each person, comparing every 1-second slice of meditation with the baseline.
* **Duration of rhythms:** how much of the time each rhythm was present.

Because earlier studies pointed to more gamma and less beta during meditation, the team tested beta and gamma with :term[one-sided tests]{id="one_tailed_test"}, which look for a change in the expected direction only.

## One Big Result: Gamma Power Rose in Both the Amygdala and Hippocampus During Meditation

Across the channels where a gamma rhythm was present, gamma power was higher during meditation than during the instructions, in both the amygdala channels (p < 0.01) and the hippocampus channels (p < 0.001). Beta power didn't change at the group level, and neither did power in any of the other bands. These group tests compared recording channels, not people.

Within individual participants, the pattern mostly held. Gamma power rose significantly in the amygdala recordings of 6 of 8 participants and in the hippocampus recordings of 6 of 7 (the paper lists no hippocampal result for participant 7). Beta power, by contrast, shifted only slightly, up in some people and down in others.

In June 2025, PNAS published a correction to the paper (Correction for Maher et al., *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 122(26), e2511043122, [10.1073/pnas.2511043122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2511043122)). As first published, the figure legend listed every participant's hippocampal gamma result as not significant (p > 0.05), contradicting the stated 6 of 7. The correction changed those values to p < 0.001, matching the text. It also fixed two figures that had been swapped during production. The numbers in this summary follow the corrected version.

The authors see this as extending the gamma findings in experienced meditators to beginners:

> These findings complement the earlier results on increased γ oscillations in LKM practices in long-term meditators by showing specific increases in these fast oscillations in novice meditators when directly examining the amygdala and hippocampus with iEEG.

Keep in mind what "higher than baseline" means here. The baseline was listening to instructions about meditation, and it always came first, so meditation is compared with an earlier and different listening task, not with rest or with another kind of exercise.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Beta Rhythms Got Shorter, and Gamma Rhythms Lasted Longer in the Amygdala

Measured by how much of the time each rhythm was present, beta rhythms were present for less of the time during meditation in both the amygdala and the hippocampus (both p < 0.05). Gamma rhythms were present for more of the time in the amygdala (p < 0.01) but not in the hippocampus.

The authors link the beta drop to attention. Beta rhythms, they write, help the brain monitor and gather information from the environment, which loving-kindness meditation sets aside. They also offer a more speculative reading:

> Decreased β oscillations with LKM may also represent a shift from negative emotional states, in turn for more positively salient γ oscillations at the network level.

They float another possibility too: because beta rhythms are involved in preparing movements, the beta change might reflect a motor plan in its early stages. The study wasn't designed to test these interpretations.

At the individual level, the paper describes beta power as changing in only a minority of participants. That matches the amygdala (3 of 8), but the figure legend lists p-values below 0.05 for 4 of 7 participants' hippocampal recordings.

### The Rhythms Changed; the Background Didn't

Background activity showed no difference between baseline and meditation in either structure. Nor did meditation bring rhythms to more channels: beta and gamma rhythms were present on every channel in both conditions, while the slow delta band showed rhythms on only about a third of channels (30.8% in the amygdala, 35.7% in the hippocampus). The change was in the rhythms that were already there: their strength and how long they lasted.

### Amygdala Versus Hippocampus

The paper is not consistent about whether the two structures responded differently. The Results report that "the amygdala displayed a significantly greater increase in the duration of γ oscillations during meditation compared to the hippocampus" (p < 0.05). The Discussion says, "We did not find differences in any oscillatory metric (periodic/aperiodic FOOOF metrics or eBOSC) between the amygdala and hippocampus." The authors also note a caveat for any comparison: each "amygdala" channel pairs an amygdala contact with one at the head of the hippocampus, so the two signals aren't fully separate.

### Self-Rated Depth Didn't Track the Brain Changes

Participants rated their meditation as fairly deep, but those ratings showed no relationship with the brain measures. The authors say this "raises the question of whether participants genuinely engaged in meditation". They also acknowledge that the changes could reflect broader shifts in arousal or mood rather than loving-kindness specifically, and suggest adding heart rate or skin conductance in future studies.

### What the Design Can't Show

The authors list several limits:

* **Eight people.** They note this is typical of studies with implanted electrodes.
* **One short session.** It says nothing about what regular practice does over time, and there was no control period after meditation to see whether any change lasted.
* **One fixed comparison.** The instructions always came first, and there was no second baseline, no reversed order and no other practice for comparison. The authors argue that the seating, the audio and the room were the same throughout, so the meditation itself was the main difference.
* **Frequencies.** The device's sampling rate ruled out activity above 55 Hz, including the high-frequency range often used as a marker of local brain activation.

Two more points are worth knowing. The paper runs tests across five frequency bands, two structures and several measures and doesn't describe a correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}. And the participants all have epilepsy, with electrodes placed where their seizures are thought to start, although the authors say each electrode lead spans just over 30 mm of tissue, so part of the recording likely comes from typical tissue. One author is a paid speaker for NeuroPace, the company that makes the device.

The abstract and closing summary go a little further than the results. The abstract reports changes in the duration of beta and gamma rhythms "in both the amygdala and hippocampus", but the gamma duration change was found only in the amygdala. The closing summary also highlights loving-kindness meditation's "potential as a targeted therapeutic intervention", though the study measured brain activity in a single session and no clinical outcomes.

## Final Reflection

By the authors' account, this is the first study to document changes in intracranial recordings during loving-kindness meditation. Its clearest finding is that gamma power in both structures was higher during a first loving-kindness session than during the instructions that preceded it, across channels and in most participants individually. Beta rhythms became briefer, while background activity and the number of active channels stayed the same.

The comparison is the weak point. Two minutes of meditation were set against two minutes of listening to instructions that always came first, in eight people with epilepsy, and their own sense of how deeply they meditated didn't line up with the brain changes. In the authors' words, it documents brain signals that may contribute to the short-term "state changes" of the practice, not long-term changes. They suggest the same implanted devices could follow novice meditators over months of practice, and compare loving-kindness with other forms of meditation.

For a very different practice and brain region, see [Hagerty et al. (2013)](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/) on :term{id="jhana" case="lower"} meditation and the brain's reward system.
