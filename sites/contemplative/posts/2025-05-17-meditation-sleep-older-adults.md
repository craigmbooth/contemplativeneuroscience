## Background Information

The authors open with how common poor sleep is in later life: an estimated 50% of people aged 55 and older have some form of sleep problem, such as trouble falling or staying asleep. In older adults, even moderate sleep disturbances often come with daytime problems, including fatigue, depressive symptoms and a lower quality of life, and can lead on to clinical insomnia. Yet, the paper notes, these moderate problems often go untreated.

When insomnia is diagnosed, the options are sleeping pills, whose benefits often fade once people stop taking them and which carry risks of next-day effects and dependence, or behavioral treatments such as :term[cognitive behavioral therapy]{id="cbt"}, which the authors describe as intensive, needing highly trained therapists and intended for people with insomnia. They argue that something cheaper and available in the community is needed for the much larger group with moderate sleep problems.

:term{id="mindfulness"} programs, which train people to attend to moment-by-moment experience without judging it, were a candidate. But the authors describe the earlier evidence on mindfulness and sleep as "preliminary yet mixed": sleep had mostly been studied as a side outcome in people with another illness, or in mixed-age groups diagnosed with insomnia. A movement-based practice, tai chi, had been shown to improve sleep quality in older adults, but to the authors' knowledge there were no reports of a "nonmovement form of meditation" being used for older adults' sleep problems. They set out to test whether a mindfulness course would improve sleep quality more than a structured :term{id="sleep_hygiene" case="lower"} course.

## What They Did

In 2012, the researchers recruited adults aged 55 and older in Los Angeles through a newspaper advertisement and flyers at the UCLA medical research center and community centers. To take part, people needed a score above 5 on the :term[Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)]{id="psqi"}, a questionnaire about the past month's sleep scored from 0 to 21, where higher means worse sleep. The study excluded smokers, people with substance dependence, significant depression or cognitive impairment (an :term{id="mmse"} score below 23), anyone already meditating more than 15 minutes a day, people with a body mass index above 34.9, and anyone with sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, an inflammatory disorder or a current illness or infection.

Forty-nine people were randomly assigned, in a :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, to one of two group courses, each meeting for two hours a week for six weeks:

- **Mindful Awareness Practices (24 people):** a standardized community course, taught by a certified teacher with more than 20 years of mindfulness practice. It included sitting meditation, mindful eating, appreciation meditation, :term[loving-kindness]{id="loving_kindness"} meditation, mindful walking and mindful movement, with 10 to 30 minutes of practice in each class alongside teaching and group discussion. Participants got a book on mindfulness and a guided-meditation CD, and their daily home practice started at 5 minutes and built up to 20 minutes by the sixth session.
- **Sleep hygiene education (25 people):** an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"} course, designed by the lead author to match the mindfulness course for time, attention, group interaction and expectation of benefit. A health educator taught sleep biology, healthy and unhealthy sleep, sleep problems, stress biology and stress reduction, self-monitoring of sleep, relaxation methods for improving sleep, and weekly sleep hygiene strategies based on National Institutes of Health and National Sleep Foundation tips. Homework was practicing sleep hygiene and weekly reading.

The participants averaged 66 years old; 67% were women, 84% were white, and they had on average 16.6 years of education. Their average PSQI score of 10.2 put them in the range of moderate or greater sleep disturbance, and their depression and anxiety scores were consistent with a nonclinical sample.

Within 10 days before and after the courses, participants filled in questionnaires alone in a private room and gave a morning blood sample. The main outcome was the PSQI. Secondary outcomes, chosen in advance, were the Athens Insomnia Scale (insomnia symptoms), the :term[Beck Depression Inventory-II]{id="beck_depression_inventory"}, the :term{id="beck_anxiety_inventory"}, the :term{id="perceived_stress_scale"} and the Fatigue Symptom Inventory, scored for how much fatigue interfered with daily life and how severe it was. The team also measured :term{id="nf_kb"}, a switch for :term{id="inflammation" case="lower"} inside cells, in white blood cells; they chose it because disturbed sleep can trigger an inflammatory response. A short form of the :term[Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire]{id="ffmq"} tracked how mindful people were in daily life.

The analysis was :term[intention-to-treat]{id="intention_to_treat"}: all 49 people were analyzed in the group they were assigned to, whether or not they attended, using a statistical model that handles missing data and adjusts for each person's starting score.

## One Big Result: Sleep Quality Improved More After the Mindfulness Course

Both groups started with an average PSQI of 10.2. After six weeks, the mindfulness group's average had fallen to 7.4, a drop of 2.8 points, while the sleep hygiene group's had fallen to 9.1, a drop of 1.1 points that the paper's table marks only at p < .09, short of the usual .05 threshold. The difference between the groups' improvements was 1.8 points (95% :term[confidence interval]{id="confidence_interval"} 0.6 to 2.9; p = .002), an :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} of 0.89.

The authors judge this a meaningful effect:

> The effect size of 0.89 for improvement in sleep quality was large and of clinical relevance…

They put it beside meta-analyses that found average effect sizes of 0.76 for all kinds of behavioral treatments on self-reported sleep quality in older adults, and, in mixed-age adults with primary insomnia, 0.87 for sleeping pills (benzodiazepines and related drugs) and 0.96 for cognitive behavioral therapy. Those figures come from other studies of different groups, so the comparison is a rough guide rather than a head-to-head test. The effect size's own 95% confidence interval was also wide, from 0.28 to 1.50, as is usual in a trial of 49 people.

Even after the course, the mindfulness group's average score of 7.4 was still above 5, the cutoff the study used to define disturbed sleep.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Daytime Problems Eased Too, Partly Because the Comparison Group Got Worse on One

The mindfulness group also improved more than the sleep hygiene group on four of the six secondary questionnaires:

- **Insomnia symptoms** fell from 11.6 to 9.1 with mindfulness and from 11.7 to 10.7 with sleep hygiene (effect size 0.65, p = .03).
- **Depression** scores fell from 10.9 to 7.0 with mindfulness and stayed at about 11 with sleep hygiene (effect size 0.68, p = .02).
- **Fatigue interference** fell from 33.2 to 22.6 with mindfulness and stayed at 33.1 with sleep hygiene (effect size 1.02, p = .001).
- **Fatigue severity** fell from 17.3 to 13.6 with mindfulness but rose from 17.1 to 19.5 with sleep hygiene (effect size 1.50, p < .001). The table marks that rise as a significant change, so part of this largest between-group difference comes from the comparison group getting more tired.

Anxiety fell in both groups, by similar amounts (from about 13.5 to 10.7), and perceived stress showed no difference between the groups.

### Mindfulness Went Up Only in the Mindfulness Group

Scores on the mindfulness questionnaire rose in the mindfulness group (3.4 to 3.6) but not in the sleep hygiene group (3.3 to 3.4), a between-group effect size of 0.76 (p = .008), which the authors read as a sign the course changed mindfulness "as intended." In the mindfulness group, people whose nonreactivity, the ability to let inner experiences come and go without reacting to them, rose more tended to show larger improvements in sleep quality (r = −0.46, p = .04) and insomnia symptoms (r = −0.53, p = .02). No such link appeared in the sleep hygiene group. Nonreactivity is the only one of the questionnaire's five facets the paper reports on, and a correlation like this doesn't show that one change caused the other.

### NF-κB Fell in Both Groups Alike

NF-κB levels fell significantly in both groups, from 0.48 to 0.36 with mindfulness and from 0.48 to 0.38 with sleep hygiene. The fall was slightly greater with mindfulness, but the difference between the groups was not significant (p = .26). The authors note that the drop after meditation matches earlier studies in older adults, and offer a cautious reading of the drop after sleep hygiene:

> The SHE program also reduced NF-κB concentrations over time, suggesting that this approach to addressing moderate sleep disturbances might also confer some anti-inflammatory benefit, but future trials are needed to elucidate this potential.

The trial had no group that received neither course, so it can't show whether either course caused the drop.

### How Much People Took Part

People attended an average of 4.2 of the six mindfulness classes and 4.7 of the six sleep hygiene classes, a similar amount. The mindfulness group logged a median of 17.2 hours of meditation over the course (ranging from 3.7 to 65.3 hours). Forty-three people (88%) completed the final assessment; the six who didn't had started with similar sleep scores to those who stayed. The trial had been planned to need 42 people at the end to have enough :term[statistical power]{id="statistical_power"} to detect a medium-sized difference in sleep quality. No adverse events related to the study were reported.

### Limits the Authors Name

The authors point out that most participants were women and well educated, so the results need replicating in more diverse groups, that the findings are limited to older adults with normal thinking abilities and moderate sleep problems, and that whether mindfulness helps people diagnosed with insomnia, or prevents sleep problems in healthy sleepers, still needs testing. They left out objective sleep measures, such as :term{id="actigraphy" case="lower"}, "because of funding constraints." All the sleep, mood and fatigue outcomes were questionnaires, filled in by people who knew which course they had taken; the data collectors were unaware of group assignment at the baseline assessment.

## Final Reflection

This was a small trial with a demanding comparison. The comparison course was not a wait-list but six weeks of classes matched for time, attention and group support, and it even included relaxation and stress-reduction methods. Against it, the mindfulness course produced a larger improvement in self-reported sleep quality, along with fewer insomnia symptoms, less depression and less fatigue, right at the end of the course. The NF-κB results don't single out mindfulness: the inflammatory signal fell about equally after both courses.

The authors frame their conclusion as a short-term one:

> The findings from our study suggest that mindfulness meditation may be introduced to older adults as a short-term solution to remediate their moderate sleep disturbances, although research is needed to determine possible longer-term effects on sleep.

**In older adults with moderate sleep problems, a six-week mindfulness course improved self-reported sleep quality more than a matched sleep hygiene course, but the trial was small, relied on questionnaires and stopped at the end of the course, so how long the benefit lasts and whether it shows up in measured sleep are still open.**
