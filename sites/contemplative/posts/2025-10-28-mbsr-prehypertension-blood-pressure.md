## Background Information

High blood pressure rarely appears overnight. Before it reaches the level that calls for medication, many people spend years in the range the paper calls :term{id="prehypertension" case="lower"}: a systolic pressure (the peak, as the heart beats) of 120–139 mm Hg or a diastolic pressure (between beats) of 80–89 mm Hg. The authors note that nearly 60 million American adults are in that range. Guidelines at the time recommended lifestyle changes for them, such as exercise, weight loss and a healthier diet, and medication only if their pressure kept rising.

Stress-management programs had long been tried for high blood pressure, with mixed results. Among meditation techniques, the best studied was :term{id="transcendental_meditation"}: a meta-analysis the authors cite found it lowered systolic pressure by 5.0 mm Hg and diastolic by 2.8 mm Hg compared with control conditions. :term[Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)]{id="mbsr"} had been claimed to lower blood pressure too, but according to the authors only one controlled trial of a similar :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} practice had tested it, in 73 middle-school students with normal blood pressure.

Joel Hughes and colleagues at Kent State University and Summa Health System in Ohio set out to test MBSR in adults whose blood pressure was creeping up. Rather than compare it with no treatment, they chose a comparison that would look just as convincing to participants: :term{id="progressive_muscle_relaxation" case="lower"} (PMR), in which people tense and release groups of muscles in turn. An earlier pooled analysis of 12 trials had found that relaxation gave little or no benefit for high blood pressure, which made it a fair test for MBSR:

> For this trial, the PMR control intervention was chosen because, although it is not considered an effective treatment for high BP, it was a very credible placebo.

## What They Did

The team advertised for healthy adults aged 30 to 60 who weren't taking blood pressure medication, didn't smoke, and had no meditation experience. To qualify, a person's blood pressure had to fall in the prehypertensive range at three separate screenings, about a week apart, each with three readings taken after 10 minutes of quiet rest. The 56 people enrolled (24 men and 32 women, average age 50, 91% white) were on average overweight, with an average :term[body mass index]{id="bmi"} of 30. All of them received the standard lifestyle advice for prehypertension.

In this :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, 28 people were randomly assigned to MBSR and 28 to PMR, an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"}. Both programs ran for eight weekly group sessions of 2.5 hours, and both asked for at least 45 minutes of home practice a day, six days a week, with recordings to guide it.

- **MBSR** taught three skills: the :term{id="body_scan" case="lower"}, sitting meditation with attention on the breath, and gentle yoga, along with discussion of stress and coping. Participants were encouraged to bring the same attention to everyday activities like walking and eating. The program followed the standard design except for one omission: it had no all-day retreat, which usually comes in the sixth week.
- **PMR** worked down from 16 muscle groups to 7, then 4, and finally to "relaxation by recall," with explicit instruction in using relaxation in stressful situations.

The same therapist led both programs: a licensed clinical psychologist with a longtime personal meditation practice, formally trained in MBSR by Jon Kabat-Zinn and Saki Santorelli. An independent researcher rated recordings of 45 of the 112 sessions for how closely they followed the treatment manuals, giving MBSR an average of 4.4 and PMR 4.3 on a 5-point scale. To keep groups large enough, each group also included 13 people who had been ineligible for the trial but still wanted the course. They weren't randomized and their blood pressure wasn't measured.

Before and after the eight weeks, each participant's blood pressure was measured in two ways. The main outcome was clinic blood pressure: three readings from an automated cuff, 5 minutes apart, after 10 minutes of rest in a quiet room. The secondary outcome used :term[ambulatory blood pressure monitoring]{id="ambulatory_blood_pressure"}: participants wore a monitor for 24 hours that took readings two or three times an hour, giving averages for waking hours and for sleep. The staff who took the measurements didn't know who was in which group, and when asked to guess, they did no better than chance.

The analysis was :term[intention-to-treat]{id="intention_to_treat"}: everyone randomized was counted, and for people who dropped out, their starting values were used in place of their final ones. The authors repeated the analysis using only those who completed.

## One Big Result: MBSR Lowered Clinic Blood Pressure More Than Muscle Relaxation Did

In the clinic, systolic pressure fell by an average of 4.9 mm Hg in the MBSR group (4.8 in the paper's abstract), compared with 0.7 mm Hg in the PMR group (p = .016). Diastolic pressure fell by 1.9 mm Hg with MBSR and *rose* by 1.2 mm Hg with PMR (p = .008). Group membership explained about 10% of the variation in how much systolic pressure changed, and about 13% for diastolic.

Among those who completed the course, the gap was a little larger: systolic pressure fell 6.5 mm Hg with MBSR versus 1.1 with PMR, and diastolic fell 2.6 mm Hg with MBSR while rising 2.0 with PMR. The drop was also widespread rather than driven by a few people: 18 of 21 MBSR completers had systolic pressure at least 1 mm Hg lower than at the start, compared with 7 of 17 in the PMR group.

The 24-hour readings told a different story. Both groups' ambulatory pressures fell slightly, and MBSR did not lower them more than PMR, either while awake or asleep, for systolic or diastolic pressure.

Why would MBSR lower clinic readings but not everyday ones? The authors say this study can't tell. It might reflect :term{id="white_coat_hypertension" case="lower"}, the tendency for blood pressure to read higher in a clinic, or the influence of daily activity on 24-hour readings. They also offer a more intriguing possibility:

> One intriguing possibility is that patients were more able to apply the principles learned in the intervention during seated resting BP assessments in a controlled room than they were when going about their daily lives.

Participants hadn't been coached to meditate or relax during the measurements, though, and the authors note that people in either group could have used their skills while wearing the 24-hour monitor, so this may not fully explain the gap.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### A Benefit Comparable to Other Approaches, if It Lasts

The authors point out that the clinic drop was similar in size to the effect of Transcendental Meditation in the meta-analysis they cite, and to the difference between groups in PREMIER, a trial of comprehensive lifestyle changes for high blood pressure. Drops of this size matter for public health, they write, and if sustained might reduce heart attacks, strokes and deaths from heart disease. The "if" is important: blood pressure was measured only once after the course, with no follow-up.

### After Treatment, Only Diastolic Pressure Was Clearly Lower

Comparing the groups' readings after treatment, rather than how much they changed, diastolic pressure was lower in the MBSR group (75.4 vs. 79.4 mm Hg, p = .023), but systolic pressure was not significantly different (p = .208). The paper gives those systolic averages as 128.1 for MBSR and 125.3 for PMR, which doesn't fit its own starting averages and reported changes (130.2 − 4.9 = 125.3 for MBSR; 128.8 − 0.7 = 128.1 for PMR). Either way, the difference wasn't significant.

### Dropouts and an Uneven Start

Seven of the 28 MBSR patients and 11 of the 28 PMR patients didn't finish the post-course assessment; five people never started, because the group times clashed with their schedules. The authors call the MBSR group's 75% completion rate "at the low end" for such trials, and say scheduling groups at convenient times was a challenge. Attendance at sessions was about 90% in both groups, and home-practice logs were handed in 74% of the time in each.

Randomization also left the groups a little uneven on the secondary measure. Clinic pressures were similar at the start, but the MBSR group's 24-hour readings were lower: for example, 113.8 vs. 120.6 mm Hg for systolic pressure during sleep (p = .019).

### Why MBSR and Not Relaxation?

The two programs were matched for time, therapist and homework, but the authors note that they differ in aim. PMR is designed to bring on a :term{id="relaxation_response" case="lower"}, which may not last beyond the exercise, while MBSR encourages a way of living, such as mindful eating, that might help people follow lifestyle advice. They list other possible routes too: less stress, calmer activity in the nervous system's fight-or-flight branch, or changes in how the body regulates blood pressure. The study didn't measure any of these, so how MBSR lowered clinic readings remains open.

### Limits the Authors Acknowledge

The authors describe the study as a pilot-and-feasibility trial and list its limits: a modest sample, high dropout, home-practice logs of questionable accuracy, the non-randomized extra participants in the groups, and no long-term follow-up. The results were also not corrected for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}. They are clear about what the design can't show:

> No conclusions about the ability of meditation to prevent hypertension can be drawn from this design, as firm conclusion would require prevention trials with incident hypertension as the primary outcome.

## Final Reflection

This was one of the first randomized trials of MBSR for raised blood pressure, and its design was stronger than many in the field: a credible active control matched for time and teacher, screening at three visits so that only people with consistently raised pressure took part, and assessors who couldn't guess who had meditated. Against that comparison, eight weeks of MBSR lowered clinic blood pressure by about 5 mm Hg systolic and 2 mm Hg diastolic more than muscle relaxation did.

The result comes with real caveats. It was a small trial with many dropouts, especially in the relaxation group, and the effect appeared only in clinic readings, not in the 24-hour readings that reflect blood pressure in daily life. It is a pattern seen elsewhere on this site: in a [trial comparing MBSR with a matched health program](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/), mindfulness made a difference on one physical measure but not on related ones.

The authors present MBSR as a possible complement to lifestyle changes for people with prehypertension, not a replacement for them, and they warn that evidence that meditation lowers blood pressure "should not be taken as a recommendation to neglect needed health behavior changes." Larger trials with longer follow-up would be needed to show whether the clinic drop holds up over time and shows up in daily life.
