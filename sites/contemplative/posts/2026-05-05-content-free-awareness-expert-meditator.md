## Background Information

Most brain research on consciousness compares being conscious with *not* being conscious: waking versus deep sleep, or before and during general anesthesia. The trouble is that waking experience is always full of something, such as sights, sounds, thoughts, feelings, a sense of being "me." That makes it hard to separate the brain activity behind being aware at all from the activity behind whatever one happens to be aware of.

Contemplative traditions offer another route. Many describe a meditative state in which awareness remains but its usual contents fall away. The authors call it content-free awareness and define it this way:

> A state of 'content-free awareness' (CFA) represents a non-pathological, wakeful state of consciousness free of the complexity of normal wakeful states, i.e., the bare state of being aware or consciousness as such.

If such a state exists, and philosophers and neuroscientists still debate whether it does, the brain activity that goes with it might point toward the minimum the brain needs for conscious experience. For questions about the self, it matters for another reason: people who report the state describe the sense of self disappearing along with everything else.

The researchers are running a larger study of meditation states with reduced content in expert and novice meditators. This short report describes one session from it, recorded at the University Medical Center in Freiburg, Germany, in which, unexpectedly, an expert said he had reached the state while being scanned. The authors note that such experiences are rare and can't be produced on demand, which is why they published a single case.

## What They Did

The meditator, identified as TB, was 56 and a former physician. He teaches in the Karma Kagyü tradition of Tibetan Buddhism. He had meditated for 40 years, lived as an ordained monk in a retreat center for 21 of them (teaching there for 17), had meditated up to 12 hours a day for ten years, and had an estimated 50,000-plus hours of formal practice. He is also listed among the paper's authors, credited with helping the team understand the states of consciousness under study.

He lay in a 3-tesla MRI scanner with his eyes closed while the team recorded brain activity two ways at once: a 256-channel :term{id="eeg"} cap measured the brain's electrical rhythms, and a very fast form of :term{id="fmri"} captured a whole-brain image every tenth of a second. Heart and breathing sensors ran throughout. The session had five recording runs:

1. Five minutes of rest, relaxed and alert but not meditating.
2. Three 3-minute runs focusing on the scanner's sound, on a memory and on bodily sensations (these weren't analyzed in this report).
3. Thirty minutes of formal meditation aiming for awareness with as little content as possible.

After each part, he rated how much mental content, bodily self-awareness and sense of time and space he had experienced, on a 10-step scale. For the rest period he gave 8, 8 and 9. For the 30-minute meditation as a whole, he gave 2, 2 and 1.

In a detailed interview afterward, he said the meditation deepened gradually and reached its deepest point in the last few minutes. There, he described what tradition calls the "ground state of awakening":

> The former Buddhist monk described his experience as a 'clear, aware openness' without any thoughts, physical, or sensory perception and even without any sense of self, time, and space – a state traditionally called the 'ground state of awakening.'

He reported no awareness even of the loud scanner noise, yet was sure he had stayed awake, and the EEG showed no signs of sleep. He came out feeling deeply refreshed, which he recognized from past experience as a sign of a very deep "non-dual" state, without the usual split between a perceiving subject and its objects. In the terms of his tradition, he described it as basic :term{id="mahamudra" case="lower"}.

Based on that account, the team treated the last five minutes of the meditation as content-free awareness. For comparison, they took the five minutes of rest and five minutes from the middle of the earlier meditation (minutes 11 to 15), which they called content-related meditation. Each five-minute window was cut into one-minute segments.

From the EEG, they measured two rhythms often linked to meditation: :term{id="theta" case="lower"} (4–8 Hz) and :term{id="alpha" case="lower"} (8–12 Hz). From the fMRI, they measured :term{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"} between the hubs of two networks: the default mode network (:term{id="dmn"}), tied to self-related thought and :term{id="mind_wandering" case="lower"}, and the :term{id="dorsal_attention_network" case="lower"}, which steers attention toward chosen targets, especially outside the body. They also measured connectivity between the attention network and the primary auditory cortex, the first stop for sound in the brain, to see whether hearing had been "switched off."

## One Big Result: The Final Minutes Showed More Theta and Weaker Links in the Back of the Default Mode Network

Compared with the earlier meditation as well as with rest, the final five minutes stood out for a strong rise in theta and weaker connections within the DMN, along with a further decoupling of attention from hearing (below).

**Theta rose sharply.** At the three electrodes with the biggest changes from rest, two over the front of the head (F6 and F7) and one at the top-back midline (Pz), theta power was higher during content-free awareness than during rest or the earlier meditation (all significant after correcting for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}). The earlier meditation had raised theta only at Pz.

**The back of the DMN loosened.** Connectivity between the :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"}, a central DMN hub, and the :term{id="inferior_parietal_lobule" case="lower"} on each side was lower during content-free awareness than during rest or the earlier meditation. Against the earlier meditation, the DMN's links to the :term[medial prefrontal cortex]{id="mpfc"} and between the left and right parietal lobules also weakened. During the earlier meditation, by contrast, some of these back-of-the-brain DMN links had *strengthened* compared with rest.

Minute by minute across the rest period and the meditation, the more right-frontal theta rose, the more connectivity in the right side of the back of the DMN fell. The authors connect this to the self:

> Taken together, the disconnections inside the posterior part of the DMN, which we found to be negatively correlated with the increase in frontal theta power may be the main neural correlate of the cessation of internal mentation, as well as the dissolution of self-consciousness, and may also contribute to the experienced state of space- and timelessness.

They point out that the interplay between the PCC and the parietal lobules has been linked before to meditative experiences of :term[non-dual awareness]{id="nondual_awareness"} and of [selflessness](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/), and that psychedelic experiences in which self, space and time dissolve also involve reduced activity and connectivity in the back of the DMN. In another single-expert study on this site, :term{id="beta" case="lower"} rhythms faded mostly in right-side parietal areas as a meditator [dissolved the boundary between self and world](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/), though that study used a different technique and measure.

Other changes separated the final minutes from rest but were already partly under way during the earlier meditation:

- **Alpha fell dramatically**, at a frontal-midline electrode (FCz) and at two over the sides and back of the head (P7 and P8). Most of this drop had already happened by mid-meditation; only the frontal decrease continued significantly into the final minutes.
- **The attention network became more tightly connected** within itself compared with rest, including links between its parietal and frontal hubs.
- **The attention network decoupled from auditory cortex.** Its links with auditory cortex, mostly on the left, were weaker than at rest, and its links with auditory cortex on both sides were weaker than during the earlier meditation. That fits his report of not hearing the scanner, and these links also weakened as frontal theta rose.

The authors read the pattern as a deliberate, top-down attentional state that shuts out both incoming senses and inner thinking, a kind of "disconnected consciousness." They argue that falling alpha has been linked to the mind's disconnection from the senses in :term[REM sleep]{id="rem_sleep"} and ketamine anesthesia, and that, unlike in those states, content-free awareness lacks inner mental activity too.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Several Predictions Didn't Hold

The team had expected connectivity to *decrease* within the attention network, the usual opposition between the attention network and the DMN to weaken, and alpha to *rise*. Instead, attention-network connectivity increased, the relationship between the two networks didn't change, and alpha fell. Most meditation studies report more alpha, especially over the front of the head. The authors note that decreases have been reported mainly in experienced meditators from Hindu yoga and Tibetan Buddhist traditions, and caution that one person's specific state can't simply be compared with averages pooled across many studies.

The [jhana case study](/jhana-brain-networks/) of another expert also found weaker alpha during deep absorption, but in that meditator theta weakened too, whereas here it rose. The practices, the states and the analyses all differed, so the two cases don't settle what these rhythms mean.

### His Brain Was Unusual Even at Rest

In most people, alpha is strongest over the back of the head. TB's resting alpha was strongest over the front, with little at the back. The authors suggest this could be a lasting effect of decades of training, though with one person and no comparison group there's no way to test that here.

### Heart Rate Slowed, but Breathing Sped Up

His heart rate fell gradually through the meditation and reached its lowest point in the final minutes. His :term[heart rate variability]{id="heart_rate_variability"}, breathing rate and estimated oxygen use all sat below resting levels for much of the meditation, then began climbing after about 20 minutes and ended significantly *above* rest. A slower heart with more variable beats points to deep physical relaxation; faster breathing and more oxygen use look more like arousal. The authors note that Tibetan Buddhist and tantric yoga traditions emphasize an "awake" quality of mind, and that arousal responses have been found in such meditators before.

### A Rare State, Caught by Chance

This wasn't a planned test of content-free awareness. The meditator was asked to aim for minimal content, and only afterward said he had reached a content-free state. The authors call the data "serendipitous" and argue that exploratory single cases can generate new hypotheses, pointing to how much neuroscience learned from famous neurological patients such as H.M. and Phineas Gage.

## Final Reflection

This report offers a rare look at a state that meditation traditions have described for centuries but that science has hardly been able to examine: awareness reported as empty of thoughts, sensations, time, space and self. In one highly experienced practitioner, that state came with rising theta, falling alpha, an attention network pulling tighter while it decoupled from hearing, and a loosening in the back of the default mode network, the network most often tied to self-related thinking.

The limits are substantial, and the authors acknowledge several of them. There was one person, one session and one episode. The content-free period was defined by his account afterward, not by any independent marker, and he is also a co-author. Each condition contributed only five consecutive one-minute segments from the same recording, so the statistics show that those minutes differed from the others, not that the pattern would recur in another sitting or another person. The content-free minutes also came last, after more than 40 minutes in the scanner, so anything that drifts over time could play a part. And only two EEG frequency bands were examined.

The authors are clear that a single case can't prove anything, but it can suggest where to look. Their proposal, that a self-less, content-free awareness depends on stable top-down attention disconnecting the brain from both the senses and inner thought, is now a hypothesis for group studies to test.
