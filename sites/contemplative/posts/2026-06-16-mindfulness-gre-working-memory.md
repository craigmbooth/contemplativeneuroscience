## Background Information

Every year millions of students take standardized tests such as the SAT and the GRE, on the premise that they measure a stable ability that predicts success in school and at work. :term[Working memory]{id="working_memory"} capacity, the amount of information a person can hold in mind while using it, was long thought of in the same way: a fixed trait that predicts performance in many settings. More recently, researchers have asked whether training can raise it.

Training studies have a familiar problem, though. Practicing for the GRE, or practicing a working memory task, will improve scores on that test without necessarily improving the ability behind it. The authors, a psychology group at the University of California, Santa Barbara, wanted a kind of training that targets a process underlying performance on many tasks at once. They chose the ability to stay on task. :term[Mind wandering]{id="mind_wandering"}, which they define as "a shift of attention from a task to unrelated concerns," had been linked in their own earlier work to worse performance on working memory tests, fluid intelligence tests and the SAT. Few strategies were known to reduce it, but the group had found that brief mindfulness exercises reduced mind wandering during a task that required :term{id="sustained_attention" case="lower"}.

So they ran a :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"} to ask whether a two-week :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} course would do better than a nutrition course at improving reading comprehension and working memory, and at reducing distracting thoughts during both. They also predicted that any gains in the test scores would be explained by less mind wandering.

## What They Did

Forty-eight undergraduates (34 women and 14 men, average age about 21) were randomly assigned to a mindfulness class (26 people) or a nutrition class (22 people). Each class met for 45 minutes, four times a week for two weeks, so eight classes in all, and each was taught by a professional with extensive teaching experience in the field.

The mindfulness class taught :term[focused-attention meditation]{id="focused_attention"}. Students sat on cushions in a circle, and each class included 10 to 20 minutes of exercises in attending to one aspect of experience, such as the sensations of breathing, the taste of a piece of fruit or the sounds of a recording. Students then described their experience and got personal feedback from the instructor. The classes covered sitting upright with legs crossed and gaze lowered, telling naturally arising thoughts apart from elaborated thinking, treating worries about the past and future as thoughts happening in the present, using the breath as an anchor, counting up to 21 breaths out, and letting the mind rest rather than trying to suppress thoughts. Outside class, students were asked to meditate for 10 minutes a day and bring mindfulness into their daily activities. The authors summed up the practice this way:

> The practice of mindfulness encouraged in our intervention entailed promoting a persistent effort to maintain focus on a single aspect of experience, particularly sensations of breathing, despite the frequent interruptions of unrelated perceptions or personal concerns.

The authors compare the course to :term{id="mbsr"}: both teach a secular form of mindfulness in eight small-group sessions with practice at home. But this course was squeezed into two weeks instead of eight and asked for much less daily practice.

The nutrition class, an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"}, covered the basics of nutrition science and practical strategies for healthy eating. To match the daily meditation, its students kept a daily log of what they ate, though they didn't have to change their diet. Both classes used lectures and group discussion, had similar numbers of students, and met in comparable rooms in the late afternoon. Everyone was recruited for what was described as a comparison of two equally viable programs for improving cognitive performance, to keep motivation and expectations similar.

Within a week before and within a week after the classes, students took two tests, in counterbalanced order. They were tested in mixed groups from both classes, with nearly all instructions given by computer.

The first was a reading test drawn from the GRE. Students had 20 minutes for a verbal-reasoning section of the GRE with its vocabulary questions removed, and were scored on the proportion of questions they answered correctly. Two versions of equal difficulty were used before and after training. As the authors note:

> Given this modification, the GRE measure is best interpreted as an assessment of reading comprehension.

Scores on it before training were correlated with students' SAT reading-comprehension scores from their college applications (r = .446), which the authors take as support that it reflects reading ability outside the lab.

The second was a working memory test, the operation span task. Students saw sets of three to seven letters, one at a time, alternating with simple equations they had to check. At the end of each of 15 trials they picked out the letters in the order shown. Working memory capacity was the proportion of letters recalled.

Mind wandering was measured three ways, all relying on students' own reports. During the reading test, eight "thought probes" appeared at unpredictable moments and asked students to rate where their attention was, from 1 (completely on task) to 5 (completely on unrelated concerns). Students also kept a tally on a sheet of paper each time they caught their own mind wandering. After the working memory test, they filled in a questionnaire about how much their thoughts had strayed from the task.

## One Big Result: The Mindfulness Group's Test Scores Rose and Their Minds Wandered Less

Before training, the two groups did not differ significantly on any measure; the questionnaire on mind wandering came closest (p = .07). After training, the change from before to after differed between the groups on all five measures, in the mindfulness group's favor:

- **Reading test accuracy** improved more after mindfulness training (p = .02).
- **Working memory capacity** rose more (p = .05).
- **Mind wandering caught by the thought probes** fell more (p = .006).
- **Self-caught mind wandering** fell more (p = .05).
- **Mind wandering reported on the questionnaire** fell more (p = .03).

Follow-up tests found that the mindfulness group improved significantly on every measure, and the authors report that the nutrition group did not change. Using standard score-conversion procedures for the GRE, they estimate that the mindfulness group's gain in reading accuracy was equivalent to about 16 percentile points.

These are striking results for eight classes, but they need some context. The paper gives the groups' average scores only in a bar chart, not as numbers, and reports no :term{id="effect_size" case="lower" plural} besides the percentile estimate. That estimate translates a shortened verbal section with the vocabulary questions removed, not a full GRE. Two of the five group differences, for working memory and self-caught mind wandering, were reported as p = .05, right at the usual threshold for significance, and their test statistics put them just above it. Working memory was also the only measure whose average across both groups rose significantly at the second testing, which may partly reflect familiarity with the task, so the comparison with the nutrition group matters most there.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Did Less Mind Wandering Explain the Gains? Only for the Most Distractible, and Only Statistically

The authors' second question was why the scores rose. They reasoned that only students whose minds wandered at the start could measurably improve their focus, so they tested whether reduced mind wandering explained the gains specifically among students who were prone to it. This is a :term{id="mediation_analysis" case="lower"} with a twist, sometimes called moderated mediation. They combined the reading and working memory changes into one performance score, and the probe and questionnaire measures of mind wandering into one mind-wandering score (the self-caught tallies were left out of both this score and the baseline measure). Mindfulness training reduced mind wandering on this combined score (p = .002). The team then estimated how much of the training's effect on performance ran through less mind wandering for students at three starting levels of mind wandering: low, average and high (one standard deviation below the mean, at the mean and one above).

The pathway through mind wandering was statistically significant only for the high group (p = .035). For students with average or low mind wandering at the start, it was not (p = .17 and p = .84). In the authors' words:

> At least for people who struggle to maintain focus, our results suggest that the enhanced performance derived from mindfulness training results from a dampening of distracting thoughts.

This result is consistent with the authors' explanation, but it can't prove it. The classes were randomly assigned, but how much each student's mind wandering changed was not. Both changes were measured at the same testing sessions, so the link between less mind wandering and better scores is a :term{id="correlation" case="lower"} within the trial. Something else could drive both, such as trying harder at the second session, or the direction could partly run the other way: a student who finds a passage easier to follow may also feel more on task. The significant pathway also appeared at only one of the three levels tested, in a sample of 48.

### How Mind Wandering Was Measured Matters

The reading and working memory scores are objective, but every measure of mind wandering depended on students' own reports. The mindfulness students had just spent two weeks practicing noticing when their attention drifts, and had learned a way of thinking about thoughts, which could change how they answer questions about them. They also knew which class they had taken. The self-caught tally is especially hard to read: a student catches fewer lapses if the mind wanders less, but also if they notice less often. The authors read the drop as less mind wandering. The thought probes, which ask at moments the student doesn't choose, gave the clearest result (p = .006). [McVay and colleagues](/mind-wandering-contexts/) used similar probes and found that how often a person's mind wanders in the lab predicts how often it wanders in daily life.

### A Careful Control Group, With Limits

The nutrition class matched the mindfulness class on time, format, expert teaching, time of day and a small daily task, and students were told both programs might improve their thinking. Testing in mixed groups with computerized instructions reduced the chance that the experimenters' expectations shaped the results. That is a stronger design than comparing meditators with people who did nothing. Still, students knew which program they were in, and the paper doesn't report whether they expected the two classes to help equally. It also doesn't report attendance or how much home practice students actually did.

### Why Use Mindfulness as Cognitive Training?

The authors make an interesting argument for mindfulness as a test case. Training studies usually try to show that practice on one task carries over to an unpracticed task measuring the same ability. Meditating on the breath looks nothing like a reading test or a letter-memory task, so improvements can't come from practicing the test itself. They also suggest a possible brain mechanism: mindfulness training has been linked to less activity in the :term[default mode network]{id="dmn"}, the brain system active when the mind wanders (as in [Garrison et al., 2015](/reduced-dmn-active-task/)). This study didn't measure brain activity, and the authors say that future research should test the idea directly.

### Strong Conclusions From a Small Study

The paper's abstract concludes that "cultivating mindfulness is an effective and efficient technique for improving cognitive function, with wide-reaching consequences," and its discussion says the study "demonstrates" that two weeks of training can raise working memory capacity and reading comprehension. That is more confidence than one trial of 48 students at one university can carry. Everyone was tested within a week of the last class, so it's unknown whether the gains last or would show up on a real exam. The work was funded by a U.S. Department of Education grant, and the authors declared no conflicts of interest.

## Final Reflection

This study took a practical question, whether a short course of meditation could help students do better on the kind of test that shapes their futures, and answered it with a randomized design and a credible comparison class. After eight classes, the mindfulness group read and remembered better on the tests and reported fewer off-task thoughts, while the nutrition group didn't change.

It fits with other work on this site. In another early randomized trial, [five days of 20-minute meditation sessions](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/) improved undergraduates' ability to resolve conflicting cues on an attention test. A [meta-analysis of 111 randomized trials](/mindfulness-cognitive-function-survey/) later found small-to-moderate improvements in working memory and attention after mindfulness training. And [Killingsworth and Gilbert](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/) found that minds wander almost half the time in daily life, so there is plenty of room for training to help.

Taken alone, this is a small, short study with self-reported mind wandering, two results at the edge of significance and a mediation finding that applies only to the most distractible students. It is best read as a promising, carefully controlled first test of the idea that training attention can improve performance on demanding mental tasks, one that larger trials with longer follow-up needed to confirm.
