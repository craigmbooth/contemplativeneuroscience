## Background Information

When you think about yourself, you are usually telling a story. Am I really "confident"? Was I "unkind" yesterday? Will I be "decisive" enough at work next week? Philosophers call this the narrative self: the "me" that links memories, traits and plans into an identity that lasts over time. Brain-imaging studies had already tied it firmly to the :term[medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)]{id="mpfc"}, along the brain's midline, which is part of the :term[default mode network]{id="dmn"} that becomes active when the mind wanders.

But there is another way of being a self: simply experiencing this moment, the thoughts, feelings and sensations happening now, without spinning them into a story. The authors note that most research on the self had ignored this momentary self, and that little was known about whether it runs on the same midline machinery or on something different. One idea they wanted to test was that it depends instead on areas that map the state of the body, especially on the right side of the brain: the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, which registers signals from inside the body (:term{id="interoception" case="lower"}), and the :term[somatosensory cortex]{id="somatosensory_cortex" case="lower"}, which registers touch and body position.

Studying it is hard, because the mind's default is narrative. Ask an ordinary volunteer to just notice the present, and they may slide back into thinking about themselves. So the team turned to people trained to do exactly this. :term{id="mindfulness"} training, they write, teaches people to direct and keep attention on the present moment, so trained people might separate the two modes more cleanly and reveal the brain networks behind the momentary self.

The two modes came from earlier work on depression, which found that a narrative, analytical focus on oneself feeds rumination (:term[repetitive negative thinking]{id="repetitive_negative_thinking" case="lower"}), while an experiential focus avoids it. One of the authors, Zindel Segal, co-wrote the book that set out :term[mindfulness-based cognitive therapy]{id="mbct"} for preventing depressive relapse.

## What They Did

The participants were people signing up for the :term{id="mbsr"} program at St Joseph's Health Centre in Toronto, not healthy student volunteers. The course followed the standard format: eight weekly classes training attention on the breath and other sensations, :term{id="body_scan" case="lower" plural} and some basic yoga, with at least 45 minutes of home practice on each of the six days between classes.

The researchers randomly assigned enrollees to be scanned either before the course began, as a :term[wait-list group]{id="wait_list_control" case="lower"} (16 people, 12 of them women, average age 42), or after they had finished it (20 people, 15 women, average age about 46). Each person was scanned only once. The authors chose this design to avoid the effects of doing the same task, in the same scanner, twice. The groups did not differ in age, gender, previous meditation experience or in anxiety, depression and other psychological symptoms measured before training.

Just before scanning, everyone spent 20–25 minutes learning two ways of attending. Experiential focus meant sensing what was happening in one's thoughts, feelings and body, "without purpose or goal, other than noticing how things are from one moment to the next," and gently returning to present experience when distracted. Narrative focus was described this way:

> NF was characterised as judging what is occurring, trying to figure out what that trait word means to the participant, whether it describes the participant, and allowing oneself to become caught up in a given train of thought.

People practiced until they could describe the difference in their own words.

In the :term{id="fmri"} scanner, a cue told them which focus to use, and then six personality words appeared one at a time over 36 seconds, each for 4 seconds. Each set mixed mildly positive words (such as "confident" or "charming") with negative ones (such as "melancholy" or "greedy"), chosen because they invite people to think about themselves. Over two runs, each person did four blocks of each kind of focus, about two and a half minutes of each. Nobody pressed buttons or answered questions during scanning, to avoid pulling people out of momentary awareness. (The session also included other tasks, such as a memory task and breath monitoring, which weren't analyzed in this paper.)

The main analyses compared the two kinds of focus within each group, searching the whole brain for clusters of activity. To count, a cluster had to contain at least 6 neighboring voxels that each passed p < 0.005, with a peak at p < 0.001, which the authors calculated as equivalent to p < 0.001 once cluster size is taken into account.

## One Big Result: After the Course, Present-Moment Focus Pulled Activity Away From the Midline and Toward Body-Sensing Areas

First, a check that narrative focus did what was expected. Pooling both groups, it activated the midline self-related regions found in earlier studies, the mPFC and :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc" case="lower"}, along with left-sided language areas.

**Novices.** In the people scanned before the course, switching from narrative to experiential focus lowered activity in a few small midline areas: the subgenual cingulate, at the base of the frontal lobes (27 voxels) and the posterior cingulate (41 voxels). In the front of the mPFC itself, the drop was tiny (4 voxels) and appeared only when the cluster-size rule was loosened. Experiential focus raised activity in left-sided areas on the outer surface of the frontal and parietal lobes. The authors read this as effortful control, or "at the very least, an attempt to resist narrative :term{id="mind_wandering" case="lower"}," rather than the brain basis of a present-moment self.

**After the course.** In the people scanned after MBSR, the same switch produced much larger drops along the midline: in both the upper (87 voxels) and lower (86 voxels) parts of the mPFC, plus a small area of the left :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}. And experiential focus now engaged a set of right-sided areas: the lateral prefrontal cortex (77 voxels), the right insula (6 voxels), the secondary somatosensory cortex (24 voxels) and, extending back from it, the :term{id="angular_gyrus" case="lower"} of the :term{id="inferior_parietal_lobule" case="lower"} (7 voxels). The authors describe this as a "marked shift" from the midline toward right-sided prefrontal areas and regions that represent the body.

To check whether novices had the same pattern, only too weak to show up, the team took small spheres around the peak of each trained-group cluster and measured every participant's activity there (a :term{id="region_of_interest" case="lower"} analysis). The differences between the two kinds of focus were clear in the trained group (p < 0.003 or smaller in each region) and absent in novices (p > 0.3).

Two cautions apply to this comparison. The regions were picked because they showed the effect in the trained group, so that group was bound to show it again when measured in the same spots. And the paper reports the effect within each group, not a direct statistical test of whether the two groups differed. An effect that is significant in one group and not in another is not the same as a significant difference between the groups.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Insula Fell Out of Step With the Midline

The authors wondered why novices found it hard to separate the two modes. One possibility was habit: in most people, body signals may be automatically read through the midline self-evaluation system. To look at this, they took the right insula, the best-known body-awareness region in their results, and measured its :term{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"}, how closely its activity rose and fell with other areas' over the whole scan.

In novices, the insula's activity went closely with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (:term[vmPFC]{id="vmpfc"}), a midline area tied to judging what matters to oneself (:term{id="correlation" case="lower"} r = 0.61). In the trained group, the two were essentially uncorrelated (r = 0.06). Instead, the insula went closely with the :term[dorsolateral prefrontal cortex]{id="dlpfc"} on the brain's outer surface (r = 0.78, against 0.28 in novices).

The authors' interpretation (here "viscerosomatic" means signals from the body's organs and surface, and MT means mindfulness training):

> This pattern of results suggests MT may afford greater access to distinct modes of self-focus by promoting a shift away from viewing viscerosomatic activity through the lens of the mPFC towards a distinct mode of sensory awareness supported by the lateral PFC.

This is the paper's most striking picture, but it should be read with care. Both the insula and the two areas it was compared with were chosen because they differed between the groups, so the contrast is bound to look sharp. The paper reports the differences as overwhelmingly significant (z = 13.4 and 12.1), but it doesn't spell out how the correlations were calculated. And because connectivity was measured across the whole scan, it doesn't show what happened during either kind of focus specifically.

### Both Kinds of Focus Felt Easier After the Course

After the scan, everyone rated how easy each kind of focus had been. Narrative focus was easier than experiential focus for both groups, and the trained group found both easier than the novices did. Training didn't make one mode easier than the other, and the groups didn't differ in how much their minds wandered in the scanner. The authors take this to mean the brain differences aren't simply a matter of the trained group trying less hard at one task. Beyond these ratings and the questionnaires given before training, the paper reports no psychological results: nothing about whether anxiety, depression or well-being changed with the course, or how much people practiced at home.

### A Body-Based Self

The authors propose two modes of self-reference that are "habitually integrated" but can be pulled apart: a narrative self that extends across time, centered on the midline, and a more basic momentary self, built from body signals in the insula, somatosensory cortex and inferior parietal lobule. They suggest the angular gyrus, where stimulation has been reported to cause :term{id="out_of_body_experience" case="lower" plural}, may help give the detached, observing perspective of mindful awareness. They also suggest that being able to step out of the narrative mode matters for mood and anxiety disorders, since a narrative focus has been linked to greater vulnerability to these disorders.

This narrative/experiential split went on to shape later thinking. The [S-ART framework](/s-art-mindfulness-framework/) builds on it, placing the narrative self in midline default-mode regions and the experiential self in the insula and prefrontal control areas such as the dlPFC. A [MEG study of experienced Vipassana meditators](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/) found a related pattern with a different tool: moving from thinking about what characterizes them to simply experiencing the present lowered fast :term{id="gamma" case="lower"} activity over frontal regions including the mPFC. And in a [later fMRI study](/decentering-imagined-stress-fmri/), novices who viewed imagined stressful events as passing mental states, rather than immersing themselves, showed less engagement of self-related regions and more of control regions.

### What the Design Can't Show

- **No one's brain was tracked over time.** Random assignment to scanning before or after the course is a real strength, but each person was scanned once, and the paper doesn't say whether anyone assigned to the trained group dropped out of the course before being scanned. The authors are clear about the limit:

> One important limitation of the present study is the lack of a true pre-/post-intervention design; as such, the evidence does not support a claim that MT per se allowed for the increased differentiation between narrative and experiential attentional networks, but rather that those with MT show qualitatively different distinctions between self-referential focus modes than those only anticipating MT (the novice group).

- **The abstract says more.** It concludes that the two forms of self-awareness "can be dissociated through attentional training," a causal reading the discussion itself steps back from.
- **No comparison course.** MBSR includes yoga, group classes and an explanation of present-moment awareness. People who had just spent eight weeks practicing exactly this kind of attention may simply have understood the instruction better, which is part of the authors' point, but it means the study can't separate a change in the brain's capacity from familiarity with the task.
- **Small groups and small clusters.** Several of the clusters were tiny (the insula cluster was 6 voxels, the novices' mPFC cluster 4), and with groups this small, some of these could be chance findings.
- **No check inside the scanner.** With no responses collected, there was no way to confirm moment by moment which mode people were in. The ease and mind-wandering ratings came afterward.

## Final Reflection

This is one of the founding studies of the neuroscience of mindfulness and the self. Its central idea is easy to grasp and has proved durable: reflecting on what a word says about *me* and simply noticing what is happening *now* are different mental acts, and they seem to lean on different brain systems, one along the midline and one on the right side, closer to the body's own signals. In people who had just completed an MBSR course, those two systems looked more clearly separated, and the insula, rather than moving with the midline's self-evaluation system, moved with an area on the outer surface of the frontal lobes.

How much weight the findings can bear is another matter. The groups were small and scanned once each, the brain regions used to compare them were chosen from the trained group's own results, and the most dramatic statistics probably overstate the certainty. The authors were candid that their design couldn't show the course caused the difference, and they called for testing the same people before and after training. The study is best read as a clear, influential hypothesis about two ways of being a self, backed by suggestive first evidence, rather than as proof that eight weeks of mindfulness rewires how the brain represents the self.
