## Background Information

How often does meditation cause problems? The answer depends a great deal on how and whom you ask. In a [US survey led by Simon Goldberg](/meditation-adverse-effects-prevalence/), 32% of people who had tried meditation said it had led to a challenging or distressing experience at some point, and 50% ticked at least one item on a checklist of specific effects. When an interviewer asked [participants in an 8-week mindfulness course](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/) about 44 specific experiences, 58% reported at least one that felt unpleasant at the time. And about a quarter of [regular meditators](/regular-meditators-unpleasant-experiences/) said they had had a particularly unpleasant experience they thought meditation may have caused.

All of these studies counted :term[meditation-related adverse effects]{id="meditation_adverse_effects"} among people who meditated. None compared them with a group of similar people who did something else, so none could say how much of what they counted would have happened anyway. People feel anxious, low or upset during ordinary days too. A randomly assigned comparison group supplies that missing baseline.

This study, led by Otto Simonsson of the Karolinska Institutet with Goldberg among the co-authors, builds that comparison into a large online experiment. The authors note that earlier randomized trials that counted adverse experiences had not used nationally representative samples or compared different kinds of meditation. Many programs also bundle several practices together, which makes it hard to tell which one is responsible for a problem. So the team tested single, brief sessions of three common practices against an everyday alternative: using the internet. Short guided sessions like these are a widely used format in classrooms, clinics and meditation apps.

## What They Did

In November and December 2023, the researchers recruited adults in the US and the UK through Prolific, an online research platform, using its option to match each country's adult population by age, sex and ethnicity. The study took about 25 minutes and paid US $3.20. Of 5,260 people who took part, 155 were excluded for failing an attention check and 56 for incomplete answers, leaving 5,049 who were randomly assigned.

Before the session, everyone filled in a long set of questionnaires: demographics, personality, anxiety, depression, stress, loneliness, current mood, mindfulness, :term{id="self_compassion" case="lower"}, gratitude, past meditation practice and use of the internet and apps for mental health.

The experiment used a :term[factorial design]{id="factorial_design"}, crossing three factors at once:

- **Practice:** a guided :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation (paying attention to the present moment without judgment), a self-compassion meditation (generating kindness and friendliness toward oneself), a gratitude meditation (generating gratitude and appreciation), or the control condition, using the internet as one normally does.
- **Length:** 5 or 10 minutes.
- **Narrator's voice:** male or female (meditation groups only).

That made 14 conditions, with about 1,260 people in each of the four practice groups. The recordings came from the Healthy Minds Program app. The page would not let anyone move on until the full 5 or 10 minutes had passed, and afterward a check question asked people what they had been told to do.

The researchers measured adverse experiences in two ways. The first was a single yes/no question asked right after the session: "Did you have a distressing experience during the task you just completed?" Earlier studies often asked about "challenging, difficult, or distressing" experiences, and the authors deliberately narrowed it:

> …the use of multiple qualifiers (ie, "challenging," "difficult," and "distressing") complicates interpretation and reduces measurement precision—many activities may be challenging or difficult (eg, learning to play the violin or swimming) but would typically not be considered adverse experiences simply for that reason.

They acknowledge that this makes their numbers less directly comparable with studies that asked the broader question. The second measure was a clinically relevant rise in negative mood: an increase on the negative-feelings scale of the :term[PANAS]{id="panas"} from before to after the session, above a threshold (0.24 standard deviations) the authors took from depression research.

These were secondary outcomes of a larger trial, and the comparison between groups was preregistered as exploratory. Because there were two outcomes, the team lowered the bar for statistical significance to p < .025 to allow for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}. They report that, with this many people, the study had at least 80% :term[power]{id="statistical_power" case="lower"} to detect an :term{id="odds_ratio" case="lower"} of 1.55 or more for distressing experiences and 1.40 or more for rises in negative mood.

## One Big Result: Meditating Was No More Distressing Than Using the Internet

Across everyone, 4.1% (204 of 4,925) said they had a distressing experience during their task, and 7.1% (348 of 4,908) had a clinically relevant rise in negative mood. The key comparison is between the groups:

- **Distressing experience:** 4.0% in the internet group, against 4.8% after gratitude, 3.7% after self-compassion and 4.0% after mindfulness. Taken together, people who meditated were no more likely to report distress than people who used the internet (odds ratio 1.05, 95% :term[confidence interval]{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} 0.76–1.47, p = .76).
- **Rise in negative mood:** 9.6% in the internet group, against 5.9–6.8% in the three meditation groups. People who meditated were less likely to have a marked rise in negative mood (odds ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.50–0.80, p < .001).

The three practices did not differ from one another on either measure.

This is what the randomized design adds. On its own, "4% of people found a brief meditation distressing" would read as a measure of meditation's risk. With a comparison group, it turns out that about 4% of people answer yes to that question after 5 or 10 minutes online, too. The authors conclude:

> Taken together, the findings suggest that the brief, digitally delivered meditation practices tested in this study carry risks of adverse experiences that are comparable to or lower than those of typical activities on the internet…

The comparison is against ordinary internet use, not against sitting quietly or a neutral task, and what people actually did online wasn't recorded. The authors point out what that might mean:

> It is therefore plausible that at least some participants spent their allocated 5- or 10-minute window engaging with emotionally activating material (eg, news coverage and social media), any of which may itself have elicited distressing experiences or increases in negative affect.

So the finding is that a short meditation carried about the same risk of distress as a realistic alternative way to spend those minutes. It doesn't show that meditation carries no risk at all. The power figures above also matter: the study was large enough to detect a sizable increase in distress after meditation, but a small one could have been missed.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Ten Minutes Brought a Little More Distress Than Five, in Every Group

People assigned to 10 minutes reported a distressing experience more often than people assigned to 5 minutes (4.8% vs 3.5%; odds ratio 1.42, 95% CI 1.07–1.89, p = .02). The authors read this as a sign that "a higher single dose may increase the incidence of distressing experiences." Note, though, that this comparison pooled all four groups, internet users included, and the test of whether the length effect differed between meditation and internet use was not significant. As the paper reports it, longer sessions went with slightly more distress whatever people were doing, and the data don't show that the effect was specific to meditation. The authors also caution that a sample with little meditation experience, and a step from 5 to 10 minutes, say little about experienced meditators or much longer sessions.

Session length made no difference to rises in negative mood overall. Answers were also missing about twice as often in the 10-minute groups (for example, 3.5% vs 1.4% for the distress question), in meditation and internet groups alike, which the authors attribute to the longer task rather than to people dropping out because they were upset.

### The Narrator's Voice Didn't Matter

Whether the meditation was read by a man or a woman made no significant difference to either outcome (odds ratios 0.94 and 0.86). The authors had tested this because studies of meditation teachers and of psychotherapists suggest that who is guiding a person can shape how they fare.

### Who Reported Distress Could Be Partly Predicted

The team trained two kinds of machine-learning model on everyone's baseline answers to see whether they could predict who would have an adverse experience, checking each model on data it had not been trained on. The better one (elastic net, a form of regression that keeps only the most useful predictors) reached an area under the :term[ROC curve]{id="roc_curve"} (:term[AUC]{id="area_under_the_curve"}) of 0.73 for distressing experiences, which the authors call acceptable, and 0.67 for rises in negative mood, which falls in the range they call poor.

The strongest predictors of a distressing experience were feeling negative just before the session, more experiences of discrimination, identifying as transgender, higher anxiety and higher depression. Identifying as Black was also among the top predictors. For rises in negative mood, higher gratitude and a less judgmental attitude toward one's own experience were protective, as was being assigned to the mindfulness or self-compassion meditation. The finding that current anxiety and depression went with more distress echoes the [2022 survey](/meditation-adverse-effects-prevalence/), where people with more depression and anxiety reported more adverse effects.

The authors suggest that these results should inform how meditation is offered to people dealing with stress related to their identity. Two points temper this. The models were built on the whole sample, including the internet group, and group assignment carried little weight in predicting distress, so they describe who tended to report distress during a short online task rather than during meditation in particular. And the models were not tested in a separate sample, which the authors list as a limitation.

### Why 4% Here and 25–58% Elsewhere?

The rates in this study are far lower than those in the surveys and course studies elsewhere on this site, but they answer a different question. Those studies asked about months or years of practice, or about an 8-week course with daily home practice. This one asked about a single 5- or 10-minute session, right after it ended, with one yes/no question about distress only. The [Britton course study](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/) found that a single broad question picked up only about a third of the people who reported effects when asked about specific experiences. The authors acknowledge that their estimates could be lower bounds of the true rate.

### What the Study Can't Say

The authors list several limits. Both outcomes were self-reported, and the distress measure was a single item rather than a validated checklist. Effects were measured only during and right after the session, so nothing is known about how long they lasted or whether any appeared later. The sample was matched to the US and UK populations only on age, sex and ethnicity, and it was recruited from an online research platform. One attention check was the only screen for careless answers. The paper's conflict-of-interest statement says that Simonsson is a cofounder of embla.ai AB and received a one-time payment from Mindfully Sweden AB for educational content.

## Final Reflection

Most research on meditation's downsides counts how often meditators report problems. This trial asks a different question: how much more often than people who spent the same few minutes doing something else? For a single short guided meditation, the answer was not more often for distress, and less often for a marked dip in mood. That is reassuring for the many people who try a brief practice in an app, a classroom or a clinic.

It doesn't cancel out the higher rates found elsewhere in this theme. A one-off 5-minute recording is not an 8-week course, a silent retreat or years of daily practice, and a single yes/no question asked immediately cannot capture effects that build up or appear later. The study's lasting contribution may be its design. Randomized comparisons like this one, applied to longer and more intensive practice, are what it would take to say how much of meditation's reported difficulty is caused by meditation itself.
