theme_description: |
  Brain regions work in networks that rise and fall together. The best known in meditation research is the default mode network, a set of regions most active during mind wandering and thinking about oneself. This theme collects brain-scanning studies of how meditation and related practices change the activity of these networks and the connections within and between them, from single sessions in beginners to expert meditators in the deepest states.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation often goes with less default mode activity and with looser, more flexible links between brain networks, but the studies are small, several follow a single expert, and only a few are randomized trials.** When a word briefly interrupted breath meditation in the scanner, the default mode response to its meaning faded sooner in 12 Zen meditators than in 12 non-meditators, though both groups judged the words equally well ([Pagnoni et al., 2008](/zen-meditation-default-mode-words/)). Even without meditation training, silently repeating a single word, the core of mantra meditation, lowered activity across much of the cortex, mainly in the default mode network, with no area becoming significantly more active, in 23 non-meditators repeating the word in 21-second blocks ([Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2015](/silent-word-repetition-brain-deactivation/)). After an 8-week MBSR course, people focusing on their present experience rather than on what personality words said about them showed less midline prefrontal activity and more in the insula and lateral prefrontal cortex than people scanned before the course, and the insula no longer moved in step with the midline, though each group was scanned once and the groups weren't directly compared ([Farb et al., 2007](/narrative-experiential-self-focus/)). The default mode network's part in mind wandering is followed in [Focus and mind wandering](/themes/mind-wandering/), and its part in self-related thinking in [The self](/themes/self/).

  In expert meditators, networks that usually stay separate become less so. The brain's inward-facing and outward-facing systems, which usually work against each other, were less opposed during nondual awareness than during focused attention ([Josipovic et al., 2012](/nondual-awareness-brain-networks/)). In a Tibetan Buddhist teacher with more than 50,000 hours of practice, minutes of reported awareness with no thoughts, sensations or sense of self showed weaker connections at the back of the default mode network, alongside more theta and much less alpha activity than rest ([Winter et al., 2020](/content-free-awareness-expert-meditator/)). A case study that scanned another expert dozens of times as he moved through all eight jhanas linked these states to less compartmentalized, more interconnected brain networks ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)), and followed moment to moment, the same meditator's brain switched between recurring states more often than in control tasks, rather than settling into stillness ([Treves et al., 2025](/jhana-dynamic-brain-states/)). These states are gathered in [Deep states](/themes/deep-states/).

  Two randomized studies tied changes in network connections to outcomes. A three-day mindfulness retreat strengthened the resting link between the default mode network and a self-regulation region, a change tied to lower inflammation four months later ([Creswell et al., 2016](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/); see [Inflammation and immunity](/themes/immunity/)). And among people taught mindfulness for pain, those whose pain fell most showed the thalamus falling most out of step with the precuneus, a default mode region ([Riegner et al., 2023](/mindfulness-pain-thalamus-default-mode/)); the other brain studies of pain are in [Brain responses](/themes/brain-responses/).

  Most of these studies are small, several follow a single meditator, and many compare groups at one point in time, so they show how meditation looks in the brain's networks more than they prove that practice changes them.
