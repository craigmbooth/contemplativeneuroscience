question: "Does meditation change how the brain reacts?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "fMRI studies of how meditation changes the brain's reactions to pain, emotional pictures and sounds, smoking cues and stressful thoughts."
theme_description: |
  Much of what meditation promises is about reacting differently: to pain, to upsetting images, to cravings, to stressful thoughts. This theme collects brain-scanning studies that measure how regions such as the amygdala, insula, cingulate and prefrontal cortex respond to these challenges, during meditation and after training, and whether those changes go with changes in how people feel and act.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation training can change how the brain reacts to pain, emotional pictures and smoking cues, with the clearest evidence from small randomized trials, but the brain changes haven't always matched changes in how people felt or behaved.** Brief training can change how the brain handles pain: after four 20-minute mindfulness lessons, meditating during painful heat reduced activity in the part of the brain where the pain signal arrives, and greater relief went with more activity in the anterior insula, anterior cingulate and orbitofrontal cortex, in a small study with no control group ([Zeidan et al., 2011](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/)). A randomized study comparing mindfulness with sham meditation and a placebo cream found that meditating lowered activity in the thalamus and periaqueductal gray, a midbrain pain-control area, more than either did, and that relief went with orbitofrontal and subgenual cingulate activity, while placebo relief went with quieter sensory cortex and sham relief only with slower breathing ([Zeidan et al., 2015](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/)). A later study of how brain connections changed with that relief is in [Brain networks](/themes/brain-networks/), and the clinical trials are in [Pain](/themes/pain/).

  Training can change the amygdala, which flags emotionally important events, even when people aren't meditating. In a small randomized trial, eight weeks of mindful-attention training lowered the right amygdala's response to emotional photos more than a health discussion class did, while compassion training showed no clear change ([Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/)). In a larger trial, MBSR participants' right amygdala responded less to positive pictures than that of people in a matched health program, though not significantly less to negative ones, and long-term meditators showed the same smaller response to positive pictures; after MBSR, the amygdala also worked more closely with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, a region that helps keep it in check ([Kral et al., 2018](/mindfulness-amygdala-reactivity/)). In 14 adults with social anxiety disorder scanned before and after an MBSR course, turning to the breath while reading harsh beliefs about themselves engaged parietal and visual attention areas more after the course, though the study had no comparison group ([Goldin and Gross, 2010](/mbsr-social-anxiety-negative-self-beliefs/)). In a single session, 30 non-meditators who viewed imagined stressful events as passing mental states engaged control and perspective-shifting areas more, and areas tied to bodily, self-related feeling less, than when they immersed themselves, though stress ratings differed only slightly ([Lebois et al., 2015](/decentering-imagined-stress-fmri/)). Studies of how practice changes emotional reactions are gathered in [Emotion](/themes/emotion/).

  Compassion practice changes responses to other people. While generating compassion, 15 long-term Tibetan Buddhist practitioners' insula responded more than that of 15 novices to emotional sounds, most of all to a woman in distress, and regions tied to understanding others' minds, such as the right temporoparietal junction, responded more too, though the groups were compared only once ([Lutz et al., 2008](/compassion-meditation-insula-emotional-sounds/)). Two weeks of compassion training was followed by more giving to a stranger treated unfairly than reappraisal training (giving was measured only after training), and changes in the right inferior parietal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex tracked that giving ([Weng et al., 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)). More is in [Compassion and kindness](/themes/compassion/).

  Craving-related activity can shift too. When smokers new to meditation mindfully attended to smoking pictures rather than just looking at them, a craving-linked area at the base of the frontal lobes (the subgenual anterior cingulate) quieted and fell out of step with the insula and striatum, though their craving eased only slightly ([Westbrook et al., 2013](/mindful-attention-smoking-cues-brain/)). In 26 smokers, two weeks of integrative body–mind training raised resting activity in the anterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex, though these changes didn't track how much less people smoked ([Tang et al., 2013](/brief-meditation-smoking-reduction/)). In a randomized trial of a month-long mindfulness app for smokers, the posterior cingulate's response to smoking pictures fell no more than with a standard quit app, but among the 33 mindfulness users, those whose response fell more cut down more ([Janes et al., 2019](/mindfulness-app-smoking-cue-reactivity/)). The smoking trials themselves are in [Craving and addiction](/themes/craving/). Meditation itself can engage the brain's reward system: in a case study of jhana meditation, an experienced meditator's brain showed reduced activity in areas tied to outward awareness and increased activity in reward areas ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)), a pattern the authors note differs from studies of Tibetan Buddhist compassion meditation and Transcendental Meditation.

  The randomized trials here are small, several studies had no comparison group, and brain changes didn't always go with matching changes in pain, stress, craving or behavior, so they show where meditation's effects may act in the brain more than they establish them.
