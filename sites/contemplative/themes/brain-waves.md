question: "What happens to brain waves when you meditate?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "EEG, MEG and intracranial studies of brain rhythms in meditation: gamma in long-term meditators, the rhythms of deep states, and traces in sleep."
theme_description: |
  The brain's electrical activity rises and falls in rhythms, from slow delta waves to fast gamma, and different rhythms go with different states of mind. This theme collects studies that record those rhythms with EEG, MEG and electrodes inside the brain, in long-term meditators and beginners, during meditation, at rest and during sleep, and asks whether particular practices and states leave a recognizable signature.
research_summary: |
  **Experienced meditators often show distinctive brain rhythms, most consistently more fast gamma activity, and particular meditative states have signatures of their own, but most of the evidence comes from small, one-time comparisons and single cases.** Long-term practitioners of loving-kindness and compassion meditation generated unusually high-amplitude gamma waves, which rose with years of practice and stayed elevated at rest ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)). Extra gamma isn't confined to one tradition: experienced practitioners of Vipassana, Himalayan Yoga mantra meditation and Isha Shoonya (16 in each group) all had more 60–110 Hz gamma over the back of the head than 16 non-meditators, rising modestly with hours of practice, a difference the authors read as a lasting trait rather than something switched on only during meditation, though the groups were compared only once ([Braboszcz et al., 2017](/gamma-three-meditation-traditions/)). Even a single session may register deep in the brain: in eight people with epilepsy and implanted electrodes, all new to meditation, gamma activity in the amygdala and hippocampus was higher during a loving-kindness session than during the instructions before it ([Maher et al., 2025](/neurological-substrates-of-meditation/)). Loving-kindness and compassion practice have their own theme, [Compassion and kindness](/themes/compassion/).

  The rhythms carry into sleep. A later study from Lutz's group found more gamma activity in meditators during non-REM sleep, linked to how much they practiced each day ([Ferrarelli et al., 2013](/meditators-sleep-gamma/)). In the same meditators, a single intensive day of practice raised slower, low-frequency activity in that night's sleep, a short-term trace alongside the long-term gamma difference ([Dentico et al., 2016](/meditation-day-sleep-brain-waves/)). Both studies are also part of [Sleep](/themes/sleep/).

  Deep states have their own rhythms, and not every one raises them. Letting go of the sense of self went with weaker beta rhythms in prefrontal and parietal regions in experienced meditators ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/); [2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)). The same meditators showed a different rhythm when they tried to step outside time and space: more theta in regions the authors tie to the sense of the body ([Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2013](/timelessness-meditation-meg/)). When 30 experienced meditators aimed for "thoughtless emptiness," overall EEG power fell below rest, including fewer slow waves, the opposite of drowsiness ([Hinterberger et al., 2014](/emptiness-meditation-eeg/)), and in one advanced meditator, alpha waves faded in the 40 seconds before each reported cessation, a brief gap in consciousness ([Chowdhury et al., 2023](/meditation-cessation-eeg/)). These states are gathered, with what meditators say about them, in [Deep states](/themes/deep-states/).

  Some studies track the brain's response to a single event rather than its ongoing rhythm. Over a three-month retreat, practitioners' brain response to the first of two quickly following targets (the P3b) shrank, and those whose response shrank most generally got best at catching the second ([Slagter et al., 2007](/meditation-retreat-attentional-blink/)). In 11 long-term loving-kindness meditators, the brain's larger response to their own face than to a close friend's looked smaller than in non-meditators, though the direct comparison was only a trend ([Trautwein et al., 2016](/loving-kindness-self-bias/)). And a later MEG study from Dor-Ziderman's lab found that in 38 experienced meditators, death-related words didn't silence the brain's automatic response to their own face, as they had in non-meditators tested earlier, though the comparison group came from that earlier study ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2025](/meditation-death-acceptance-brain/)); the studies of death are in [Death](/themes/death/).

  Most of these studies compare experienced meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time, or follow one or a few people, so they show what meditation looks like in the brain's rhythms more than they prove that practice caused the differences.
