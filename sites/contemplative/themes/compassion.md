question: "Can meditation make you kinder?"

meta_description: "Research on compassion and loving-kindness meditation: generosity, helping, positive emotions, and how these practices show up in the brain and body."
theme_description: |
  Loving-kindness (*mettā*) and compassion (*karuṇā*) are among the oldest meditation practices in the Buddhist tradition. Practitioners deliberately call up warmth and goodwill, first toward themselves or someone they love, then toward strangers, difficult people and eventually all beings. Today these practices are taught in secular courses and apps, often with the promise that they will make people happier and kinder.

  This theme gathers studies that test that promise: whether compassion training changes how generously people act toward strangers, how practicing kindness affects everyday emotions and well-being, and what these practices look like in the brain and body of beginners and long-term practitioners.
research_summary: |
  **Compassion and loving-kindness practice seem to make people somewhat kinder and warmer, at least in the short term, though most of the studies are small.** In a randomized trial, adults who practiced compassion for 30 minutes a day for two weeks then gave more of their own money to help a stranger who had been treated unfairly ($1.14 of $5, against $0.62) than adults who had practiced reappraisal, a technique for easing their own distress, though giving wasn't measured before training, and changes in their brain responses to suffering tracked their giving differently ([Weng et al., 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)). Kindness may not even need to be taught directly: in a small randomized trial, 37% of students who used a mindfulness app for three weeks gave their seat to a woman on crutches who seemed to be in pain, against 14% of students who used a brain-training app, although the meditation never mentioned compassion and both groups were equally good at reading emotions ([Lim et al., 2015](/mindfulness-app-compassion/)). Even preschoolers may benefit: in a small trial randomized by classroom, four-year-olds given a 12-week mindfulness-based Kindness Curriculum gained slightly more in teacher-rated social skills, and got better grades in three of five areas, than children on a wait-list, who kept more stickers for themselves in a sharing game by the end; the teachers who gave the ratings and grades knew which children had taken part ([Flook et al., 2015](/kindness-curriculum-preschool/)); its results for self-control are in [Focus and mind wandering](/themes/mind-wandering/). Loving-kindness practice may also build warmth slowly: in a seven-week workshop for working adults, daily positive emotions rose gradually compared with a wait-list, and people whose positive emotions rose most reported gains in purpose, self-acceptance and positive relationships, though those links are correlations and the group difference didn't hold when everyone randomized was counted ([Fredrickson et al., 2008](/loving-kindness-positive-emotions-resources/)). Other studies of how practice changes feelings are gathered in [Emotion](/themes/emotion/).

  The practices also leave measurable traces in the brain. Long-term Buddhist practitioners produced unusually strong, synchronized gamma waves during compassion meditation ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)). While generating compassion, long-term Tibetan Buddhist practitioners' insula, a region tied to feeling emotions in the body, also responded more than novices' to emotional sounds, most of all to a woman in distress, though this one-time comparison can't show that practice caused it ([Lutz et al., 2008](/compassion-meditation-insula-emotional-sounds/)). In eight epilepsy patients with implanted electrodes, all new to meditation, gamma activity in the amygdala and hippocampus was higher during a single ten-minute loving-kindness session than during the instructions that preceded it ([Maher et al., 2025](/neurological-substrates-of-meditation/)). Training doesn't always shift the brain's response to suffering in a clear way, though: in a small randomized trial, eight weeks of compassion training didn't clearly change the amygdala's response to emotional photos, unlike mindful-attention training, although compassion trainees whose response to images of people in difficult situations rose more had larger drops in depression scores, a link resting on 12 people ([Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/)). Over nine months of training, a three-month module of loving-kindness and partner exercises was linked to thickening in different parts of the cortex from modules on attention or perspective-taking, changes that tracked individual gains in the trained skills ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)). In the first study of brain structure in loving-kindness meditators, 10 men with at least five years of practice had more gray matter than 15 beginners in the right angular gyrus, a region tied to understanding others, and the right posterior parahippocampal gyrus, though the comparison was small and one-time ([Leung et al., 2013](/loving-kindness-gray-matter/)); both studies are weighed with other structural research in [Brain structure](/themes/brain-structure/). Long-term loving-kindness meditators also showed hints of a smaller brain bias toward their own face over a friend's, though the direct comparison with non-meditators was only a trend ([Trautwein et al., 2016](/loving-kindness-self-bias/); see [The self](/themes/self/)).

  Effects on the body are mixed. Six weeks of compassion meditation didn't change students' hormonal, inflammatory or emotional responses to a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group, although those who practiced more had smaller rises in the inflammation marker IL-6 ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)). But in the ReSource Project, the nine-month training study behind Valk's results, people tested after training in compassion or perspective-taking had cortisol rises 32–51% smaller than untrained people during a stressful mock job interview, while three months of attention training lowered reported stress but not cortisol; each person was tested only once, and the differences were small by conventional standards ([Engert et al., 2017](/compassion-perspective-training-cortisol-stress/)). Both studies are set beside other stress and inflammation research in [Stress and the body](/themes/biological-health/) and [Inflammation and immunity](/themes/immunity/).

  Most of this evidence comes from small samples, lab measures of kindness and short follow-ups, and several studies compare experienced practitioners with non-meditators at a single point in time, so they show what these practices can do more than how large or lasting the effects are in everyday life.
