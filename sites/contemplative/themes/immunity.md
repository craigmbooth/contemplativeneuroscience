question: "Can meditation calm inflammation and help the immune system?"

meta_description: "Research on meditation and the immune system: flu vaccine response, inflammation markers, and inflammation-related gene activity in immune cells."
theme_description: |
  Inflammation is the immune system's response to injury and infection, and psychological stress can push it up. This theme gathers studies asking whether meditation changes the immune system: antibody responses to a flu vaccine, inflammation markers in blood and skin, and the activity of inflammation-related genes in immune cells, along with research linking inflammation to attention and loneliness. Research on [stress responses and the body](/themes/biological-health/) and on [aging](/themes/aging/) has its own themes.
research_summary: |
  **Mindfulness training shows tentative signs of calming inflammation and helping immune function, but most studies are small, many results haven't been replicated, and in one trial a vacation shifted gene activity as much as a meditation retreat did.** In a randomized trial, [Davidson et al. (2003)](/brain-and-immune-function/) found that employees who took an 8-week MBSR course produced more antibodies after a flu vaccine than a wait-list group. A systematic review of 20 randomized trials with 1,602 participants found tentative, repeated signs, counting results that fell short of significance, that mindfulness training lowers NF-κB activity and the inflammation marker CRP and raises telomerase activity and, in people with HIV, CD4+ T-cell counts, but most other immune markers showed no consistent change, and only 8 of the trials used a matched comparison program ([Black and Slavich, 2016](/mindfulness-immune-system-review/)).

  Some studies trace a path from the brain or the mind to inflammation. A randomized trial found that a three-day mindfulness retreat, unlike a matched relaxation retreat, strengthened a resting connection between the default mode network and a region involved in self-regulation, and that change statistically accounted for part of a drop in the inflammation marker IL-6 four months later ([Creswell et al., 2016](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/)); more on what meditation does to brain networks is in [Brain networks](/themes/brain-networks/). In an earlier small trial with 40 older adults, an 8-week MBSR course eased loneliness and damped inflammation-related gene activity in immune cells compared with a wait-list, though the blood markers CRP and IL-6 didn't change significantly ([Creswell et al., 2012](/mbsr-loneliness-older-adults/)); its loneliness result is discussed in [Mental health](/themes/mental-health/). A review of mindfulness studies in older adults noted that in one sleep trial, inflammatory NF-κB gene activity fell as much after sleep hygiene classes as after mindfulness training ([Fountain-Zaragoza and Prakash, 2017](/mindfulness-healthy-aging-review/)). And on a three-month retreat, participants with higher inflammation had weaker attention control ([Shields et al., 2020](/deconstructing-concentration-interference-control/)); the retreat's attention results are in [Focus and mind wandering](/themes/mind-wandering/).

  Gene activity in blood cells responds quickly, but not only to meditation. After a single eight-hour day of practice, 19 experienced meditators had lower activity in three HDAC genes and two inflammation-related genes in their white blood cells than 21 non-meditators who spent the day at leisure, though the groups weren't randomized and IL-6 and TNF-α didn't differ ([Kaliman et al., 2014](/meditation-day-gene-expression-inflammation/)). Inflammation-related gene pathways were least active in long-term practitioners of relaxation-response techniques, such as meditation, yoga and repetitive prayer, and dipped further during a single 20-minute session, though the study wasn't randomized and measured gene activity only ([Bhasin et al., 2013](/relaxation-response-gene-expression/)). But when healthy women were randomly assigned to six days at a resort, a vacation shifted blood gene activity, turning down stress- and inflammation-related genes, as much as a meditation retreat did; learning to meditate showed up instead in fewer depressive symptoms ten months later ([Epel et al., 2016](/meditation-retreat-vacation-gene-expression/)).

  Against matched comparison programs, inflammation under stress hasn't clearly changed. After an 8-week MBSR course, a nerve-driven skin flare provoked during a stress test grew less than after a health program matched to MBSR, but inflammatory proteins in the skin didn't differ, and the flare difference was gone four months later ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)). Six weeks of compassion meditation didn't change the IL-6 response to a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group, although students who practiced more had smaller IL-6 responses, a link the design can't show was caused by practice ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)). In adults with generalized anxiety disorder, IL-6 and TNF-α responses to a stress test fell after MBSR and rose after a stress class, but the MBSR group started with much larger responses and its own drops weren't significant ([Hoge et al., 2018](/mbsr-anxiety-stress-hormones-inflammation/)).

  The main caveats are size and design. Most of these studies are small; some compare practitioners with non-practitioners rather than assigning people at random; many trials compared meditation with a wait-list rather than a matched program; and changes in gene activity or blood markers don't show whether anyone's health improved.
