theme_description: |
  What happens in the brain when people meditate, and does practice change it for good? This theme brings together every study on the site that measures the brain, in four topics: the brain's electrical rhythms, the networks that work together across it, how its regions respond to pain, emotion and craving, and its physical structure. Different practices, from focused attention to open awareness and compassion, often leave different signatures, so the findings are best read practice by practice.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation produces distinct, measurable brain states that depend partly on the kind of practice, but the largest randomized trials so far haven't found that it changes brain structure.** The evidence falls into four kinds of study.

  [Brain waves](/themes/brain-waves/): long-term meditators from several traditions show more fast gamma activity than non-meditators, even at rest ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/); [Braboszcz et al., 2017](/gamma-three-meditation-traditions/)) and during sleep ([Ferrarelli et al., 2013](/meditators-sleep-gamma/)), and deep states such as letting go of the sense of self, aiming for emptiness or reaching a cessation each have their own rhythms. Most of these studies compare groups once or follow a few experts.

  [Brain networks](/themes/brain-networks/): meditation often goes with less activity in the default mode network, the regions most active during mind wandering and self-related thinking, and in experts with looser, more interconnected networks ([Josipovic et al., 2012](/nondual-awareness-brain-networks/); [Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). A randomized trial tied a strengthened default mode connection after a three-day retreat to lower inflammation months later ([Creswell et al., 2016](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/)).

  [Brain responses](/themes/brain-responses/): in randomized trials, mindfulness training changed how the brain reacts to painful heat and emotional pictures, including a quieter amygdala ([Zeidan et al., 2015](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/); [Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/); [Kral et al., 2018](/mindfulness-amygdala-reactivity/)), and compassion and craving studies show similar shifts, though brain changes haven't always matched changes in how people felt or acted.

  [Brain structure](/themes/brain-structure/): one-time comparisons of meditators with non-meditators and small early studies reported more gray matter in meditators, but the two large randomized trials that looked for lasting structural change, one over eight weeks and one over 18 months, found none beyond what their comparison groups showed.

  Most of these studies are small, and many compare experienced meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time, so they map what meditation looks like in the brain more than they prove how it changes it.
