## Background Information

As the authors explain in their introduction, studies have shown that a wide range of pleasures, including food, sex, music, humor, money and a mother's love, activate the brain's reward system. In that system, dopamine neurons deep in the brainstem signal to the :term{id="nucleus_accumbens"} in the :term[ventral striatum]{id="striatum"}, which connects to the :term{id="orbitofrontal_cortex"} just above the eyes. In every one of those studies, the pleasure came from something outside the person. The authors of this paper set out to test whether a person could switch the system on with his mind alone.

They studied :term{id="jhana"} meditation, a Buddhist concentration practice whose short-term goal, the authors write, is joy. The jhanas are a sequence of eight states, each said to build on the one before. The meditator first settles into "access concentration," a preparatory meditation on the breath. The first jhana brings strong physical pleasure; in the second, joy "permeates every part of the body"; the third is "deep contentment and serenity"; and the fourth is "equanimity—a profound peace and stillness." The fifth to eighth jhanas are named for subtler perceptions: "infinite space," "infinite consciousness," "nothingness," and "neither perception nor non-perception." Each is said to be more remote from the outside world than the last.

The usual sequence runs from rest to access concentration, then through the first three jhanas, the highest in joy, and on to the fourth to eighth. Adapted from Hagerty et al., 2013 (CC BY).](/assets/article_images/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/jhana-map.jpg)

The authors note that earlier meditation studies of emotion had tested how people learn to turn down negative emotions such as stress. This study asked about turning up a positive one. The paper calls itself the first neural recording during the jhanas.

## What They Did

The participant was a 53-year-old, left-handed man with 17 years of Buddhist practice, about 6,000 hours in all, trained in the Sri Lankan jhana tradition. The authors write that he was, to their knowledge, the only person in the US at the time with the training who was willing to take part.

He meditated in his usual order: access concentration, then up through the jhanas and back down. He signalled with a double finger tap when he began moving to the next jhana and a mouse click when he arrived. Each state lasted about two minutes, with about 30 seconds of transition. The order wasn't randomized, because each jhana builds on the one before.

The team recorded him in two sessions, several months apart:

* **:term{id="fmri"}.** In a 1.5-tesla scanner, he rested, tapped as a control, entered access concentration, and then moved through jhanas 2 to 5. He skipped the first jhana because the head movements that come with it would have spoiled the images, and the scan ended after the fifth jhana because the scanner's memory was full.
* **:term{id="eeg"}.** A 256-electrode cap recorded his full sequence. Data from the first jhana had to be thrown out: it showed gamma power at least ten times higher than any other state, which the authors attribute largely to muscle tension from head movements.

Before looking at the data, the authors wrote down six predictions, each tied to what jhana practitioners report and to known functions of :term[brain regions chosen in advance]{id="region_of_interest"}. Compared with rest, the jhanas should show:

1. Less activity in visual and auditory areas, because awareness of the outside world dims.
2. Less activity in :term{id="brocas_area"} and Wernicke's area, two language regions, because inner talk fades.
3. Less activity in a parietal "orientation area," because the sense of the body's boundaries changes.
4. More activity in the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"}, which regulates and monitors attention, because attention is highly focused.
5. More activity in the reward system (the nucleus accumbens and medial orbitofrontal cortex), because joy rises to very high levels.
6. No increase in movement areas (the :term{id="somatosensory_cortex"}, motor cortex and :term{id="cerebellum"}). An increase would support a rival explanation: that the pleasure came from subtle rhythmic body movements, which can induce ecstatic states.

For the fMRI, the main comparison pooled jhanas 2 to 5 and compared them with rest and with access concentration, using the :term{id="bold_signal"} in 11 brain regions.

## One Big Result: Reward and Attention Areas Grew More Active, and Sensory, Speech and Orientation Areas Less Active, Than at Rest

In the fMRI, all 11 regions moved in the predicted direction during the jhanas compared with rest, and all 11 differences were statistically significant after correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}. The biggest drop was in the orientation area (−1.8% BOLD signal change) and the biggest rise in the medial orbitofrontal cortex (+1.44%). The nucleus accumbens, deep in the reward system, was more active than at rest (+0.88%) and than during access concentration (+0.94%).

![Change in fMRI activity during the jhanas (jhanas 2 to 5 pooled) compared with rest, in the 11 brain regions chosen in advance, for one meditator. Blue bars are regions predicted to quiet down; pink bars are regions predicted to become more active. All 11 differences were statistically significant. Drawn from the values in Table 1 of Hagerty et al., 2013.](/assets/article_images/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/jhana-vs-rest-bold.jpg)

Each region was also compared with access concentration. Across all 22 planned contrasts, 20 were significant; the two exceptions were the auditory areas and the anterior cingulate compared with access concentration. The authors call the attention prediction "more weakly confirmed" for that reason.

Activity in the movement areas fell rather than rose, which the authors take as evidence against the idea that subtle rhythmic movements drove the pleasure.

The EEG told a broadly similar story, with some gaps. The authors combined the frequency bands into one measure: power in fast rhythms (:term{id="beta" case="lower"} and :term{id="gamma" case="lower"}) minus power in slower ones (:term{id="theta" case="lower"} and a low band of :term{id="alpha" case="lower"}). On that combined measure, the largest increases were over the front of the head near the medial orbitofrontal cortex, with smaller increases near the anterior cingulate. Ten of the 14 electrode sites over the areas predicted to quiet down showed significant declines, and the other four showed no significant change. Gamma power on its own, though, rose significantly only near the reward and attention areas and showed no significant change over most of the other regions. The authors acknowledge that scalp EEG has limits in locating where activity comes from, and it can't observe deep structures such as the nucleus accumbens at all.

The study didn't find a quiet brain overall. A whole-brain search turned up 63 clusters that were more active during the jhanas and 27 that were less active, which the authors describe as "patchy" and as suggesting "an overall pattern of diffuse activation during jhana."

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Reward Signal Peaked in the Second Jhana

Looking at each jhana separately, the nucleus accumbens rose steeply from rest and access concentration to the second jhana, then fell back to near resting levels in the third and lower still in the fifth. That runs against reports that the third jhana is just as joyful. The medial orbitofrontal cortex, by contrast, was most active in the fourth jhana, which practitioners describe as less joyful than the second and third. The authors conclude:

> We conclude that full activation of the dopamine reward system occurred only in J2, while J3 activated only the Med OFC portion of the reward system.

The authors also checked the rhythmic-movement explanation in the second jhana alone, since it was the one that activated the full reward pathway. Activity in the somatosensory and motor cortex fell significantly compared with rest, and the cerebellum showed no significant change.

### Deeper Jhanas Didn't Look Deeper

Tradition holds that each jhana is more remote from the outside world than the last, so activity in sensory and orientation areas should keep falling. It didn't. Activity in the visual, auditory and orientation areas was below rest in the second, third and fifth jhanas, but it didn't keep falling with each jhana, and in the fourth it was as high as or higher than at rest. The authors suggest a distraction during the fourth jhana could explain it, though the meditator didn't report one when asked afterward. They caution that these are single meditation states of one person, so one distracting event could greatly alter the pattern.

### The Changes Came Within Seconds

When the meditator signalled that he was moving from access concentration into the second jhana, activity in the orientation area began to drop within about 5 seconds and bottomed out within about 20. The authors note that the jhana was "not so fragile" that the presence of curious experimenters or the noise of an MRI scanner could destroy it, so it can be studied scientifically. Because transitions were quicker than expected, they suggest future studies might alternate meditation with control states in a randomized design, though he might not be able to switch states "on demand."

### Ruling Out Other Explanations

The authors considered how the meditator might have triggered his reward system other than by the meditation itself. Movement areas quieted rather than activating, which argues against subtle rhythmic movements. Visual and auditory areas also quieted, which argues against him calling up happy memories as images or sounds. And because the nucleus accumbens was activated, they argue the joy wasn't purely a cortical expectation effect. (The abstract says three mechanisms were tested and rejected; the discussion lists four: expectations, rhythmic movement, visual memories and auditory memories.) They suggest some untested possibilities, such as a feedback loop between the nucleus accumbens and the orbitofrontal cortex, kept going by the small, repeated successes of following the breath.

### Extreme Bliss, Moderate Signal

The meditator compared the first jhana to continuous multiple orgasms, the second to "opening a birthday gift and getting exactly what you most wished for," and the third to postcoital bliss. Yet the reward-system activation measured in the second jhana was "not extreme." The authors suggest that when the rest of the cortex is quiet, a smaller reward signal stands out more and is felt more intensely:

> If this signal-to-noise view is correct, then jhana's reduced sense awareness is not incidental to achieving extreme pleasure but is a contributing condition.

### A Note of Caution About Self-Stimulation

Because drugs of abuse also stimulate the reward system and can lead to tolerance and withdrawal, the authors advise caution with any voluntary stimulation of it. They report that jhana practitioners describe the opposite of tolerance, though they point to one possible warning sign in this recording:

> In contrast to the drugs, jhana meditators report negative tolerance because they can achieve the same state more quickly with less effort over time, and no withdrawal symptoms have been reported when meditation is stopped. Nevertheless, Figure 3 shows that NAc activity dropped below normal resting consciousness in J5, which may be a sign of short-term tolerance and neurotransmitter depletion.

They also weigh the possible downsides. A meditation that stimulates the reward system without harmful effects such as obesity could be beneficial, they argue, but one that undercut the drive to work toward long-term goals could become dysfunctional.

### A Different Signature From Other Meditations

The authors note that the pattern they found differs from the dominant gamma rhythms reported during [Tibetan Buddhist compassion meditation](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/) and from the alpha dominance reported during :term{id="transcendental_meditation"}. They suggest meditation may give access to a wide range of altered states, depending on which brain regions are given awareness and which are inhibited.

## Final Reflection

This was the first look inside the brain during the jhanas, and the fMRI results lined up neatly with what the authors predicted from practitioners' descriptions. The most striking finding is that the nucleus accumbens, a deep part of the reward system usually studied with food, money or drugs, was more active during meditation with no outside reward at all.

But it rests on one man, recorded once by each method, months apart. The fMRI covered only four of the eight jhanas, the first jhana was lost to head movement in both recordings, the states always came in the same order, and the only comparison was ordinary rest or access concentration. The authors themselves suggest that a better control would be a condition known to raise happiness, such as remembering a happy event or picturing a loved one. They also caution that because the meditator was left-handed, results involving one side of the brain, such as Wernicke's area, should be read with care. The abstract says the results "demonstrate" a new way of self-stimulating the reward system; with one participant, they are better read as a promising case to test in more meditators.
