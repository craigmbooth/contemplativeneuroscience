## Background Information

People have many ways of dealing with pain. The authors list two broad families: turning attention away from it, such as listening to music, and changing the context of the experience, as in suppressing one's reaction, :term{id="cognitive_reappraisal" case="lower"}, hypnosis or a :term{id="placebo" case="lower"}. All of these try to control some part of the pain.

Acceptance and :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} take a different approach. The paper's introduction describes a state that does not strive to ignore, reject or avoid pain, and says this should be especially useful when pain can't be avoided but is known to be safe, because it may weaken the mind's habit of avoidance and anxiety, which can make pain worse. Treatments built on this idea, such as :term{id="mbsr"} and :term[acceptance and commitment therapy]{id="act"} (ACT), had been found to reduce pain unpleasantness and the symptoms of :term{id="chronic_pain" case="lower"}.

The authors place this attitude at the core of :term[open monitoring]{id="open_monitoring"} meditation, in which practitioners cultivate an effortless, accepting awareness of whatever is happening, without reacting to it or getting absorbed in it. The aim is not to change the pain but one's relationship to it:

> In that sense, the sensation produced by the painful stimulus can be experienced during this state with equal or increased vividness in the moment it occurs, without fear.

The study focused on the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}, a region tied to emotion and anxiety, and on what the paper calls the "so-called 'salience network'": the anterior (front) :term{id="insula" case="lower"} and the anterior mid-cingulate cortex, together with deeper regions involved in emotion, bodily regulation and reward. Earlier imaging studies had linked activity in these areas to the anticipation of pain and to how bad the pain then feels. The authors made three predictions. During pain, experts would rate it as less unpleasant and show more activity in the :term{id="salience_network" case="lower"}. Before pain, they would show less anticipatory activity in the amygdala and salience network. And over the session, their brain responses would fade faster with repetition, a process called :term{id="habituation" case="lower"}.

## What They Did

The researchers used :term{id="fmri"} to compare 14 long-term practitioners with 14 novices. The practitioners had at least 10,000 hours of formal meditation in the Nyingma and Kagyu traditions of Tibetan Buddhism (27,000 hours on average), which the paper calls "experts" for brevity. They were 45 years old on average, 9 men and 5 women; 11 were Caucasian and 3 Tibetan. The 14 novices were recruited from the local community and matched for age, sex and pain temperature. They had never meditated but were interested in learning.

The experts practiced Open Presence, which the paper treats as an advanced form of open monitoring from the Dzogchen and :term{id="mahamudra"} traditions, in which openness and acceptance are vividly experienced with minimal effort to control. The novices received written instructions in the practices and meditated at home for 30 minutes a day for 7 days before the experiment. To make up for the experts' likely greater motivation, the novices were told that the four of them with the largest drop in pain-related brain activity during meditation would get a $50 bonus.

Before scanning, each person's heat level was calibrated in a mock scanner so the stimulus felt like about 8 on a 0–10 pain scale. A thermal probe on the inside of the left forearm, just below the wrist, delivered the heat, at 46 to 49°C (48.1°C on average for experts and 48.2°C for novices).

In the scanner, each person did 32 trials in 8 blocks of 4. Each trial began with a cue to meditate, either in Open Presence or in :term[focused attention]{id="focused_attention"} on a fixation cross (the main analyses use the Open Presence trials). After 45 seconds of meditation came 12 seconds of warmth at 38°C, then 10 seconds of either painful heat or a warm stimulus 6°C cooler. Participants weren't told in advance which it would be. They then rated how intense the heat was ("how hot was it") and how unpleasant ("how much did it bother you"), each from 0 to 10. Between blocks the researchers checked that participants were following the instructions, and a camera on one eye monitored them throughout.

The analysis compared three things. The pain response was activity during the 10 seconds of heat minus activity during the warm stimulus. Anticipatory activity (the paper calls it "baseline activity") was the change from the start to the end of the 45-second meditation, as the stimulus approached. Habituation was the trend in activity across the 8 blocks: a downward slope meant activity was fading over the session.

## One Big Result: Experts Rated the Heat as Just as Intense, but Less Unpleasant

The groups' ratings diverged on one scale only. Experts rated the painful heat as less unpleasant than novices did (t(25) = 3.6, p = .001), but their intensity ratings were similar (t(25) = 0.6, p = .52). The difference between the two patterns was significant (F(1,25) = 9.5, p = .005). The paper's text doesn't give the groups' average ratings.

The experts' brains also responded differently to the heat. In two salience-network regions, the left anterior insula and the anterior mid-cingulate cortex, the response to painful heat (compared with warmth) was larger in experts than in novices. Within these regions, experts responded significantly more strongly to the heat than to the warm stimulus, while novices showed no significant difference between them. In the posterior insula and secondary sensory cortex, regions that handle the physical sensation, the groups did not differ. The authors read this as a change in higher-level processing of pain rather than in raw sensitivity, which fits the matching intensity ratings.

In novices, more activity in these two regions went with higher unpleasantness ratings (r = 0.70 in the insula and 0.65 in the cingulate). In experts it didn't (r = −0.26 and −0.15), and the difference between the groups was significant. Both analyses took intensity ratings into account. The authors offer an interpretation:

> We propose that the phasic enhancement of this network during pain could relate to an enhanced capacity to flexibly modulate conditioned automatic reactions to an aversive event.

Because the groups were compared once, rather than people being trained and followed, this shows a difference between experts and novices, not that meditation practice produced it.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Less Build-Up Before the Heat

As predicted, novices' activity in the left anterior insula and anterior mid-cingulate cortex rose more than experts' over the meditation period leading up to the stimulus. The same was true in the amygdala, tested as an anatomically defined :term[region of interest]{id="region_of_interest"} (t(26) = 2.3, p < .05). A whole-brain search also found stronger anticipatory activity in novices in the left insula. Experts were not simply switched off, though: they too showed anticipatory activity in several overlapping regions, which the authors take to mean Open Presence modulated these processes rather than suppressing them.

Less build-up went with a bigger response during the heat. In a set of exploratory analyses, the more a person's left anterior insula rose before the stimulus, the less it responded to the heat itself (r = −0.52 across both groups, with the group difference factored out). Among the experts, more lifetime practice went with less anticipatory activity in the left anterior insula (r = −0.63, with age taken into account). Anticipatory activity in these regions did not correlate with unpleasantness ratings, however (all p > .17).

### "Faster Habituation" Mostly Meant Novices Ramping Up

The paper's abstract says expert meditation was associated with enhanced neural habituation before painful stimulation. The results show where that came from. Before the heat, the trend across blocks was more negative for experts than novices in the amygdala and three pain-related regions, but the difference was driven by the novices' anticipatory activity *increasing* over the session. During the heat, the picture was closer to habituation in the usual sense: in the posterior insula and secondary sensory cortex and in the mid-insula, experts' responses declined across blocks while novices' did not.

In both groups, unpleasantness fell relative to intensity across the session, and the intensity ratings themselves did not drift (p = .26). In an exploratory analysis, people whose mid-cingulate activity before the heat declined more over the session also showed more of this change in their ratings (r = 0.40, controlling for group). People with less anticipatory activity in the amygdala also showed faster declines across the session in mid-cingulate activity during the heat.

### Experts Were Likely More Motivated

The authors acknowledge that the two groups probably differed in more than meditation:

> In particular, the experts likely have greater motivation to demonstrate effectiveness of the meditation practices due to their long-term personal investment in them.

They concede the $50 bonus for novices was "unlikely to match the motivation of lifelong practitioners." A supplementary analysis found that, among experts, Open Presence affected pain more than focused attention, another familiar practice, which the authors say makes these :term{id="demand_characteristics" case="lower"} a less likely explanation. They also note that their account of anticipatory activity relies on reverse inference, interpreting a brain region's activity by what it has been linked to elsewhere, because anticipation wasn't experimentally manipulated. A ceiling effect in the anterior insula could account for part of the link between activity before and during the heat.

### Two Participants Worth Noting

One expert had been diagnosed with :term{id="fibromyalgia" case="lower"} nearly 20 years earlier, and another had a lesion in the left frontal cortex after an abscess was removed in childhood. The authors report that leaving either one out did not change the significance of any test (for the second, of any test in the main regions).

### Same Lab, Same Design, Earlier Ratings

The authors describe the ratings as in line with their "preliminary behavioral report" from the same lab, [Perlman et al. (2010)](/open-monitoring-pain-unpleasantness/), which compared Open Presence with focused attention in experts and novices. The paper doesn't say how many participants the two reports share.

### Not Quite the Same as an Eight-Week Course

The paper's findings partly replicate earlier studies, including one in which [brief mindfulness training](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/) lowered pain ratings and increased activity in the anterior insula and anterior cingulate. They don't replicate every earlier finding, such as lower amygdala activity during pain in experienced meditators, which the authors say could reflect errors in a young field or differences in meditation style and expertise. They also caution that the Open Presence of lifelong practitioners "is likely to induce different effects than a short 8-week meditation-based therapy." They predict that programs like MBSR will also affect pain anticipation, habituation and emotional reactivity, but expect the effects to be stronger in Open Presence, a distinction they say "needs future empirical validation."

## Final Reflection

This study separated two parts of pain that are easy to run together: how strong it is and how much it bothers you. Long-term practitioners in an open, accepting meditation felt the heat as strongly as novices but were less bothered by it, and their brains showed a matching pattern: less build-up in emotion- and salience-related regions as the heat approached, and a larger salience-network response once it arrived, one that no longer tracked how unpleasant they found it.

The limits are those of most expert–novice comparisons. Fourteen people per group is small; experts chose decades of practice, likely cared more about the outcome, and differ from novices in many ways besides meditation; and many of the brain–rating links were exploratory. The authors call for longitudinal research that follows people as they train. On its own terms, this is a careful early picture of what pain looks like in the brains of people who have practiced meeting it with openness, and a set of hypotheses for training studies to test.
