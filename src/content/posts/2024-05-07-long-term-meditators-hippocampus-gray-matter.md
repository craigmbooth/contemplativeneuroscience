## Background Information

By the late 2000s, studies had shown that adult brains can change their structure with intensive training. Gray matter grew, for example, in people who learned to juggle or studied for a medical exam. Meditation, which traditionally means years of daily practice, seemed a natural candidate for similar changes. Yet when this study was carried out, only two studies had directly compared the brain structure of meditators and non-meditators. Both looked at Insight (:term{id="vipassana"}) practitioners. Between them, they had found a thicker cortex or more concentrated :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"} in meditators in places including the right :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, the right :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, the left :term{id="inferior_temporal_gyrus" case="lower"} and parts of the right frontal lobe.

Eileen Luders, Arthur Toga and Natasha Lepore at UCLA's Laboratory of Neuro Imaging, with Christian Gaser of the University of Jena, set out to add to that small literature in two ways. Their meditators had practiced for much longer than those in the earlier studies, on average for about 24 years. And rather than studying one tradition, they deliberately recruited practitioners of several, hoping to find brain features common to long-term meditation in general rather than to one style.

## What They Did

The team recruited 25 active meditators through referrals and advertisements at meditation venues. Three were excluded because their scans showed brain abnormalities that weren't clinically significant, leaving 22 (13 women and 9 men). Their practice ranged from 5 to 46 years, with an average of 24. Their styles included :term[Zazen]{id="zen"}, :term[Samatha]{id="shamatha"}, Vipassana and others. Asked what their practice involved, 63% named deep concentration, about a third named control of the breath (36%), visualization (32%) or attention to external and internal events (32%), and fewer named letting go of thoughts (18%) or withdrawing from the senses (14%). Sessions lasted 10 to 90 minutes, and 59% of the meditators sat daily.

The 22 comparison participants were not recruited for the study. They came from an existing database of brain scans of healthy adults (the International Consortium for Brain Mapping), chosen to match the meditators pair by pair for sex and age, with no more than a year's difference within a pair. The groups were aged 30 to 71, with an average age of 53 in both, and had similar levels of education. The paper doesn't say whether the comparison participants had ever meditated.

Everyone had a single high-resolution structural MRI scan. The team then looked for differences at three scales:

- **Global:** the total volume of the brain and of its gray matter.
- **Local:** using :term{id="voxel_based_morphometry" case="lower"} (VBM), they compared the amount of gray matter at every point across the brain. This version of VBM measures gray matter *volume*, the actual amount of tissue at each point, rather than the gray matter *density* used in some earlier studies (explained in our [summary of Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)). Because this means thousands of separate tests, they corrected for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}, using a correction applied within a sphere 60 mm across (a "small volume correction"), and counted only clusters of at least 693 neighboring voxels.
- **Regional:** software traced the outlines of five brain structures in each scan, the ones highlighted by the two earlier studies: the left inferior temporal gyrus, right insula, right hippocampus, and right superior and middle frontal gyri. The team compared the volume of each of these :term[regions of interest]{id="region_of_interest"} between the groups, after adjusting for overall brain size.

Finally, they checked whether gray matter was related to how many years people had meditated.

## One Big Result: More Gray Matter Above the Right Eye, and a Larger Right Hippocampus

Overall, the meditators' brains were no bigger than the comparison group's. Neither total brain volume nor total gray matter differed (both groups averaged about 653 cm³ of gray matter). The differences were local.

The whole-brain search found one cluster that passed the correction for multiple comparisons: a region of more gray matter in the meditators in the right :term{id="orbitofrontal_cortex" case="lower"}, the part of the frontal lobe just above the eye socket (p = 0.04, corrected). No part of the brain had more gray matter in the comparison group than in the meditators.

Of the five regions measured as whole structures, only one differed. The right hippocampus was larger in the meditators, averaging 3.73 cm³ against 3.53 cm³ (p = 0.01). The other four regions, including the right insula and the frontal areas where the earlier studies had found differences, did not differ significantly, and the two frontal regions were slightly larger in the comparison group. The paper reports a separate test for each of the five regions and doesn't mention correcting for having tested five.

The authors suggest that both regions fit with what meditators are known for. The orbitofrontal cortex has been linked to regulating emotion and to acting less on habit, and the hippocampus to emotional responding and attention. The abstract goes further, saying the larger volumes "might account for meditators' singular abilities and habits to cultivate positive emotions, retain emotional stability, and engage in mindful behavior." These are the authors' interpretations, drawn from other research. The study didn't measure the participants' emotions, attention or behavior, so it can't link the brain differences to anything the meditators do.

The authors are also clear that the design can't show which came first:

> Obviously, the outcomes of the current study do not provide any indicators for a causal relationship between the long-term practice of meditation and brain structures.

They describe two possibilities. People with these brain features might be drawn to meditation, or find it easier to keep practicing (a form of :term{id="self_selection_bias" case="lower"}). Or decades of practice might have reshaped regions that meditation routinely uses. They lean toward the second, noting that the hippocampus keeps generating new neurons in adulthood, but call for studies that follow people over time to decide between them.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Two Further Clusters Were Weaker Than the Abstract Suggests

When the team dropped both the correction for multiple comparisons and the minimum cluster size, two more small clusters of extra gray matter appeared in the meditators: one in the right :term{id="thalamus" case="lower"} (150 voxels) and one in the left inferior temporal gyrus (230 voxels). Both fell well short of the 693-voxel threshold. In an extra, exploratory analysis that adjusted for age, also without correction, the thalamus cluster grew larger, the orbitofrontal cluster shrank, and the inferior temporal cluster appeared only when the minimum cluster size was lowered further. Two more clusters, near the paracentral lobules on both sides of the brain, appeared only in this analysis, and the authors set them aside as preliminary.

The abstract describes the thalamus and inferior temporal results as "significantly larger" gray matter "when co-varying for age and/or lowering applied statistical thresholds." A footnote is more careful:

> Although group effects in the right thalamus and left inferior temporal gyrus also became evident without co-varying for age, these findings neither survived corrections for multiple comparisons nor the specified extent thresholds.

The authors note that the inferior temporal cluster sat almost exactly where one of the earlier studies had found a difference that approached significance, but they call both findings ones that "clearly require further investigation."

### More Years of Practice Didn't Mean More Gray Matter

If meditation gradually builds gray matter, people who have practiced longer should have more. The team found no link between gray matter and years of practice anywhere in the brain. They suspected that age might hide such a link: those with the longest practice were also the oldest, and gray matter shrinks with age. In this sample, 68% of the meditators were over 50. So they also split the meditators into those with fewer than 20 years of practice (13 people) and those with more than 20 (9 people), and compared each group with its own age-matched controls. Again, the more experienced meditators showed no greater difference from their controls than the less experienced ones.

The authors caution against reading too much into this. Even their "less experienced" group had practiced for 5 to 20 years, so the study had no true beginners. They suggest that any change might happen in the first few years of practice and then level off, which this sample couldn't detect. A 2014 meta-analysis of brain-structure studies in meditators found a similar pattern across studies: [differences were, if anything, smaller in studies of more experienced meditators](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/), and its authors also pointed to age as one possible reason.

### Earlier Findings Only Partly Repeated

The study did not find the differences that an earlier study had reported in the right insula or in parts of the right frontal lobe. The authors offer several reasons. That study measured :term{id="cortical_thickness" case="lower"}, a different property from gray matter volume. The insula difference in another earlier study was very small. And this study's meditators had practiced almost three times as long as those in the earlier studies (24.2 years against 8.6 and 9.1) and were older (around 53 against around 34 and 37), and practiced a mix of styles rather than Vipassana alone. Different practices, the authors note, may engage different brain networks.

The hippocampus did line up. One of the earlier studies had found more concentrated gray matter in the right hippocampus of Vipassana meditators, and the larger right hippocampus here agrees with it.

### Where This Fits

Other studies on this site have looked at the same regions with different designs. An eight-week :term{id="mbsr"} course was [linked to denser gray matter in the left hippocampus](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/) in a small study of 16 newcomers, but a much larger randomized trial of 218 people [found no structural changes after MBSR](/mindfulness-brain-structure/). And a meta-analysis that pooled 21 brain-structure studies, most comparing long-term meditators with non-meditators as this one did, found that [the hippocampus was among a handful of regions where differences recurred](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/), while warning that small studies and :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"} probably inflate the size of such differences.

## Final Reflection

This study compared the brains of people who had meditated for an average of 24 years with closely matched non-meditators. The meditators' brains were no larger overall, but they had more gray matter in one region of the right orbitofrontal cortex and a larger right hippocampus. Those are modest, local differences, from 22 people per group, and only one of them came from the main whole-brain search after correction. Other reported clusters were exploratory, and the larger hippocampus came from one of five separate tests.

The bigger limitation is the one the authors state themselves. A single scan of people who chose to meditate for decades can't tell whether meditation shaped their brains or whether people with these brains are drawn to meditation and stay with it. That the differences didn't grow with years of practice doesn't settle the question either way. The study is best read as an early map of where to look. As the authors say, only studies that follow people over time can show whether long-term practice itself changes these regions.
