## Background Information

From about our mid-twenties, the brain starts to lose volume and weight, and the loss continues with age. This :term[brain atrophy]{id="brain_atrophy"} is gradual and normal, but it goes along with a higher risk of cognitive decline and dementia. With people living longer, the authors argue, it matters to find things that protect the brain, not only risk factors to avoid.

Meditation is one candidate. Several studies have found that meditators' brains differ in structure from non-meditators', and a few had asked a sharper question: is the usual link between getting older and losing brain tissue weaker in people who meditate? When this paper was written, the authors counted only three such studies. The closest to theirs, by Pagnoni and Cekic (2007), looked at :term{id="gray_matter" case="lower"} in just 25 people with an average age in their thirties.

This study set out to repeat that approach in a larger and older group: 100 people, half of them long-term meditators, with an average age in their early fifties.

## What They Did

The meditators were 50 people (28 men, 22 women) recruited around Los Angeles, with between 4 and 46 years of meditation experience (about 20 years on average). The comparison group was 50 people with the same mix of men and women, whose scans came from the International Consortium for Brain Mapping database of healthy adults. The two groups were closely matched for age: everyone was between 24 and 77, and the average age was 51.4 for meditators and 50.4 for the comparison group. All scans were made at the same site, on the same scanner, with the same settings. The paper describes the work as a re-analysis of this sample.

Each person had a single structural MRI scan. Using :term{id="voxel_based_morphometry"}, the team measured gray matter in two ways:

- **Global gray matter**: the total volume of gray matter across the whole brain.
- **Local gray matter**: the amount of gray matter at each point (voxel) throughout the brain, so that every region was examined rather than a few chosen in advance.

For each measure, they first checked how strongly gray matter was related to age within each group (a :term{id="correlation" case="lower"}, taking account of sex). Then came the key test, a group-by-age :term[interaction]{id="moderation"}: was the link between age and gray matter significantly different in meditators than in non-meditators?

Because everyone was scanned only once, "decline with age" here means that older participants had less gray matter than younger ones. Nobody was followed over time.

## One Big Result: Older Age Went With Less Gray Matter in Everyone, but Less So in Meditators

In both groups, older people had less gray matter in total, a clear negative correlation (p < 0.001 in each group). But the link was stronger in the non-meditators (r = −0.77) than in the meditators (r = −0.58), and the non-meditators' regression line was considerably steeper. The group-by-age interaction for total gray matter was statistically significant (p = 0.003): gray matter differed less between younger and older meditators than between younger and older non-meditators.

In other words, meditators also showed the usual pattern of less gray matter at older ages. They didn't escape it, but it was shallower.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Regional Picture Was Weaker Than the Abstract Suggests

Looking point by point across the brain, older age went with less gray matter in both groups, and these links held up after strict correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}. The age-linked areas were much more widespread in the non-meditators. Neither group had any region where older people had more gray matter.

![Brain areas (red) where older age went with less gray matter, in non-meditators (top) and meditators (bottom), shown from the side, back and top. The areas are more widespread in non-meditators. From Luders et al., 2015 (CC BY 4.0).](/assets/article_images/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/local-gray-matter-by-age.jpg)

The formal test of whether that difference between groups was real, region by region, is less solid. With the strict correction, it found nothing. The authors explain what they did next:

> FWE-corrections resulted in a lack of significance clusters for the group-by-age interaction, but given that such interactions have been reported previously, we repeated the analysis without the rather conservative FWE-corrections.

In the repeated analysis, they counted only clusters of at least 1,039 neighboring voxels, a size threshold meant to filter out chance specks. That found nine clusters where the age link differed between groups. The largest (about 68 ml of gray matter) ran from the left :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"} and :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} back through the :term[occipital cortex]{id="occipital_lobe"} and parietal cortex, including the left :term[posterior cingulate]{id="pcc"}. The others included both :term[insulas]{id="insula"}, the :term[anterior cingulate]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} and orbital frontal cortex, the left :term[temporoparietal junction]{id="tpj"}, the :term{id="cerebellum" case="lower"}, and the hypothalamus and medial :term{id="thalamus" case="lower"}.

The abstract describes these as "significant group-by-age interactions in numerous clusters throughout the brain" without mentioning that they appeared only after the stricter correction was dropped. The methods section is open about it, but the regional findings are best treated as tentative. The whole-brain result above doesn't depend on that correction.

### The Earlier Study Found Something Stronger

In Pagnoni and Cekic's smaller and younger sample, total gray matter tended to fall with age in non-meditators, but not in meditators, where there was essentially no link with age (r = 0.006). Here, by contrast, meditators showed a clear decline too. The authors suggest the difference may come from their much older group. The earlier study also found one regional difference, in the putamen; this one found nine clusters, none of them in the putamen.

### Two Possible Explanations, Both Untested

The authors offer two ways meditation might produce this pattern. One is growth: mental training might add gray matter, which over time could "mask" normal age-related loss. They point to an [eight-week mindfulness study](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/) that found gray matter increases in the hippocampus, posterior cingulate, temporoparietal junction and cerebellum, all regions where this study also found clusters. The other is preservation: meditation might slow the loss itself, for instance by reducing stress, whose effects on the brain are especially strong in regions such as the hippocampus.

They also note a simpler possibility: meditators might simply be healthier, with better habits around eating, sleep, work and exercise, or differ in intelligence or socioeconomic status. None of these was measured, so the authors call all of it "merely conjecture."

### Who Becomes a Long-Term Meditator?

The authors are candid that people who meditate for decades may differ from everyone else to begin with:

> For example, in order to keep meditating for close to 20 years, individuals need to possess a minimum level of discipline and commitment, a well-organized life that allows them the spare time, an awareness of the possibility to control their own life, perhaps even a calm nature to begin with.

Such :term{id="self_selection_bias" case="lower"} could produce both the meditation habit and the healthier-looking brain. None of these traits was assessed in either group.

### What Wasn't Tested

The paper doesn't report whether meditators with more years of practice showed less age-related loss, or whether different meditation styles differed; the authors list both as questions for future work. It also didn't measure thinking or memory, so it can't say whether the preserved gray matter made any practical difference.

## Final Reflection

This study gives a tidy result for total gray matter: across a wide age range, the gap between younger and older adults was smaller among long-term meditators than among non-meditators. The title's "Forever Young(er)" is playful, and the authors' own language in the paper is careful:

> On this note, we also wish to emphasize that, given the cross-sectional design of our study, it is impossible to draw any clear causal inferences.

A snapshot of different people at different ages is not the same as watching brains age. The 70-year-old meditators here may have started out with more gray matter, lived healthier lives, or been different kinds of people from the 70-year-olds in the database. The regional findings needed a looser statistical standard to appear. The authors call for longitudinal studies, following the same people over years, to show whether meditation actually slows the brain's aging. Until then, this is an encouraging association, not proof of protection.
