## Background Information

Most brain research on :term[emotion regulation]{id="emotion_regulation"} has studied strategies that work through deliberate mental control: distracting yourself, or :term[reappraisal]{id="cognitive_reappraisal"}, rethinking what a situation means so that it feels less upsetting. Reappraisal is thought to rely on the prefrontal cortex, the brain's hub for effortful control, which in turn damps down emotional areas such as the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}. The authors point out that this kind of control may not work well for everyone, including children, older adults and some patient groups, or in every situation, such as under stress. So they asked whether there are effective strategies that don't depend on it.

Their candidate was mindful acceptance: paying attention to what is happening right now, even when it is unpleasant, and letting it be as it is, without reacting to it, judging it or trying to avoid it. Mindfulness- and acceptance-based treatments help with depression, anxiety and chronic pain, but those are long programs with many parts. Brain studies of meditators, such as [experts receiving painful heat](/activation-during-anticipation-pain/) or [beginners after four days of training](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/), involve practice built up over days or years, and people who choose to meditate may differ from those who don't. This study asked a narrower question: what happens in the brain when people who have never meditated simply adopt an accepting attitude toward something unpleasant, in the moment?

The team, which included the developers of a brain-based measure of pain called the Neurologic Pain Signature, set out to test three things: whether acceptance makes pain and negative emotion feel less bad, whether it changes the brain's responses to them, and whether it works through the prefrontal control regions that reappraisal relies on.

## What They Did

Seventeen right-handed adults in New York City (5 women, aged 18 to 45) with no meditation experience took part. They were screened for neurological and psychiatric conditions and for psychoactive medication. One man was scanned but excluded before analysis because he said he hadn't followed the instructions, leaving 16. The authors chose the sample size in advance, based on emotion-regulation studies of the time, which typically had 16 to 18 participants, and on their funding.

Before scanning, each person had 30 minutes of training in two instructions. REACT meant to "react naturally, whatever your response might be," and served as the comparison. ACCEPT meant attending to the present sensation and accepting it without judging it, an instruction the authors modeled on a standard two-part definition of :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} and on mindfulness programs, :term[Acceptance and Commitment Therapy]{id="act"} and Dialectical Behavior Therapy. For example:

> For example, participants were told ‘if you feel a sensation of warmth on your forearm, you should simply attend to what is felt, without making any judgment of the “goodness” or “badness” of that sensation.’

People practiced on sample pictures, saying their responses aloud, and didn't start scanning until they showed they could follow both instructions.

In the :term[fMRI]{id="fmri"} scanner, they saw 30 negative and 30 neutral photos from a standard research set and felt 30 painfully hot and 30 warm heat pulses on the forearm. The temperatures were set for each person beforehand: the hot ones (45–48°C) at 8 on a 10-point pain scale, the warm ones (40.5–44°C) at 2, described as non-painful warmth. Stimuli came in blocks of about a minute, each starting with a 3-second ACCEPT or REACT cue, with a colored border around the screen as a reminder throughout the block. Each stimulus lasted 8 seconds, and after a short pause, people rated how negatively they felt on an eight-point scale. Each of the five scanning runs held 24 trials.

Besides comparing brain activity region by region, the team applied the Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS), a pattern of activity spread across many brain areas that members of the team had built with machine learning (a form of :term[multivoxel pattern analysis]{id="multivoxel_pattern_analysis"}) and validated in earlier studies. It responds in step with rising heat and pain reports and, in that earlier work, didn't respond to threat cues or emotional pictures. The authors note that reappraisal had not lowered it in previous research.

## One Big Result: Accepting Painful Heat Lowered a Brain Signature of Pain by About a Quarter

First, the NPS behaved as it should in this group. It responded more strongly to hot than warm heat under both instructions, picked out the hot condition in 93.75% of participants, and didn't respond to either kind of photo. The authors treat this as a check that the scans were of good quality.

The key comparison was between the hot trials: the same temperatures, felt with an accepting attitude or reacting naturally. The NPS response was significantly lower during ACCEPT, a 26% drop (t(15) = 2.59, p = .02, :term[effect size]{id="effect_size"} d = 0.65). The authors note that this effect was larger than the effects of :term[placebos]{id="placebo"} in 19 of 20 studies in a recent review of placebo effects on the NPS.

Because the NPS is read from brain activity rather than from what people say, the authors argue that acceptance changed the pain response itself, not just how people judged or reported it. They suggest acceptance might work early in the chain of events that produces an emotion, so that the same hot stimulus registers as less intense, perhaps as a passing physical sensation rather than something harmful. Another of their explanations draws on the Buddhist idea that acceptance keeps a second layer of distress from being added to the first, "the second arrow." They offer several such explanations, and the study wasn't designed to tell them apart.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### People Felt Less Bad About Both Pictures and Heat

As expected, negative photos and hot heat made people feel much worse than neutral photos and warm heat. Accepting lowered their ratings for both kinds of stimulus: for the photos (t(15) = 4.56, p < .001, d = 1.14) and for the heat (t(15) = 6.05, p < .001, d = 1.51), with no significant difference between the two in how much acceptance helped. The paper gives these as test statistics; its Figure 2 shows the averages, and also marks ACCEPT ratings as significantly lower for the neutral photos and warm heat.

The heat ratings used the same "how negatively do you feel" scale as the photos. So unlike [other pain studies on this site](/open-monitoring-pain-unpleasantness/), this one doesn't separate how intense the heat felt from how unpleasant it was.

### A Quieter Amygdala, but Only for Upsetting Pictures

Negative photos activated the amygdala, along with the thalamus, the :term[periaqueductal gray]{id="periaqueductal_gray"} in the midbrain and the :term[dorsal anterior cingulate]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"}. Accepting reduced the right amygdala's response to negative photos, and this was the one area that both responded to negative photos and was quieted by acceptance. The effect was specific: the same spot showed no acceptance effect for neutral photos or on hot trials. Acceptance also lowered activity in nearby midbrain areas, parts of the temporal lobe and the frontopolar cortex. A quieter right amygdala also turned up after eight weeks of mindfulness training in a [small randomized trial](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/), though there it was measured while people weren't meditating.

### Pain Areas Quieted Too

Painful heat activated a familiar set of pain-responsive regions, including the dorsal anterior cingulate, the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, the :term{id="thalamus" case="lower"}, the :term[somatosensory cortex]{id="somatosensory_cortex"} and the periaqueductal gray. Accepting painful heat lowered activity in many of the same regions, and in back-of-the-brain areas such as the posterior cingulate and precuneus. The overlap between areas that responded to pain and areas that acceptance quieted included the insula, the thalamus and nearby sensory cortex.

### No Sign of the Brain's Control Regions

None of the whole-brain comparisons showed extra prefrontal activity during acceptance. The team then ran two more targeted tests. Four prefrontal :term[regions of interest]{id="region_of_interest"} that a meta-analysis by the same group had found reliably active during reappraisal, including the :term{id="dlpfc"}, were no more active during ACCEPT than REACT, for either photos or heat. And a :term[psychophysiological interaction]{id="psychophysiological_interaction"} analysis found no prefrontal or cingulate region that coordinated differently with the amygdala or the pain regions during acceptance.

The authors read this as evidence that acceptance works "bottom-up," changing how a stimulus is first registered, rather than through "top-down" control. They are candid that this is an absence of evidence:

> Finally, we acknowledge that we rely on a series of null results in reporting that mindful acceptance does not recruit PFC. It is possible that future analyses might reveal different findings, especially with larger samples.

A [similar single-session study of smokers](/mindful-attention-smoking-cues-brain/), on which the first author was a coauthor, also found that mindfully attending to smoking pictures quieted a craving-linked area without engaging prefrontal control regions.

### What the Design Can't Show

This was one session with 16 healthy volunteers, and the authors list the small sample as the first limitation. Everyone did both conditions, a :term[within-subject]{id="within_subject_design"} design that lets each person serve as their own comparison, but acceptance was compared only with reacting naturally, not with another strategy, so the study can't say whether acceptance works better than, or differently from, reappraisal. The authors say a direct comparison is needed. Participants knew which instruction they were following, so their ratings could partly reflect what they thought was expected, the :term[demand characteristics]{id="demand_characteristics"} problem; the brain measures, especially the NPS, don't depend on a rating, which is why the authors put weight on them. The authors also note that their version of mindful acceptance may not represent every mindfulness or acceptance practice. And the study measured only what happened during the scan: it says nothing about whether the effect lasts, grows with practice or helps people with chronic pain or emotional disorders.

## Final Reflection

This study's appeal is its simplicity. With half an hour of instruction and no meditation practice, people who adopted an accepting stance toward an upsetting photo or a painful burst of heat felt less bad, and their brains responded less in the amygdala and in pain regions. A brain pattern designed to track physical pain fell by about a quarter, and the prefrontal control regions behind reappraisal showed no sign of extra effort. The authors draw a strong conclusion:

> Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that mindful acceptance is a powerful emotion-regulation strategy, that can be learned quickly, deployed effectively, and that may not depend on PFC to profoundly alter the psychological and neural consequences of negative affect and pain.

The results fit with other work on this site. Beginners who had [four short lessons in meditation](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/) rated heat as less painful while meditating, and a [later study from the same lab](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/) found mindfulness relieved heat pain more than a sham meditation or a placebo cream did. But "powerful" and "profoundly" are big words for a single session with 16 people, one comparison condition and a central claim, no prefrontal involvement, that rests on not finding something. The study is best read as a promising, carefully measured first look at acceptance as an in-the-moment strategy, one the authors themselves say needs a larger study and a head-to-head comparison with reappraisal.
