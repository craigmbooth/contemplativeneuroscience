## Background Information

By 2012, a series of brain-imaging studies had reported that long-term meditators' brains differ in structure from non-meditators'. Almost all of them measured the amount of :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"} or the :term{id="cortical_thickness" case="lower"} of the cortex, the brain's outer layer. The regions they pointed to included the right :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, parts of the frontal cortex and the left inferior temporal gyrus. One of them, by the same lead author, found [more gray matter in a frontal region and a larger right hippocampus](/long-term-meditators-hippocampus-gray-matter/) in 22 people who had meditated for decades.

The cortex has other features, though. To fit inside the skull, it is folded into ridges (gyri) and grooves (sulci). This folding, called :term{id="gyrification" case="lower"}, describes the shape of the brain's surface rather than how much tissue it holds. Eileen Luders and colleagues at UCLA, with Christian Gaser of the University of Jena, reasoned that studies looking only at gray matter might have missed differences in how the cortex is folded. Theirs was the first study to ask whether meditators' cortex is folded differently, and with 100 participants, the authors describe it as the largest sample of meditators studied with structural brain imaging up to that time.

They didn't predict a direction, but they did predict places: the differences should appear where earlier studies had found them. Their list had seven regions, including the right insula, right and left frontal cortex, left inferior temporal and fusiform gyri, the cortex around the top of the brain that handles touch and movement, and the cingulate cortex.

## What They Did

The team recruited 50 meditators (28 men and 22 women) from meditation venues. They had practiced for between 4 and 46 years, about 20 years on average. Their self-reported styles varied: :term{id="vipassana"} was the most common, named by 23 of them, alone or with other styles, followed by :term[Zen]{id="zen"} (9) and :term[Samatha]{id="shamatha"} (8), and eight didn't name a style. Sessions ranged from 10 to 240 minutes, and 31 of the 50 meditated every day.

The comparison group came from an existing database of brain scans of healthy adults (the International Consortium for Brain Mapping). Each meditator was paired with someone from the database of the same sex and similar age. The average age gap within a pair was 1.8 years, and the groups' average ages were 51.4 for meditators and 50.4 for the comparison group, within a range of 24 to 71. Everyone was scanned on the same type of MRI scanner, and nobody had a neurological or psychiatric disorder. The paper doesn't say whether the comparison participants had ever meditated.

From each person's single structural scan, software built a 3D model of the cortex's surface. The team then measured how sharply that surface curved at thousands of points across it. Sharp ridges and deep grooves give high values, so the measure picks up both deeper folds and more frequent ones. The values were averaged over the surrounding area, so each point reflects how folded the cortex is in its neighborhood.

They ran two analyses, point by point across the cortex:

- **Group comparison:** was folding greater in meditators or in the comparison group, after accounting for sex and age?
- **Years of practice:** among the meditators alone, did folding rise or fall with the number of years they had meditated, again accounting for sex and age?

Testing thousands of points at once raises the chance of false positives, and studies usually correct for these :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}. This one didn't. The authors explain why:

> Given our regional hypotheses (and since previous studies with focus on cortical gyrification do not yet exist), significance profiles were established using uncorrected thresholds at p ≤ 0.05 as well as p ≤ 0.01. This allows for a first exploratory characterization of such effects and also provides a framework against which outcomes from future analyses can be compared.

## One Big Result: Meditators' Cortex Was More Folded in Five Areas, Most of All in the Right Insula

At the looser threshold (p ≤ 0.05), the meditators' cortex was more folded in many places on both sides of the brain. These included the front of the insula on both sides, the area around the left central sulcus (the groove between the brain's movement and touch regions), the left inferior and middle temporal gyri, the left angular gyrus, and the right :term{id="fusiform_gyrus" case="lower"} and :term{id="cuneus" case="lower"}. There were also some spots where the comparison group's cortex was more folded.

At the stricter threshold (p ≤ 0.01), still uncorrected, the spots favoring the comparison group disappeared. Five areas of greater folding in meditators remained:

1. the left :term{id="precentral_gyrus" case="lower"}, which holds the primary motor cortex
2. the left anterior dorsal insula (the upper front part of the insula)
3. the right anterior dorsal insula
4. the right fusiform gyrus, part of the visual system that recognizes what we see
5. the right cuneus, a visual area at the back of the brain

The strongest difference anywhere was in the right anterior dorsal insula.

The years-of-practice analysis pointed to the same place. At the looser threshold, folding rose with years of practice across much of the left and right outer surfaces, while on the inner surface of the right hemisphere it mostly fell. At the stricter threshold, almost all of the negative links disappeared and only a few positive ones remained, the strongest in the right anterior dorsal insula, at much the same spot as the largest group difference. A cluster in the right posterior temporal lobe also remained. The authors note that no other area showed both a group difference and a link with years of practice.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Exploratory Results, Reported Without Numbers

The paper presents its results only as colored maps. It doesn't give the size of any difference or correlation, or how large the clusters were. Without a correction for multiple comparisons, some of the spots may be chance findings, and the authors present the study as a first, exploratory look. The abstract describes "pronounced group differences" and correlations that were "similarly pronounced" without mentioning that the thresholds were uncorrected; the methods section says so plainly.

The discussion also calls the five areas "in line with our a priori hypotheses." Not all of them were on the list of seven regions, though: the authors write that differences in the right cuneus "have not been previously reported," and the fusiform result was on the right side, while their list named the left.

### Born With It, or Made by Practice?

The authors spend much of their discussion on whether meditation could have caused these differences, and they argue both sides. About 30% of the variation in folding between people, they note, is estimated to be genetic, and the basic folding pattern is laid down early, though the degree of folding still changes during childhood and adolescence. Long-term practice might reshape the cortex through changes at the level of cells and connections, and the link with years of practice in the right insula supports that idea.

But the insula's folds are among the first to form in the developing brain, and that leads the authors to an awkward conclusion for their main finding:

> Since the folding pattern of the insula appears to be established around birth, it is likely that the observed group differences within the insula (as well as in the vicinity of the central sulcus) constitute innate cortical characteristics.

They point out that an eight-week mindfulness course [didn't change the insula's gray matter density](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/), though they caution that eight weeks is short next to these meditators' 20 years, and that the course's participants, people taking a stress reduction program for a health condition, were a selective sample. They also suggest a mix: people with a particular brain anatomy might be drawn to meditation (a form of :term{id="self_selection_bias" case="lower"}), and practice might then add further changes. In the end, they leave the question open:

> Notwithstanding, further research on links between meditation practices and cortical gyrification is necessary, and any conclusions with respect to the possible determinants of the observed group differences and correlations remain speculative.

### What a More Folded Insula Might Mean

The authors offer two ideas, both drawn from other research rather than tested here. One concerns the :term{id="dmn"}, the brain network most active during daydreaming and mind-wandering. The right front of the insula has been shown to help switch between that network and the task-focused :term[central executive network]{id="central_executive_network"}. Meditators might be better at turning mind-wandering off, the authors suggest, citing [a study in which Zen meditators' default mode network responded more briefly to words](/zen-meditation-default-mode-words/), and a differently folded insula might be one basis for that skill. The other concerns body awareness. The insula is thought to bring together signals from inside the body (:term{id="interoception" case="lower"}) with emotion and thought, and in an earlier study of people who weren't selected for meditation experience, activity and gray matter in the right anterior dorsal insula went with how accurately they could judge the timing of their own heartbeat.

For the other areas, they suggest that the motor-cortex difference might relate to attending to the body, and that the cuneus and fusiform differences fit with earlier reports linking meditation to better visual perception. The study didn't measure attention, body awareness, mind-wandering or visual perception, so none of these links can be checked against its data. The authors say as much: the idea that folding reflects traits like introspection or compassion "remains to be tested with actual behavioral data."

### The Same Meditators as a Later Study on This Site

The meditators and comparison group here match, number for number, the sample in a later study by the same team, which describes itself as a re-analysis: [Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/), which found that gray matter was lower at older ages in both groups but less so in the meditators. Both papers give 50 people per group, 28 men and 22 women in each, average ages of 51.4 and 50.4, and 4 to 46 years of practice (average 19.8), though this paper gives the upper end of the age range as 71 and the later one as 77. This paper doesn't say whether its meditators include the 22 from the [2009 study](/long-term-meditators-hippocampus-gray-matter/).

### What the Measure Can't Tell

The authors list several limits of their method. Their curvature measure can't distinguish a ridge from a groove, or a patch of cortex with many shallow folds from one with a few deep ones. That matters for interpretation: deeper, earlier-forming grooves are thought to be more strongly shaped by genes, while shallower, later-forming ones may be more open to experience. They also note that folding has been linked to intelligence in earlier work, so future studies should take IQ into account, which this one didn't.

## Final Reflection

This was a first look at a new measure. Comparing 50 long-term meditators with 50 closely matched non-meditators, the authors found the cortex more folded in meditators in five areas, the strongest in the right anterior insula, where folding also rose with years of practice. The insula has turned up repeatedly in structural studies of meditators, and a [meta-analysis of 21 such studies](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/) found it among the handful of regions where differences recurred.

The evidence here is still thin. The thresholds were not corrected for the thousands of comparisons made, the results come without numbers for their size, and a single scan of people who chose to meditate for decades can't show what came first. The authors themselves note that the insula's folding pattern seems to be set around birth, which would make the main difference more likely a trait that meditators brought with them than a result of practice. The years-of-practice link points the other way, but it comes from the same uncorrected analysis. The study is best read as the authors framed it: a map of where to look in future studies, ideally ones that follow people over time and measure what they can actually do.
