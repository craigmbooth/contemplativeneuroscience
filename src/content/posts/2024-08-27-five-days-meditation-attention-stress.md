## Background Information

By 2007, several studies had found that meditation could improve attention, but most of them tested people who had practiced for months or years. That made a clean experiment hard to run. When the training takes that long, it is difficult to randomly assign people to meditate or not, and without random assignment it is hard to rule out that the people who choose to meditate differ in other ways. The authors noted that even a strong recent study had compared groups that were "not completely randomly assigned."

Their solution was to test a method designed to work quickly. Integrative body–mind training (IBMT) was developed in China in the 1990s and draws on traditional Chinese medicine. It combines body relaxation, breathing adjustment, mental imagery and :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"}, practiced to background music, with a trained coach on hand. Unlike techniques that ask beginners to rein in their wandering thoughts, it aims for an easier state:

> The method stresses no effort to control thoughts, but instead a state of restful alertness that allows a high degree of awareness of body, breathing, and external instructions from a compact disc.

The researchers' focus was executive attention, the ability to resolve conflict between competing signals and stay on task. It relies on a frontal brain network that includes the :term[anterior cingulate]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} and the lateral prefrontal cortex, and it has been linked to how well people regulate their thoughts and emotions. The team predicted that if IBMT strengthened this network, people would also handle mood and stress better.

## What They Did

In this :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, 80 healthy undergraduates at the Dalian University of Technology (44 men, average age about 22) with no previous training experience were randomly split into two groups of 40. One group practiced IBMT for 20 minutes a day for five days, in group sessions led by a CD of instructions and music. The other group, an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"}, had the same number and length of group sessions, but its CD guided them through relaxing each part of the body, a form of relaxation training popular in the West. A coach watched the sessions over closed-circuit television and answered questions afterward.

A week before training and again right after the last session, everyone took the :term{id="attention_network_test"} (ANT). On each trial, a person presses a key to show which way a central arrow points while arrows on either side point the same way or the opposite way. Cues signal when a trial will start and where the arrow will appear. Comparing reaction times across trial types gives three scores: alerting, orienting and conflict, the last being a measure of executive attention. Everyone also rated their mood on the :term[Profile of Mood States (POMS)]{id="poms"}, and 48 of them took Raven's Progressive Matrices, a standard intelligence test designed to be fair across cultures. The paper says these tests were scored objectively by people who did not know who was in which group.

After the five days of training, a random half of each group (20 and 20) took a stress test in the afternoon, to limit the effect of the daily rhythm in :term{id="cortisol" case="lower"}. After a 5-minute rest, they spent 3 minutes counting backward aloud in steps of 47 from a four-digit number, as fast as possible. A harsh tone sounded at any mistake or delay, and they had to start again. Then each group did another 20 minutes of its own practice, IBMT or relaxation. Saliva samples taken after the rest, the arithmetic and the extra session were analyzed for cortisol, a stress hormone, and :term[secretory IgA (sIgA)]{id="secretory_iga"}, an antibody in saliva that the authors used as a marker of immune response.

## One Big Result: The Meditation Group Got Better at Resolving Conflicting Cues

Before training, the two groups did not differ on any of the three attention scores. Afterward, only the IBMT group improved its conflict score, the extra time a person needs when the flanking arrows point the opposite way to the target. A smaller conflict score means the conflict is resolved more efficiently. The difference in change between the groups was statistically significant (F(1,78) = 10.839, p < .01). Alerting and orienting did not differ between the groups after training.

That fits the authors' prediction that IBMT works on executive attention specifically. The paper reports this result as a test statistic, though, without the groups' average scores in its text. Its Figure 1 shows the scores after training, with a wide spread from person to person.

The authors drew a broad conclusion from it:

> These outcomes after only 5 days of training open a door for simple and effective investigation of meditation effects.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Mood Improved in the Meditation Group

The groups' moods did not differ before training. Afterward, the IBMT group changed more than the relaxation group on four of the six POMS scales: tension–anxiety, depression–dejection, anger–hostility and vigor–activity. Within the IBMT group, those four and fatigue all improved significantly, while the relaxation group did not change significantly on any of them. Confusion did not change. The paper's abstract lists lower fatigue among the results, but for fatigue only the within-group comparison was significant.

### A Smaller Cortisol Rise and More Secretory IgA Around a Stress Test

Before the arithmetic task, the groups did not differ in cortisol or sIgA. The task was stressful for both: cortisol and sIgA rose in both groups. The two then diverged. Across the session, cortisol climbed less in the IBMT group than in the relaxation group, and after the extra 20 minutes of practice, sIgA was significantly higher in the IBMT group. The authors summed it up: "Five days of training reduced the stress response to the mental challenge especially after an additional 20 min of practice."

The paper's abstract describes "a significant decrease in stress-related cortisol," but its Figure 3 shows that cortisol in both groups was higher after the extra session than at the start. The IBMT group's cortisol rose less, rather than falling. Two other details limit what this part of the study can show. The saliva samples were taken only after the five days of training, so there is no measure of how each group reacted to stress before training. And the clearest differences came right after a fresh 20-minute session, so they can't be separated from the effect of the session just completed.

### A Hint, but Only a Hint, on a Reasoning Test

Both groups scored higher on Raven's Matrices the second time, as people often do on a repeated test. The IBMT group's gain was significant on its own and the relaxation group's was not, but the difference between the groups fell short of significance (p = .085). The authors call the advantage "only marginally" larger than the control group's.

### Why So Fast? The Authors' Guesses

Most meditation studies of attention involve months of practice, so the authors offer three reasons IBMT might work after five days: it combines several techniques that may reinforce one another, a coach helps every session go well, and the music occupies the beginner's wandering mind. They don't know which matters most, and they single out the coach:

> The trainers could well be a part of the effective ingredient of IBMT, and their role requires additional research.

### Some Questions the Design Leaves Open

The authors acknowledge that they had tested IBMT with random assignment only in Chinese undergraduates, and that group practice at the same time each day may itself help. Participants could not be blind to which program they were doing, and their expectations weren't measured. The authors argue that belief in meditation is not high among Chinese undergraduates, and that the relaxation group's lack of improvement argues against a simple expectation effect. The first author is closely tied to the method: the paper's sources on IBMT are his books, and he recorded the instructions on its CD himself. Everything was measured right after training, so it's unknown how long the changes last. Finally, the results are reported briefly, without group averages in the text or :term{id="effect_size" case="lower" plural}, and the significance levels stated for the cortisol and sIgA results are stricter than their test statistics imply, though both remain significant by the usual standard.

## Final Reflection

This short paper made a simple but important point: meditation's effects on attention can be tested like any other treatment, by randomly assigning beginners to it or to a credible alternative and comparing them. Five days of 20-minute sessions, under two hours in all, were enough to separate the groups on executive attention and mood, with signs of a gentler stress response.

The attention result fits with later work on this site. A [three-month concentration retreat](/deconstructing-concentration-interference-control/) strengthened deliberate, controlled attention on a similar :term[flanker task]{id="flanker_test"}, and a [meta-analysis of 111 randomized trials](/mindfulness-cognitive-function-survey/) found small-to-moderate gains in executive attention after mindfulness training. Those studies suggest the effect is real but more modest than a single small trial can pin down.

Taken alone, this is one small study of 80 students, tested only right after training, with the stress measures taken only afterward, and run by a team closely tied to the method. It is best read as an early, promising demonstration that a brief, coached practice can shift attention and stress responses, one that later trials with fuller reporting and longer follow-up needed to confirm.
