## Background Information

The word :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} is used for many different things. It can mean a passing state of non-judgmental, present-moment attention, a lasting personality trait, a kind of meditation, or an eight-week course such as :term{id="mbsr"}. By 2012 there were at least eight questionnaires measuring mindfulness as a trait, and their scores often didn't agree with each other. That makes it hard to study: if researchers don't agree on what mindfulness is, they can't easily work out how it works.

David Vago and David Silbersweig, of the Functional Neuroimaging Laboratory in the psychiatry department of Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston, tried to bring order to this. Their paper is not a new study. It has no participants and no new data. It is a review that proposes a framework, called S-ART, and gathers evidence from brain imaging, psychology and Buddhist texts to support it. They summarize the core idea in the abstract:

> Mindfulness is described through systematic mental training that develops meta-awareness (self-awareness), an ability to effectively modulate one's behavior (self-regulation), and a positive relationship between self and other that transcends self-focused needs and increases prosocial characteristics (self-transcendence).

The framework puts the self at the center. It tries to join two traditions: a roughly 2,500-year-old Buddhist model of training the mind and the roughly 25-year-old clinical model that grew out of Jon Kabat-Zinn's MBSR course. The authors argue that both aim to reduce suffering, and that both trace much of it to a distorted, biased sense of self.

## What They Did

### Starting point: a self that keeps reinforcing itself

The authors begin with a model of suffering borrowed from Aaron Beck's cognitive theory of depression. People carry beliefs about themselves, their world and their future. Those beliefs bias what they notice and how they interpret it, and the biased experiences then confirm the beliefs. Over time this loop makes a fixed, often negative self-story feel solid and true. The authors compare it to "kindling": each time the pattern runs, the vulnerability grows and the self-story becomes harder to change.

A key ingredient is biased attention. Someone prone to anxiety may be drawn quickly to anything threatening (hypervigilance), steer away from it (avoidance), or find it hard to let go once caught. The authors note that this last tendency is similar to what meditators call "mental stickiness." Such biases are not only a feature of mental illness. The authors see them on a spectrum: most people have them to some degree, and even meditators with no risk of a disorder likely have subtler versions. On this view, mindfulness training is a way to notice and loosen these habits.

### Three self-systems in the brain

The framework then splits the self into three systems, each tied to brain networks. The authors say this is "a simplified parcellation," not a complete account of the self.

- **The narrative self** is the "me" we think and tell stories about, the self that wonders "Am I a nice guy?" or decides "I have a bad memory." It links the past and imagined future into a stable identity. The authors place it in midline brain regions that form part of the :term[default mode network (DMN)]{id="dmn"}, including the :term[posterior cingulate cortex (PCC)]{id="pcc"} and parts of the medial prefrontal cortex. When this network stays active during tasks that need outward attention, it is often linked with :term{id="repetitive_negative_thinking" case="lower"} and rumination.
- **The experiential self** is the sense of being the one having an experience, right now, without reflecting on it or judging it. The authors link it to the anterior :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, which they describe, drawing on earlier work, as representing a "global emotional moment," and to control regions such as the :term[dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC)]{id="dlpfc"} and the dorsal :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"}.
- **The enactive self** is the body's automatic, mostly unconscious processing of the senses, movement and :term{id="interoception" case="lower"} (signals from inside the body, such as heartbeat and digestion). It runs through the brainstem, thalamus, hypothalamus, the back of the insula and motor regions.

The narrative system and the systems that handle outward, task-focused attention tend to work in opposition: when one is active, the other tends to quiet. The authors add a fourth piece, a **fronto-parietal control network** (the network also known as the :term{id="central_executive_network" case="lower"}). They propose that it integrates the three self-systems and helps the brain shift between them.

### Three kinds of practice and six processes

The authors focus on three styles of meditation. In **focused attention** practice, you hold attention on one object, such as the breath, and bring it back when it wanders. In **open monitoring**, you notice whatever arises (sights, sounds, sensations, thoughts) without settling on any one object. **Ethical enhancement** practices, such as :term{id="loving_kindness" case="lower"} and compassion meditation, deliberately cultivate warmth toward oneself and others. Traditional loving-kindness practice moves from oneself to a good friend, a neutral person, a difficult person, all four equally, and gradually the entire universe.

For each style, the authors sketch a step-by-step model of what the mind does during practice. Early on, practice takes effort. With experience, the effort is predicted to fall, and awareness itself becomes something the practitioner can notice, a skill called :term{id="meta_awareness" case="lower"}.

From these models they draw six processes that are trained together and that, they propose, support S-ART: intention and motivation, attention regulation, emotion regulation, extinction and reconsolidation (unlearning old habits of reaction), prosociality, and non-attachment and decentering.

This is a narrative review. The authors don't say how they chose the studies they cite, don't rate their quality and don't pool their results. Most of the brain studies they draw on are small, and many compare experienced meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time. That kind of :term{id="cross_sectional_study" case="lower"} can't show that meditation caused a difference, because people who choose to meditate for years may differ in other ways. The authors declared no commercial or financial conflicts of interest.

## One Big Result: Six Trainable Processes May Loosen a Biased Sense of Self

The paper's central proposal is that mindfulness works by reducing biases in how we process the self. It does this, the authors argue, by training six processes that together build self-awareness, self-regulation and self-transcendence, and by changing how the narrative, experiential and enactive self-systems work together.

An important part of the proposal is that mindfulness doesn't simply switch off the narrative self. A few meditation studies have reported less activity in the narrative self network during tasks, and less :term{id="mind_wandering" case="lower"}, which fits [a later study from Judson Brewer's lab](/reduced-dmn-active-task/): in meditators, but not in non-meditators, default mode activity was lower during meditation than during a thinking task. But some studies have also found the narrative network more closely connected with control regions in meditators. The authors read this as better coordination: mindfulness may help the brain switch to whichever network suits the moment. They are clear that this is a hypothesis:

> The evidence suggests state-dependent activation and integration of task positive and task negative networks support reduction of self-specific biases and may contribute to a sustainable healthy mind; however, such hypotheses need to be further tested.

The strength of the evidence differs a lot from one process to the next.

### Intention and motivation

Why you practice matters. The authors propose that as practice advances, motivation becomes more internally driven and less focused on outcomes, and that wanting to engage with experience "without craving or aversion" supports self-regulation. They link motivation to prefrontal regions and to reward circuits such as the ventral striatum. But they say there "has yet to be extensive investigation" of motivation in meditation research. Among the few direct findings they cite is a brain-scanning (PET) study that found more dopamine released in the ventral striatum during Yoga Nidra meditation, along with a reported "decrease desire for action."

### Attention regulation

This is where the evidence is fullest. Attention is broken into parts: alerting, orienting toward a target, staying on it, noticing distraction, letting go and coming back. Studies using the :term{id="attention_network_test"} found differences between experienced meditators and controls, and changes after MBSR and after retreats. [Five days of integrative body–mind training](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/) (IBMT) improved executive attention. A three-month retreat [sharpened perceptual discrimination and vigilance](/shamatha-retreat-perception-vigilance/), and another three-month retreat [shrank the attentional blink](/meditation-retreat-attentional-blink/) while participants' brains spent fewer resources on the first of two targets.

The authors read these as signs that skilled attention becomes less effortful. In one study, expert meditators with 10,000 to 24,000 hours of practice showed more activity than novices in the left dlPFC during focused meditation, while those with 37,000 to 52,000 hours showed less than either group. The results aren't uniform, though. More meditation experience went with less interference on the Stroop color-word task in one study, but another found no such effect after an eight-week MBSR course.

### Emotion regulation

The authors propose that beginners rely on deliberate strategies, such as rethinking a situation, while advanced practitioners rely more on simply noticing and labeling feelings as they come and go. Labeling emotions has been shown to reduce activity in the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}. In one study of experienced Zen meditators, lower pain sensitivity went with weaker connections between brain regions for evaluating pain and those that register it, as if the meditators were taking in the sensation without the usual layer of judgment. Those with the most experience showed the largest drops in activity in evaluative and emotional regions.

The authors also predict that mindfulness helps the body handle stress, and they cite studies of meditators' physiology, including [an MBSR trial in which participants made more antibodies to a flu vaccine](/brain-and-immune-function/). But they add that meditation's effects on the body's automatic regulation are "an aspect of self-regulation that needs further investigation."

### Extinction and reconsolidation

This is one of the framework's more original ideas. In :term[extinction]{id="extinction"}, a learned reaction fades when its trigger stops predicting what it used to. It doesn't erase the old association; it lays down new learning that competes with it. :term[Memory reconsolidation]{id="memory_reconsolidation"} is the window in which a recalled memory can be changed before it is stored again. The authors propose that mindfulness uses this same machinery to rewrite habitual reactions about the self, through repeated, calm, nonreactive exposure to whatever arises.

The evidence is indirect, and the authors say "relatively few studies have explored this possibility." It rests mainly on structural changes in brain regions involved in extinction, such as the :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex. Some of these come from :term[cross-sectional studies]{id="cross_sectional_study"} of meditators. Others come from small before-and-after studies, such as [one that found denser gray matter in the hippocampus after an eight-week course](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/) (a later, much larger trial did not replicate it) and [one in which people whose stress fell most showed the largest drops in amygdala gray matter density](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/). The authors also say "the dosage and quality of meditation time required for such change remains unclear."

### Prosociality

Self-transcendence, in this framework, means a relationship with others that goes beyond self-focused needs. The authors propose that training care for others may soften the line between self and other. They point to brain regions for understanding others' minds (:term{id="theory_of_mind" case="lower"}), such as the :term[temporoparietal junction (TPJ)]{id="tpj"}, and to regions for sharing others' feelings, such as the anterior insula. In one study they describe, expert meditators generating compassion showed a stronger response in the right anterior insula to sounds of human distress, compared with other sounds, than novices did.

Later studies on this site take up the same questions. In [a 2013 randomized trial](/compassion-alters-altruism/), two weeks of compassion training made people more generous to a stranger. And [a small EEG study of loving-kindness practitioners](/loving-kindness-self-bias/) looked for a smaller brain bias toward one's own face, a possible sign of a softer line between self and other, and found only hints of one.

### Non-attachment and decentering

Decentering means stepping back from your thoughts and seeing them as passing events rather than facts about who you are. The authors' example contrasts thinking "I am worthless" with recognizing "I am having the thought that I am worthless." Non-attachment, in the Buddhist sense they draw on, means letting go of mental fixations, including fixation on a solid, unchanging self.

These processes are central to the framework's account of the self, but the authors are candid that the brain evidence is missing:

> Laboratory studies of mindfulness-based meditation practices have yet to identify biological correlates for the psychological constructs of decentering and non-attachment.

They point to a few hints, such as changes in how a part of the medial prefrontal cortex connects with body-sensing regions during attention to inner sensations. Later studies have tried to look at this directly, for example by recording the brains of [experienced meditators as they set aside the sense of self](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/).

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### "Mindfulness" Originally Meant Remembering

The English word was one of the first translations of the Pali word *sati*, which literally means "memory." The authors note that Buddhist accounts tie mindfulness closely to memory as well as attention: keeping something in mind without forgetting or getting distracted. Fittingly, the framework gives memory processes, such as extinction and reconsolidation, a central role.

### Meditators Don't Seem to Feel Their Heartbeat Better

Many meditators report being more aware of their bodies, but the authors note "a paucity of good evidence" that they actually are. In one study, advanced Tibetan and Kundalini practitioners with more than 15 years of practice were no better than non-meditators at detecting their own heartbeat. The authors suggest heartbeat detection may simply not capture the kind of body awareness meditation trains, which future studies would need to test.

### The Raincoat and the Towel

The authors offer two metaphors for how mindfulness might protect against stress. The "raincoat effect" is protection: not getting soaked by a stressor in the first place. The "towel effect" is recovery: drying off quickly once you have gotten wet. In this view, smaller stress responses to harmless cues and a quicker return to baseline after real threats should show up in stress hormones and immune signaling molecules. They cite studies in which experienced meditators' skin conductance fell back faster after unpleasant stimuli or their startle responses were smaller.

### The Authors Flag What Isn't Known

The conclusion lists gaps that remain open: how the networks change over time in the same people, how much practice is needed, and whether people can even report accurately on the quality of their practice. The authors also call it "critical" to study the adverse effects of long-term practice, which they say typically go unnoticed in clinical trials but are more common on longer retreats. Later research has taken this up. [A study of people taking mindfulness courses](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/) found that over half reported at least one unpleasant effect.

### Many of the Brain Findings Are Small and Early

The paper names the dlPFC, the anterior cingulate cortex and the insula as the three most widely cited brain areas in meditation research. Much of that evidence came from small studies comparing long-term meditators with non-meditators. [A later meta-analysis of 21 such brain-structure studies](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/) found differences in a handful of regions, including the insula, but they were modest and possibly overstated.

## Final Reflection

S-ART doesn't show that mindfulness changes the self. What it offers is a map: it breaks a vague idea into three skills, lists the processes thought to train them, and ties each to brain systems that can be measured. That turns "does meditation work?" into sharper questions, such as which practice trains which process, and which brain changes go with which experiences.

Read that way, its value lies as much in what it marks as unknown as in what it claims. The attention evidence was the most developed when it was written. The ideas closest to the self, that meditation rewrites old habits of reaction and loosens identification with thoughts, were mostly hypotheses, and the authors said so. Much of the later research on meditation and the self, including several studies on this site, has been an effort to test the kind of claims this framework laid out.
