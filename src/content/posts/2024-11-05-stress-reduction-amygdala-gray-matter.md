## Background Information

The :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} is a small, almond-shaped structure deep in each side of the brain that helps detect threats and set off the body's stress response. Brain-imaging studies have repeatedly found it overactive in people with anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder and depression. Whether stress also changes the amygdala's *structure* in people was much less clear. Some studies found a larger amygdala in people with mood disorders, others found it unchanged or smaller, and one study of healthy people found no link between chronic life stress and the size of the amygdala.

Animal studies told a more consistent story. In rats, prolonged stress made nerve cells in the basolateral part of the amygdala grow longer, more branched dendrites (the branches that receive signals from other cells). Britta Hölzel, Sara Lazar and their colleagues at Massachusetts General Hospital suggested that the human studies might disagree partly because of their design. Most had compared different groups of people at a single point in time, while the animal studies followed the same animals as their stress was changed. As far as the authors knew, no study had tracked the amygdala's structure in healthy people as their stress levels changed.

So they turned the question around. Instead of adding stress, they used :term{id="mbsr"}, an 8-week course designed to reduce it, and asked whether people whose stress changed the most also showed the most change in the amygdala's :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"}.

## What They Did

The team recruited adults who reported high stress over the previous month, scoring at least one standard deviation above the population average on a short version of the :term{id="perceived_stress_scale"} (PSS). They had to be free of current psychiatric or medical illness and have no significant experience of meditation or yoga. Twenty-seven people enrolled. One was dropped from the analysis for doing less than four hours of home practice in total, leaving 26 right-handed adults (44% men, average age 36). They were paid for completing the assessments.

Everyone took MBSR, which teaches sitting meditation and yoga in weekly group classes with daily practice at home. The sample combined two similar studies. Of the 27 who enrolled, sixteen took the standard course at the Center for Mindfulness at the University of Massachusetts Medical School. Eleven took a shorter version at Massachusetts General Hospital, with 12 hours of classes instead of 23 and 20 minutes of daily home practice instead of 40. On average, participants logged just under 20 hours of home practice over the eight weeks.

Before and after the course, participants filled in the 10-item PSS, rating how often they had felt stressed from never (0) to very often (4), so that scores can range from 0 to 40. They also had an MRI scan about a week before the course and within two weeks after it.

The scans were analyzed with :term{id="voxel_based_morphometry" case="lower"}, which estimates, for every tiny cube (voxel) of the brain, how much of the tissue looks like gray matter. This is gray matter *density*, which is not the same as the volume or size of a region. For each person, the team calculated how much their stress score changed and how much the density changed at each voxel, then tested whether the two changes went together.

The main test was limited to a :term[region of interest]{id="region_of_interest"}: both amygdalae, outlined from a standard brain atlas. Because the test was still run at many voxels within that region, the result was corrected for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}. The team also searched the rest of the brain on an exploratory basis, with a much more lenient threshold.

There was no comparison group. Everyone took the course, so the study can't separate the effects of MBSR from the effects of time passing or of being scanned twice. Its question was narrower: among people taking the course, did changes in stress track changes in the amygdala?

## One Big Result: People Whose Stress Fell Most Showed the Biggest Drop in Right Amygdala Gray Matter Density

On average, perceived stress fell from 20.7 to 15.2 points (p < 0.001), a drop of about a quarter. Without a comparison group, it isn't possible to say how much of that drop came from the course. People who join a study at a stressful time may also feel better later on their own.

Within the right amygdala, the brain changes tracked the stress changes:

> Larger decreases in perceived stress were associated with larger decreases in amygdaloid gray matter density.

The cluster where this held was small, 10 voxels, in what the authors judged to be the basolateral or lateral part of the right amygdala. It passed the corrected threshold for the amygdala region of interest (p = 0.042), and it held up after adjusting for age and sex (p = 0.045). The left amygdala showed no significant link. The exploratory search of the whole brain found no other region where density changes tracked stress changes, even at the lenient threshold. Stress and amygdala density were also not linked at the first scan. The paper doesn't express the strength of the link as a single number, or say how large the density changes were, beyond plotting each participant's change.

The link ran in both directions. The authors note that amygdala density went *up* in some participants, and that many of them reported more stress after the course, not less.

Just as important is what didn't happen. Taken as a group, participants' amygdala gray matter density did not fall significantly over the eight weeks. The authors are explicit about what this means:

> Although a correlation was found between changes in amygdaloid structure and perceived stress, the present study did not show a significant overall main effect of the training on amygdaloid gray matter density. Thus, the results do not support the conclusion that MBSR training per se leads to decreases in gray matter in this region.

The abstract states that the study "demonstrates that neuroplastic changes are associated with improvements in a psychological state variable," but doesn't mention this point. The finding is a link between two changes within a small group, not evidence that MBSR shrinks the amygdala.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### More Practice Wasn't Clearly Linked to Less Stress

People who logged more home practice tended to report somewhat bigger drops in stress (r = 0.35), but the link was not statistically significant (p = 0.079). With 26 people, the study had little power to detect a modest relationship, so this neither confirms nor rules out a dose effect.

### Echoes of Animal Research

The authors point out that the basolateral region where they found their cluster is the same part of the amygdala where stressed rats' nerve cells grew longer, more branched dendrites. They suggest that easing stress might reverse something similar in people. Interestingly, in one rat study, removing the stressor did not reverse those changes within 21 days. The authors read their result as a hint that people may be able to change the amygdala's structure through how they respond to stress, even when their circumstances stay the same. That reading goes beyond the data. MRI can't see individual cells, and the authors note that finding out what changed at the cellular level would require examining brain tissue after death.

They also offered a reason for the right-sided result: some researchers think the right amygdala handles fast, automatic detection of threats, and the left a slower evaluation. If so, the course might mainly change people's first, automatic reaction to stress. The authors say this would need to be tested directly.

### Other Things That Change With Stress

When people feel less stressed, they may also sleep, eat, exercise or smoke differently, and any of these could affect the brain. The study didn't measure them:

> These variables were not assessed in the current study, and so it is unknown if the relationship between perceived stress and gray matter observed here is direct or indirect.

Stress itself was measured only by questionnaire, with no physical measures such as stress hormones. The two versions of the course, which differed in length, were analyzed together.

### How It Fits With Later Studies

The 16 participants who took the standard course seem to overlap with the same group's better-known study of gray matter after MBSR ([Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)). This paper says that an analysis of a subgroup of its participants, then under review, found changes in gray matter density in the hippocampus, inferior temporal lobe, posterior cingulate cortex, temporoparietal junction and cerebellum, and that none of these changes tracked changes in stress.

A decade later, a much larger randomized trial of 218 newcomers found that eight weeks of MBSR changed brain structure no more than a matched wellness program or a waitlist ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)). Its participants were unusually healthy volunteers with very low anxiety, so they may have had less room to improve. It did find a hint that MBSR participants who practiced more showed a larger drop in the volume of the right amygdala, but that result did not survive the team's checks with other analysis methods.

## Final Reflection

This was one of the first studies to follow the same people's brains as their stress changed, and its central result is easy to overstate. Among 26 stressed adults taking a mindfulness course, those whose stress fell most showed the largest drop in gray matter density in a small part of the right amygdala. The amygdala did not change on average, there was no comparison group, and the result rests on a single small cluster within a region chosen in advance.

What the study does suggest is that the amygdala's structure may shift along with how stressed people feel, in either direction. Whether a mindfulness course causes such changes, and whether they last, are questions this study wasn't designed to answer, and the larger trial that followed did not find structural changes from MBSR.
