## Background Information

People who have had deep meditative, mystical or psychedelic experiences often say that time stopped or that space lost its edges. Scholars of mysticism have long counted "non-spatial and non-temporal" experience among its core features, and Buddhist and Vedic writings describe the deepest states of meditation as beyond time and space. Such accounts also often mention the body: its boundaries soften or it seems to expand or fade.

These experiences are hard to study, because they can't be produced on demand in a laboratory. This study, from Bar-Ilan University and the Weizmann Institute in Israel, tried a different approach: ask long-term meditators who already know such states to bring them about deliberately, and record their brains while they do.

The authors had a specific hunch. Because altered time and space so often come with an altered sense of the body, they predicted that the brain activity behind them would show up in regions that handle bodily experience, including the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, which represents the body's internal state (:term{id="interoception" case="lower"}) and has been linked to the sense of time.

They recorded with :term{id="meg"}, which tracks brain activity millisecond by millisecond and splits it into frequency bands, and used :term{id="neurophenomenology" case="lower"}: the meditators' own descriptions of what they experienced helped guide the brain analysis.

## What They Did

The participants are the same people, recorded in the same visit, as a companion study on the sense of self, [Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/). Sixteen meditators were recruited. Two were dropped because of severe tiredness and back pain, and two because they practiced other forms of meditation, leaving 12 who practiced mindfulness meditation within the Theravada Buddhist tradition (3 women; aged 31 to 64). The companion paper describes them all as practicing forms of :term{id="vipassana"}. They had meditated for an average of 16.5 years and an estimated 11,225 hours (about 1,300 to 29,000). There was no comparison group of non-meditators.

The visit involved seven MEG recording sessions, about two hours in the scanner. This paper reports the third and fourth sessions, called "Time" and "Space." The "Self" session that followed is the one described in the companion paper. In each session the meditators, lying with eyes closed, were cued into three conditions, each held for three 30-second periods:

**Time session**

- **Then** (control): "Try to be in the near past (in the same place—the lab)"
- **Now** (baseline): "Try to be in the present moment"
- **Timelessness** (target): "Try to be outside time"

**Space session**

- **There** (control): "Try to be elsewhere (at the moment, with the experimenters outside the shielded MEG room)"
- **Here** (baseline): "Try to be here"
- **Spacelessness** (target): "Try not to be in the center of space"

The control conditions were there to rule out a simpler explanation. Trying to leave time or space might just mean remembering or imagining being somewhere else. If the target states showed the same brain pattern as recalling the recent past or imagining another room, that would be the likely explanation.

Before the sessions, everyone filled in Hood's Mysticism Scale, a questionnaire about self-transcendent experiences. Their average score on its "timelessness and spacelessness" items was 4.47 out of 5, so all of them had been through such experiences before. Afterwards, over an intercom, they rated how successful and how steady each condition had been and described their experiences. The first author sorted those descriptions into categories, and the categories were accepted when at least three of five judges agreed.

The brain analysis compared each state with its baseline: Timelessness with Now, Spacelessness with Here, and likewise for the controls. The key question was where the two target comparisons *overlapped*, since a shared pattern would point to something common to leaving both time and space. One participant who described an :term{id="out_of_body_experience" case="lower"} was set aside because that experience didn't fit the others (more on this below), so the brain results come from 11 people.

## One Big Result: Stepping Outside Time and Space Both Raised Theta Activity in Body-Related Regions

The present-moment baselines, Now and Here, didn't differ from each other in any frequency band. Compared with them, both Timelessness and Spacelessness showed changes in only one band: more :term{id="theta" case="lower"} activity (4–8 Hz). This held after correcting for the many sensors tested, though the Timelessness result was only just significant (p = 0.049; for Spacelessness, p = 0.014).

Each state had its own wide pattern. Timelessness drew mostly on the right side of the brain (88% of the active area), in regions earlier research links to timing, such as motor areas, the basal ganglia, thalamus and cerebellum, as well as the right insula. Spacelessness was spread across both sides, in regions tied to spatial processing and balance.

Where the two overlapped, theta was higher in four clusters: the :term{id="pcc"} and neighboring :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"} on both sides of the brain's midline, the back of the right superior temporal gyrus (part of the :term{id="tpj"}), and a midline part of the left :term{id="cerebellum" case="lower"} that handles balance signals. The authors argue that all four are involved in the felt sense of the body: the PCC and precuneus in representing the body, the TPJ in body-related self-processing and the first-person perspective (disruptions there are linked to out-of-body experiences), and this part of the cerebellum in balance.

The control states told a different story. Recalling the recent past and being "elsewhere" also raised theta, but their overlap fell in different places: the right posterior parietal lobe, two neighboring right frontal areas (the precentral and middle frontal gyri), and the precuneus, a network the authors link to memory and imagery. Within the precuneus, the control overlap sat higher than the target overlap, in a separate spot. The authors summarize:

> To summarize, we show that an altered experience of time and space involves theta activity in regions related to a sense of the body.

This interpretation is reasonable but indirect. The study measured theta activity, not bodily experience; the link to the body comes from what these regions are known to do in other studies. And theta rose in the control states too, so it was the location of the increase, rather than the theta itself, that set the target states apart. The insula, central to the original hunch, showed theta in both target states, but in different spots, so it didn't appear in the overlap.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### What the Meditators Described

The interviews are a rich part of the paper. Two broad themes emerged: the sense of time and space, and the sense of the body's boundaries. Many reports describe the body expanding or fading rather than time or space alone. One participant described the Spacelessness condition like this:

> I released the subject-object contrast, and then the center of space became endless, without a reference point in the middle. There was a sense of floating in a sea of being.

Others reported "a sense of spaciousness, boundlessness," feeling "something wider than the body," or losing "discrimination between different body parts." Not everyone got there. Some described no change in the sense of time or of their bodily boundaries, and a few used deliberate strategies, such as "visualizing black" or "imagining that the body is un-real." The meditators rated both target states as significantly less successful than being here and now.

### Deeper Experiences, Different Body-Region Activity

Using the descriptions, the team split the 11 meditators into two groups: 5 who reported changes in both their sense of time and space and their bodily boundaries, and 6 who didn't. The groups didn't differ in age, years of practice or Mysticism Scale scores, and their success ratings were similar. Yet their brains differed: the group with the deeper changes had lower theta in the right TPJ cluster and higher theta in the cerebellum cluster.

The team then tested the insula on its own, as an anatomically defined :term{id="region_of_interest" case="lower"}. After excluding two people with unusually high values, the deeper-change group (4 people) had lower insula theta than the others (5 people). These comparisons involve very small groups and are best read as leads for future studies.

The authors note that the ratings and the descriptions didn't line up: some of the most experienced meditators rated their success lower than less experienced ones did. They suggest this reflects differences in self-criticism and expectations, and conclude that verbal reports are needed alongside numerical ratings.

### The Out-of-Body Exception

One meditator had a different experience altogether: "The body stayed in bed but I saw myself from the corner of the room." The body didn't dissolve or expand; this meditator felt located outside it. Because this didn't fit the scale running from ordinary to dissolved bodily boundaries, the authors set it aside. That person's theta in the PCC and cerebellum clusters was much lower than the two groups' averages, a pattern the authors find suggestive but which comes from a single case.

### Two Meditators Aimed for Something Deeper

Two of the three people who rated their Timelessness success at zero came from the Burmese Mahasi school, whose map of insight stages culminates in "cuts" in consciousness called "fruition." They had tried to reach that state in 30-second windows and failed. Once the researchers explained they weren't expecting fruition, these two rated Spacelessness much higher (6 and 8).

### A Note on the Mysticism Scores

The meditators' average Mysticism Scale score (4.30) and timelessness-and-spacelessness score (4.47) were well above those of 191 religious participants in an earlier study (3.58 and 3.42). The authors read this as evidence that meditation practice raises the frequency of such experiences. Comparing this small, hand-picked group with a different study's sample can't show that practice caused the difference. People drawn to long-term meditation may differ in many ways.

## Final Reflection

This study took on states that neuroscience had barely studied, and did it with care: control conditions for memory and imagination, detailed interviews and a rare group of practitioners who could produce the states on cue. Its main finding is that two different instructions, to leave time and to leave space, converged on more theta activity in a shared set of regions, which the authors argue are tied to the felt sense of the body.

The evidence is limited, and the authors say so plainly:

> First, sample size was rather small after exclusion of five of the participants. The 11 remaining participants were further divided into two sub-groups, resulting in very small group sizes. Second, the study design was fixed, as opposed to counterbalanced, between the Time and Space sessions.

The fixed order went further than the two sessions. The paper's protocol figure shows the conditions in the same order in both sessions, with the "outside" condition always last. Because theta with closed eyes also accompanies deep relaxation and drowsiness, as the authors note, that order makes it harder to rule out that simply coming later in each session played a part. There was no group of non-meditators, the states were held for about a minute and a half each, and whether people reached them rests on their own reports, which varied widely. The authors call all of their results "preliminary." The paper's conclusion that these states "do not rely on memory and mental imagery" is also stronger than the design allows; the control conditions show only that the overlap differed from that of recalling and imagining, and some participants described imagery-based strategies.

Read alongside its companion study, where [letting go of the sense of self went with less beta activity](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/), the paper sketches a picture in which different aspects of deep meditative experience may run on different brain rhythms. It is an early map, built from a dozen people, of experiences that meditators have described for centuries.
