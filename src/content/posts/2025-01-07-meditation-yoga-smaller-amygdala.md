## Background Information

Most studies of meditation and the brain's structure are small. They compare a few dozen long-term meditators with non-meditators, or scan a few dozen beginners before and after a course. A recurring target is the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}, a pair of almond-shaped structures deep in the brain that flag threats and help set off the stress response. In one widely cited study, people whose stress fell most during a mindfulness course showed the largest drops in gray matter density in part of the right amygdala ([Hölzel et al., 2010](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/)). Another target is the :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, which is central to memory; some studies have found it larger in meditators ([Luders et al., 2009](/long-term-meditators-hippocampus-gray-matter/)).

As the authors of this study pointed out, all of this earlier work had been done either in patient groups or in small samples of healthy young volunteers, and no study had looked at a whole population. They had access to one. The Rotterdam Study is a long-running Dutch :term[cohort study]{id="cohort_study"} of 14,926 people aged 45 and over, re-examined every two to three years, and since 2005 its participants have been invited for brain scans. That made it possible to ask, in ordinary people rather than volunteers recruited for a meditation study, whether those who practice meditation or yoga differ in stress, mood and the size of the amygdala and hippocampus.

## What They Did

The researchers included everyone examined at the research center between 2008 and 2013 who had answered questions about meditation and yoga and had a brain MRI. After excluding people with stroke, dementia or Parkinson's disease and those with unusable scans, 3,742 remained. Their average age was 64, and 55% were women.

In a home interview, trained interviewers asked whether people practiced any of three kinds of activity: "looking for stillness inside oneself," meditation or mindfulness (grouped as meditation); yoga, tai chi, qigong or a similar movement therapy (grouped as yoga); or relaxation or breathing exercises. They also asked how many hours a week and how many years people had practiced, and whether the practice helped them cope with stress. Practitioners were defined as people who practiced for at least an hour a week and for at least a year. Those who practiced less were counted with the non-practitioners.

Each person had an MRI scan, and software measured the volume of the left and right amygdala and hippocampus. Stress was a single question ("how much stress do you experience," rated from 1, not at all, to 5, very much). Depressive symptoms were measured with the :term{id="ces_d"} and anxiety with the anxiety items of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A).

The comparisons of brain volume were adjusted for age, sex, education and the space inside the skull (intracranial volume), and the main regression analyses also for cardiovascular risk factors (such as blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, smoking and alcohol), depression, anxiety and stress. For a look over time, the researchers turned to the 2,397 participants who had also had a brain scan five years earlier, and counted as practitioners the 218 (9%) who had practiced for five years or more. They analyzed both scans together with a :term[mixed-effects model]{id="mixed_model" case="lower"}.

## One Big Result: Practitioners' Right Amygdala Was About 2% Smaller

Of the 3,742 people, 588 (15.7%) reported some form of practice, and 289 met the cutoff of an hour a week for at least a year. Meditation was the most common (159 people), then yoga or similar (98) and breathing exercises (75); 43 did more than one. Practitioners had kept at it for a median of 10 years. They were younger (61.9 versus 64.3 years on average), more often women (73% versus 53%) and better educated than non-practitioners.

Their brains were no larger or smaller overall (943.1 versus 940.4 milliliters). But after adjusting for age, sex, education and intracranial volume, their right amygdala averaged 1,386.8 cubic millimeters, against 1,415.8 in non-practitioners (p < .01), a difference of about 2%. The left amygdala didn't differ significantly (1,321.4 versus 1,334.8). With all the adjustments, including stress, depression and anxiety, practice was linked to a right amygdala 31.8 cubic millimeters smaller (95% :term[confidence interval]{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} 9.8 to 53.8 smaller, p = .005).

In the 2,397 people with two scans, the right amygdala showed a similar link. In the mixed-effects model with all the adjustments, practicing for five years or more was again linked to a smaller right amygdala, by an estimated 24.4 cubic millimeters (95% confidence interval 2.3 to 46.5, p = .031), a result the authors describe as "a significant interaction over time." The paper doesn't report each group's volume at each scan, so it doesn't show whether practitioners' right amygdala shrank more over the five years or was smaller at both scans. And since these practitioners had practiced for at least five years, this is not a look at people before and after they took up practice.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Practitioners Reported More Stress, Not Less

After adjusting for age, sex and education, practitioners rated their stress slightly higher than non-practitioners (2.2 versus 2.0 on the 1-to-5 scale) and had slightly more depressive symptoms (6.2 versus 5.2 on the CES-D). Anxiety didn't differ. Looking at the practices separately, depressive symptoms were higher among meditators but not among yoga or breathing practitioners, while stress was higher in the yoga and breathing groups but not among meditators. At the same time, 90.7% of practitioners said their practice helped them cope with stress (5.5% said it didn't, and 3.8% didn't know).

The authors offer two readings. One is :term[confounding by indication]{id="confounding" case="lower"}: people under stress may seek out these practices.

> Meditation and yoga practices are known for their stress-reducing intent, making it plausible that people experiencing stress make use of these strategies, and that practice could even be seen as a marker of stress.

The other is that practice changes how people notice and handle stress:

> The higher reported stress and depression levels could also mean that meditation and yoga practitioners have become more aware of their stress, but are at the same time more able to deal with it hence the smaller amygdala volume.

Neither can be tested with a single questionnaire. The authors also suggest it "seems plausible" that the smaller amygdala is due to "less experienced stress through meditation practice," but in this sample practitioners reported more stress, not less, and stress ratings weren't significantly correlated with right amygdala volume (r = −0.02, p = .227). The amygdala difference remained when stress, depression and anxiety were adjusted for, which the authors take as a sign that confounding by indication doesn't explain it.

### More Practice Didn't Mean a Smaller Amygdala

If practice shrank the amygdala, more practice might be expected to shrink it more. That's not what the study found. The amount of practice wasn't correlated with amygdala volume, depression or stress, and in the analysis over time, hours times years of practice had no significant effect on the right amygdala (p = .15). When the cutoff for counting as a practitioner was raised from one hour a week to two, the difference between the groups was no longer significant. The authors suggest this happened because people practicing one to two hours a week, who also had smaller amygdalas, were then counted with the non-practitioners, and conclude that practicing more hours a week doesn't necessarily increase the effect.

### The Left Hippocampus Was Smaller, Not Larger

Earlier studies had found larger hippocampi in meditators, but here practitioners' left hippocampus was smaller in the one-time comparison (3,891.2 versus 3,958.1 cubic millimeters; in the fully adjusted analysis, 75.3 smaller, p = .025). The right hippocampus didn't differ significantly, and neither hippocampus showed a significant link with practice in the analysis of two scans.

### Little Difference Between Types of Practice

Comparing meditation, yoga and breathing exercises separately, the right amygdala was smallest among meditators and the amygdalas were largest in the yoga group, but none of these differences between the practices was statistically significant. Relative to total brain size, the right amygdala was slightly smaller than non-practitioners' in the meditation and breathing groups. The authors attribute the right-sided result to the right amygdala's link, in earlier research, with negative emotions and immediate action.

### What the Design Can't Show

The authors list several limits. The participants were generally healthy people willing to take part in research, mostly older, and practice was recalled rather than recorded. Stress was measured with one question, and only once, so there are no data on how it changed. The study grouped meditation, yoga, tai chi, qigong and breathing exercises together, each of which comes in many styles. Physical activity wasn't adjusted for, because the authors considered it a likely step on the path from practice to the brain rather than a separate cause. Most fundamentally, people chose whether to practice, and the direction of the link can't be settled:

> It could be that people with a smaller right amygdala are naturally drawn to meditation and yoga practices.

The study also tested several brain volumes, and the paper doesn't report a correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}.

## Final Reflection

This study's strength is its size and setting. Rather than recruiting meditators, it looked at practice as it occurs in a whole community of middle-aged and older adults, with brain scans and health data that most meditation studies don't have. In that setting, the 289 people who practiced meditation, yoga or breathing exercises for at least an hour a week had a right amygdala about 2% smaller than the rest of the sample, after adjusting for many differences between them.

What it can't show is why. Practitioners reported a little more stress and depressive symptoms, more practice didn't go with a smaller amygdala, and the left hippocampus came out smaller, opposite to earlier small studies. When the question was put to a randomized test elsewhere on this site, eight weeks of MBSR [changed gray matter, including in the amygdala, no more than a matched wellness course](/mindfulness-brain-structure/), and a [meta-analysis of 21 structural studies](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/) found that the smallest studies reported the largest effects.

Taken together, this is a large observational finding of a small difference in the right amygdala of people who practice. It fits the idea that these practices are linked to the brain's threat system, but people who already differ may be the ones who take them up, and only experiments that assign practice can tell the two apart.
