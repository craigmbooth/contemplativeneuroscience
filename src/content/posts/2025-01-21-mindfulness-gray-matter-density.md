## Background Information

By the late 2000s, a handful of MRI studies had reported that experienced meditators' brains looked different from non-meditators'. They found more :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"} or a thicker cortex in various places, though most regions turned up in only one study. The :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"} and the right :term{id="insula" case="lower"} were the exceptions, each appearing in at least two.

All of those studies compared meditators and non-meditators at a single point in time. As Britta Hölzel, Sara Lazar and their colleagues at Massachusetts General Hospital and the University of Massachusetts Medical School pointed out, that design can't tell what came first:

> Studies of experienced meditators have also suggested the possibility of structural plasticity, but their cross-sectional designs did not exclude the possibility of pre-existing group differences, precluding causal conclusions.

People who are drawn to meditation for years might have had different brains to begin with. Meanwhile, other studies had shown that adult brains can change with training: gray matter increased after people learned motor skills or abstract information, or took up aerobic exercise. So the team asked whether the standard eight-week :term{id="mbsr"} course would change gray matter in newcomers, scanned before and after.

This paper was widely cited and widely covered in the press, and it did much to spread the idea that meditation "grows" the brain. It is worth reading closely, both for what it found and for how much weight it can bear.

## What They Did

The meditation group came from four MBSR courses at the Center for Mindfulness at the University of Massachusetts Medical School. These were people from across New England, referred by a doctor or by themselves, who were seeking stress reduction. To take part they had to be 25 to 55, physically and psychologically healthy by their own report, on no medication, and to have little or no meditation experience. Eighteen enrolled, but two found the first scan uncomfortable and didn't come back, leaving 16 (10 women and 6 men, average age 38). Their reward for taking part was a discount on the course fee.

The comparison group was 17 people (6 women and 11 men, average age 39) on a wait list, scanned twice about two months apart. They were not randomly assigned: the MBSR group had already signed up for a course. Age and sex were included in the analysis to adjust for the groups' different makeup.

MBSR met for two and a half hours a week for eight weeks, plus a 6.5-hour day in week six. It taught a body scan, mindful yoga and sitting meditation, and participants were asked to practice daily at home with 45-minute guided recordings and to log how long they practiced. Everyone also filled in the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire, which rates aspects of :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} such as observing experience and acting with awareness.

### What "Gray Matter Density" Means

The team analyzed the scans with :term{id="voxel_based_morphometry" case="lower"} (VBM). An MRI scan is a 3D image made of tiny cubes called voxels, a millimeter or two across in this study. Software sorts the tissue in every voxel into gray matter, white matter or fluid based on how it looks on the scan. Each person's brain is then warped to fit a standard shape, so that a given voxel sits in roughly the same place in everyone. What's left, for each voxel, is a number for how much of the tissue there looks like gray matter. That number is what the title calls gray matter *density* and the paper's text calls gray matter *concentration*. It is not the same as volume, which is how big a region is.

A rise in density does not mean the scan counted more neurons. MRI can't see individual cells, and the reading reflects whatever makes the tissue look more like gray matter. The authors are clear on this point:

> The cellular mechanisms underlying training-induced neuroanatomical plasticity are not yet understood however.

### A Two-Step Analysis

VBM runs a separate statistical test at every voxel, which raises the problem of :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}: with so many tests, some will look significant by chance. The team corrected for this and ran the analysis in two steps.

First, they looked only within the MBSR group for voxels whose density rose between the two scans. They did this in two ways. One was a focused test in regions chosen in advance, the hippocampus and the insula on both sides, because they had come up in earlier studies of meditators. The other was an exploratory search of the whole brain, which counted only clusters of at least 250 neighboring voxels.

Second, for each cluster they found, they averaged the density for each person and tested whether the MBSR group's change was bigger than the wait-list group's.

## One Big Result: Gray Matter Density Rose in Five Areas After MBSR, but Not in the Wait-List Group

In the regions chosen in advance, a small cluster in the left hippocampus became denser in the MBSR group. The wait-list group showed no change there, the two groups hadn't differed at the start, and the difference between the groups' changes was statistically significant (p = .035). The right hippocampus and the insula on both sides did not change significantly.

The whole-brain search found four more clusters that became denser in the MBSR group, and in each of them the MBSR group's change was significantly larger than the wait-list group's (p ≤ .004). No region became less dense after MBSR. The authors offered ideas about what each region might contribute, drawn from what those regions are known to do rather than from anything measured in this study:

- **Left hippocampus:** learning, memory and regulating emotion. It tends to be smaller in depression and post-traumatic stress disorder.
- **Posterior cingulate cortex (:term{id="pcc"}):** judging how relevant something is to oneself.
- **Left temporoparietal junction (:term{id="tpj"}):** the sense of one's self in one's body, and understanding other people's perspectives.
- **Left lateral cerebellum:** besides coordinating movement, helping regulate emotion and thinking.
- **Cerebellar vermis, extending into the brainstem:** an area that appears to include nuclei that release norepinephrine and serotonin, brain chemicals involved in arousal and mood.

The paper reports these results as statistical tests. It doesn't say how large the changes were in everyday terms, such as a percentage.

The results section says the findings suggest that taking the course "causes structural changes in these brain regions," and the title says mindfulness practice "leads to" increases. Those are stronger words than a small, non-randomized study can fully support, and the authors themselves list several of the reasons why.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Brain Changes Didn't Track Practice or Mindfulness

On average, the MBSR participants logged 22.6 hours of formal home practice over the eight weeks, about 27 minutes a day. About half of it was body scan; time spent on sitting meditation ranged from none at all to more than 12 hours. Among the 14 people per group with usable questionnaires, the MBSR group improved more than the wait-list group on three of the five mindfulness scales: acting with awareness, observing, and not judging inner experience.

Yet neither the amount of practice nor the gain in mindfulness scores was linked to the gray matter changes in any of the regions. The authors read this as a sign that "the MBSR program as a whole" drove the changes, rather than minutes of formal practice. They also note that the course includes much besides meditation, such as group support, stress education and gentle stretching, and that exercise is known to promote the growth of new neurons in the hippocampus. With a wait list as the only comparison, the study couldn't separate meditation from those other ingredients. They suggested that future studies use an :term[active control group]{id="active_control_group"} that shares those ingredients.

### Some Details Call for Caution

A few features of the design and results make the findings less certain than the headline suggests:

- **Selecting regions, then testing them.** The group comparisons were run only in clusters that had first been picked out because the MBSR group's density rose there. Some of any rise picked out this way can be chance, so testing the same clusters again tends to make the difference between groups look bigger than it really is.
- **A decline in the controls.** In the posterior cingulate cluster, part of the group difference came from the wait-list group's density *falling* significantly over two months, although nothing had been done to them.
- **Unequal starting points.** In the cerebellar vermis cluster, the wait-list group started with higher density than the MBSR group.
- **Different groups.** The MBSR group was mostly women and the wait-list group mostly men, the MBSR participants were people seeking help with stress, and the time between scans averaged 56 days for the MBSR group and 66 for the controls.
- **A missing prediction.** The insula, one of the two regions the team expected to change, did not. The authors suggest it may need more practice, or that the insula differences seen in earlier studies of meditators were there before they started meditating.

The authors close their list of limitations plainly:

> Finally, the current study employed a rather small sample size and replication is necessary.

### What a Later, Larger Trial Found

That replication came a decade later. A team at the University of Wisconsin randomly assigned 218 newcomers to MBSR, a matched wellness program, or a wait list ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)). Hölzel's group shared the maps of the clusters reported here, so the new study could test those exact regions. Eight weeks of MBSR changed gray matter no more than either comparison condition, whether measured as density, volume or cortical thickness, in those regions or across the whole brain. Its authors suggest one reason for the difference: participants in earlier studies like this one had sought out a course for stress, so they may have had more room to improve.

Longer and more focused training may be another matter. A nine-month study of 332 adults found that separate modules of attention, compassion and perspective-taking practice were each linked to thickening in different parts of the cortex ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)).

## Final Reflection

This study was a genuine step forward in its day. Instead of comparing meditators with non-meditators, it scanned the same people before and after training, alongside a comparison group, and the authors were candid about its limits. Those limits are substantial: 16 people in the training group, no random assignment, a wait-list comparison, and an analysis that tested regions after first selecting them for change.

Read alongside the larger randomized trial that followed, the most likely conclusion is that eight weeks of MBSR does not measurably change gray matter in healthy adults. That doesn't mean the course is without benefit, or that meditation can never change the brain's structure. It does mean that the popular claim that a few weeks of mindfulness "grows" your brain rests largely on small early studies like this one, and a larger, better-controlled trial did not confirm it.
