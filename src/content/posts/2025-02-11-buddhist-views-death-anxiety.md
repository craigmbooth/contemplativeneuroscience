## Background Information

Psychology's best-known account of how people cope with knowing they will die is :term{id="terror_management_theory"}. It holds that this knowledge could fill us with dread, and that shared cultural beliefs, which make life feel orderly and meaningful, act as a buffer against it. Reminders of death, a state researchers call :term{id="mortality_salience" case="lower"}, make people hold on to those beliefs more tightly. If beliefs are the buffer, some may work better than others, and religions differ sharply in what they say death is.

Jiaqing Qiu and Xianjun Xu, of the Department of Psychology at Tongji University in Shanghai, argue that Buddhism's view of life and death is an effective buffer. Their article is short and was published as an Opinion piece: it has no participants and no data. Although its title speaks of "effectiveness" in "regulating mortality salience," nothing was measured, and no one was given a reminder of death. What it offers is an argument, built from Buddhist teachings and terror management theory, for why these teachings should ease :term{id="death_anxiety" case="lower"}.

It is also only loosely about meditation. Its subject is a set of beliefs. Mindfulness practice comes up in a single sentence, and the authors' conclusion is that Buddhism should work through philosophy rather than religion. It is included here because the question it raises, whether Buddhist views of death and of the self change how people face dying, is the question this site's Death theme is built around, and several studies summarized here have started to test it.

## What They Did

The authors begin with terror management theory. Death, they write, is both inevitable and unpredictable: its inevitability makes people aware of their "certain end," and its unpredictability brings a sense of crisis and of being out of control. Cultural worldviews shape how people perceive death, and the authors cite studies suggesting that strong religious faith or belief in an afterlife can reduce death anxiety, and that Christians and Buddhists, who don't see death as the end, experience less of it than people who see death purely as a natural process.

They then set out three Buddhist teachings and argue that each could serve as a buffer. Besides psychology papers, they draw on classical Buddhist texts, on a Chinese-language commentary on a classic text of the Yogācāra school, and on a 2023 paper by the second author comparing the psychoanalytic unconscious with Yogācāra's *ālaya* consciousness (described below). They don't say how they chose their sources, and they don't propose a study or a way to test their claims.

## One Big Result: Three Buddhist Teachings Could Each Buffer Death Anxiety, the Authors Argue

### Life and death as a continuous chain

Buddhism teaches that living beings pass through an endless chain of lives, linked by :term{id="karma"}: the previous life is the cause of the present one, and the present life the cause of the next. Believing that death is the absolute end is, on this view, a form of "nihilism" that Buddhism rejects. The authors argue that this teaching does three things. It gives the present life lasting value, since what one does now shapes what comes later. It lets people talk about death, a subject almost every culture treats as taboo, and, citing Freud, they hold that talking about death eases anxiety repressed in the unconscious. And it lightens the pressure on this life: death is "a new beginning," and wishes left unfulfilled "can be pursued in the next." They also cite a study indicating that Asians can ease death anxiety through beliefs in fatalism and karma. They state the benefit as settled:

> This teaching significantly reduces the crisis and urgency brought by uncertainty, enhances the sense of control, and mitigates death anxiety

The sentence cites a terror management study of people's need for control, not a study of Buddhist belief, and the article itself has no data to back it.

### "No life, no death"

The second strand comes from the :term{id="four_noble_truths"}. Death is one of eight kinds of suffering. The cause of suffering is "self-attachment," attachment to a permanent and controlling self, which, the authors write, generates intense fear and reluctance as death approaches. Suffering ends when that attachment and craving for the self are eliminated, and the way there is the Eightfold Path, which includes Right Mindfulness and Right Concentration. This is the one place the article turns to practice: it notes, citing several studies and reviews, that many studies have found mindfulness practice to benefit body and mind. The state free from death anxiety, the authors write, means not identifying the body, the mind or anything else as the self, the Buddhist teaching of :term[no-self]{id="anatta"}. As support they cite a study of [12 advanced Buddhist practitioners who said they could enter near-death experiences at will](/meditation-near-death-experiences/), as having shown that overcoming attachment to the body can ease death anxiety. That study, summarized on this site, measured non-attachment and near-death experiences, not death anxiety.

The authors worry that the focus on suffering "implies the idea of escaping from real life," and offer Zen as a corrective:

> Zen advocates an outlook on real life and does not require such forms as sitting in meditation, reading scriptures, or worshiping the Buddha; one can become a Buddha in daily life.

### No life or death, only the ālaya-vijñāna

The third strand comes from :term{id="yogacara"}, which the authors say denies the reality of life and death altogether. It divides the mind into eight levels of consciousness: the five senses, ordinary thinking, a seventh consciousness (*manas*) and an eighth, the *ālaya-vijñāna*, which the authors describe as a repository storing the seeds of all phenomena, and which projects both the self and the outside world. The seventh consciousness keeps mistaking the eighth for a constant self, and the authors locate "the root of death anxiety" there. On this view, "it is not a soul that traverses around the six realms," the realms of rebirth; rather, the realms are manifestations within the ālaya-vijñāna, which continues from life to life while bodies perish.

The authors draw two comforts from this. It breaks the usual picture of life as a line running to a fixed end, offering "hope for endless rebirth." And by letting go of that linear thinking, people can focus on the present moment, which they compare to Gestalt therapy's emphasis on the here and now.

They close by setting Buddhism beside Western philosophers who tried to dissolve the fear of death: Plato's immortal soul, the Epicurean argument that we can't feel death once our senses are gone, the Stoics, Hadot and Nussbaum. Buddhism, they write, is different:

> It points out that death is not a real event but just an illusion; if people cling to the concept of self and being, then this illusion will torture them, otherwise they will be able to obtain true liberation.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Meditation Plays Almost No Part

Apart from the one sentence on mindfulness, the article treats Buddhism's effect on death anxiety as a matter of outlook. Its Zen corrective explicitly doesn't require sitting meditation, and its conclusion says that although Buddhism is primarily considered a religion, it "must exert its power not through the path of religion, but through the path of philosophical thinking and philosophical life practice." This is a claim about what believing and understanding these teachings would do, not about training the mind. A survey summarized on this site bears on exactly that distinction. [Tibetan Buddhist monastics who most firmly denied a lasting self feared its destruction the most](/no-self-fear-of-death-monastics/), and the authors of that study suggested that doctrine can remove the belief in a self while leaving the felt sense of one in place.

### Two Comforts That Pull in Different Directions

The article's strands offer different kinds of comfort, and it doesn't say how they fit together for an ordinary believer. Two of them console with continuation: death is a new beginning, and Yogācāra provides "a protective barrier of the supernatural myth of eternal life." Yet in the Four Noble Truths strand, the same cycle is part of the problem: attachment to the self, the authors write, leads "to a cycle of rebirth and new death anxiety," and liberation means escaping it. And the conclusion goes further still, calling death an illusion. The hope of going on and the absence of a self that could end are different kinds of comfort, and the article presents them side by side.

### A Title That Promises More Than the Article Does

"Effectiveness" and "regulating mortality salience" are the language of experiments. In terror management research, mortality salience is usually created on purpose, by asking people to think about their own death, and then compared with a neutral task. This article reports no such experiment, nor any survey or trial. Readers looking for evidence that Buddhist beliefs ease death anxiety won't find it here. They will find the hypothesis stated, with three distinct versions of it.

## Final Reflection

What this article adds to the research on meditation and death is the case for a hypothesis, not evidence for it. The case is worth having: it spells out, in the vocabulary of terror management theory, how three different Buddhist teachings might buffer the fear of death, and it draws on Chinese Yogācāra sources as well as the Four Noble Truths.

The studies so far give the hypothesis a mixed reception. The one test of the no-self strand on this site, the survey of Tibetan monastics, found the opposite of what the article predicts. [Long-term Sufi practitioners, whose tradition treats death as a reunion with God](/sufi-meditators-death-reminders/), were less anxious than non-meditators after writing about their own death, which fits the idea that a worldview can soften a reminder of death, though anxiety was measured only afterwards and the groups chose themselves. [Students who rated themselves as more mindful reacted less defensively to reminders of death](/trait-mindfulness-death-reminders/), but mindfulness there was a questionnaire score, not Buddhist belief or practice. And in [the only randomized trial](/fear-of-death-mindfulness-contemplation/), six weeks of daily mindfulness or reflection, neither of which mentioned death, went with less fear of one's own dying but not of one's own death, equally in both groups.

None of these studies measured the belief this article returns to most often, the continuity of life and death through karma and rebirth. Whether people who hold that belief react differently to reminders of death, and whether it comforts them or, as the Four Noble Truths strand suggests, brings worries of its own, is an open question.
