## Background Information

In 2005, Sara Lazar and colleagues reported that experienced Insight meditators had a thicker cortex than non-meditators in parts of the brain. Over the next eight years, about 20 more MRI studies looked for differences in the physical structure of meditators' brains. They reported more :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"}, a thicker cortex or differently organized :term[white matter]{id="white_matter"}, in places scattered across the brain. One of the best known was [Hölzel and colleagues' 2011 study](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/), which linked an eight-week :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} course to denser gray matter. By 2014 these studies had been cited more than 2,200 times.

But the findings were hard to add up. The studies looked at different meditation styles, used different scanning measures and had small samples, and many differences turned up in only one study. Kieran Fox, Kalina Christoff and colleagues at the University of British Columbia, together with Peter Sedlmeier of Chemnitz University of Technology, who had led an earlier meta-analysis of meditation's psychological effects, set out to answer two questions. Is meditation linked to differences in brain structure that show up consistently across studies? And if so, how large are they?

They also flagged a third question they could only partly address: whether meditation causes any of these differences. Most of the studies compared people who already meditated a great deal with people who never had. Such studies cannot tell whether meditation changed the brain, or whether people with certain brains are more likely to take up intensive practice. The authors also noted that very few of the studies had been directly replicated, and that what the structural differences mean for how meditators think and feel was still poorly understood.

## What They Did

The team searched MEDLINE, PsycINFO and Google Scholar for every study since 2005 that measured brain structure (the size, shape and makeup of brain tissue, rather than its activity) in meditators. They left out studies of related practices such as tai chi and yoga, studies that measured mindfulness only by questionnaire, and two studies of people with Parkinson's disease or mild cognitive impairment, conditions that themselves change the brain.

Twenty-one studies qualified, covering about 300 meditators. Seventeen were :term[cross-sectional]{id="cross_sectional_study"} comparisons of long-term practitioners, with thousands of hours or several years of practice, against people who had never meditated. The other four, by the authors' count, scanned beginners before and after brief training (about 5 to 60 hours of practice) and compared them with a :term[wait-list group]{id="wait_list_control"}. The practices varied widely: Insight (:term{id="vipassana"}), :term{id="zen"}, Tibetan Buddhist, :term{id="mbsr"}, integrative body-mind training (the method in [this five-day study](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)), loving-kindness and more. So did the measures: gray matter density and volume from :term{id="voxel_based_morphometry" case="lower"}, :term{id="cortical_thickness" case="lower"}, white matter structure from :term{id="diffusion_tensor_imaging" case="lower"}, and the folding of the cortex.

The team combined the studies in three ways:

- **A pooled map of where differences were found.** Brain-imaging studies report the coordinates of the point where each difference between groups is strongest. A technique called anatomical likelihood estimation (ALE) pooled 78 such points from 14 studies and tested whether they clustered in the same places more than chance would predict.
- **A count of regions reported in at least three studies.** This let them include the seven studies that didn't report coordinates.
- **A meta-analysis of effect sizes.** For the 16 studies with enough data, they converted 132 results into :term[effect sizes]{id="effect_size"}, using Cohen's *d* (the difference between groups measured in standard deviations, where by convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large). Forty-one of these came from results that weren't statistically significant.

They then checked for :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"} by plotting each study's average effect size against its sample size, and repeated the location analyses using only results that passed strict statistical thresholds, or only those with large effects.

## One Big Result: Differences Recurred in a Few Regions, and Were Probably Modest in Size

Across the five versions of their location analysis, six regions came up in at least three, which the authors judged the most dependable findings:

- the left rostrolateral prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 10, at the very front of the brain), which the authors link to introspection and awareness of one's own thinking
- the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"} and the neighboring mid-cingulate cortex, linked to self-regulation and :term{id="emotion_regulation" case="lower"}
- the front of the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, linked to awareness of the body's internal state
- the primary and secondary somatomotor cortices, which process touch and the body's position
- the :term{id="inferior_temporal_gyrus" case="lower"}, involved in high-level vision
- the :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, important for memory and the stress response

Two white matter pathways, the corpus callosum (which links the two hemispheres) and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (which links the back of the brain with the front), were also reported in at least three studies each. The functional links above are the authors' interpretations, drawn from other research on what these regions do. The studies themselves didn't test whether the structural differences mattered for meditators' attention, emotions or behavior. The abstract also credits the pooled ALE map with eight regions, but the list it gives mixes the map's clusters with regions from the study count. The hippocampus and both white matter pathways are not among the map's clusters, and on the main map the prefrontal cluster reached only trend level (it was significant in a stricter version).

How big were the differences? Averaged across studies and weighted by sample size, the effect size was *d* = 0.77, between "medium" and "large." The authors argue that this is inflated. Brain studies usually report the strongest point within each cluster of difference, not the average across it. In the one study that reported both (44 results from Kang et al., 2013), the cluster averages were about 57% the size of the peaks. Scaling every study down by the same amount gave an adjusted average of *d* = 0.44 (the abstract gives 0.46). The abstract and conclusions call this "medium." The results section is more precise:

> With these conservative adjustments, effect sizes for morphometric neuroimaging in meditators fall between the 'small' and 'medium' effect size level

The authors note that this is similar to the average effect of meditation on attention, thinking and emotion in behavioral studies, and to the effects of many psychological and educational programs. But they caution that the conventions for "small" and "medium" come from behavioral research, and that what an effect size means for brain structure is poorly understood. Even the adjusted figure is probably too high. Most studies reported only their significant results, so differences too small to reach significance are largely missing from the average. And the 57% adjustment rests on a single study.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Small Studies Reported the Biggest Differences

If every study were published whatever it found, small studies would scatter widely around the true effect, some above and some below, while larger studies would cluster near it. That isn't what the authors saw. Very large average effects turned up only in small studies, and the larger studies mostly showed moderate ones (see the chart above). One explanation is that small studies with big, significant effects got published, while small studies with smaller effects stayed in the file drawer. Almost no study reported meditators having *less* of something than controls. The authors couldn't run a formal statistical test, because the original studies didn't report the necessary data, but their reading of the plot was blunt:

> the lack of publication of almost any negative results in morphometric neuroimaging of meditation to date suggests a fair degree of publication bias, and indicates a strong chance of there being a 'file-drawer problem' in this literature

### More Experience Didn't Mean Bigger Differences

If meditation gradually builds brain tissue, studies of more experienced practitioners should find larger differences. The authors found the opposite: average effect sizes were smaller in studies of more experienced meditators. They suggest that age may explain this. Long-term practitioners tend to be older, and gray and white matter decline with age, which could mask any gains. Another possibility is that structural change matters most while a skill is first being learned. But the analysis rested on only about 15 studies, and the authors call any firm conclusion premature.

Within studies, links between brain structure and hours of practice were weak. Of 16 such :term{id="correlation" case="lower" plural} reported in 8 studies, only 6 passed strict statistical thresholds. And 14 were tested only in regions chosen in advance or already picked out by the same study's comparison of meditators and controls, which, the authors note, makes their reliability questionable.

### Brief Training Pointed to Similar Regions, but the Evidence Was Thin

The studies of beginners found differences after brief training in seven gray matter regions, including the anterior and :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"}, insula and hippocampus, and in five white matter pathways. Long-term practitioners showed differences in most of the same places. The authors read this overlap as support for meditation being the cause. But there were too few beginner studies to apply even the three-study standard, they used wait-list groups rather than :term[active control groups]{id="active_control_group"}, and the authors' overall verdict on causation was cautious:

> Evidence for meditation practice as the causative factor in structural brain change remains tenuous, and much further work is needed before such a relationship is either established or disconfirmed.

Later work has added to that caution. Hölzel's 2011 study, one of the beginner studies here, had one of the largest average effect sizes in the analysis (*d* = 1.91, with 16 people trained). A much larger randomized trial published in 2022, with 218 participants and an active control group, [found no structural changes after eight weeks of MBSR](/mindfulness-brain-structure/).

### Body-Focused Practice and the Insula

The authors noticed that the studies reporting insula differences mostly involved practitioners whose training put heavy emphasis on body awareness, such as attention to breathing, posture and sensations in the body, as in some Vipassana traditions. Differences in the somatomotor cortices followed a similar pattern. They propose that these regions reflect body-centered practice specifically. By contrast, differences in the front of the prefrontal cortex appeared in studies of quite different traditions (Insight, Tibetan Buddhist and Brain Wave Vibration), suggesting a more general link. These are hypotheses: because most practitioners combine several kinds of meditation, the authors could not compare traditions directly.

### A Few Differences Ran the Other Way

Eleven results showed more brain structure in non-meditators than in meditators, and ten of them came from a single study. Many were in regions associated with the :term{id="dmn"}, which is active during :term[mind-wandering]{id="mind_wandering"}. That study's authors suggested this might reflect less mind-wandering in meditators, an idea Fox and colleagues call "highly speculative." They point out that most studies looked for differences in only one direction (meditators greater than controls) when using lenient thresholds, and recommend testing both.

### Few Labs, Shared Participants and Uncontrolled Lifestyles

Most of the studies came from a few research groups, and five used samples of practitioners that partly or entirely overlapped with earlier ones. What looks like independent replication may therefore partly be the same people scanned again, and these groups' practitioners may be unusual in some way. The studies typically matched controls only for age, sex and handedness, not for lifestyle differences that often come with intensive practice, such as diet, sleep, fasting or stress. And :term{id="self_selection_bias" case="lower"} is a real concern. People drawn to thousands of hours of meditation may already have differed in attention, body awareness or temperament, which would show up in their brains.

## Final Reflection

This was the first meta-analysis of brain-structure studies in meditators, and much of its value lies in its candor. It found that differences between meditators' and non-meditators' brains were not scattered at random but recurred in a handful of regions linked to body awareness, self-regulation, introspection and memory. Yet the studies behind that finding were few and small, mostly one-time comparisons, concentrated in a few labs and probably skewed by publication bias. The authors' best estimate of how large the differences are is modest, and by their own account it is likely still too high.

Whether long-term practitioners' brains differ *because* of meditation, and by how much, remains an open question. The authors call for studies that follow beginners over time with active control groups, report effect sizes and results in both directions, and check whether structural differences relate to anything practitioners can actually do. At least for eight-week courses, the [larger trial that followed](/mindfulness-brain-structure/) suggests that the dramatic early findings overstated what a short course can change.
