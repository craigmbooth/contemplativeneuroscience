## Background Information

People with :term{id="social_anxiety_disorder" case="lower"} live with an intense fear of being judged. In social situations they tend to turn their attention inward, onto critical thoughts and images of themselves, and outward, onto other people's faces for signs of disapproval. The authors, Philippe Goldin and James Gross at Stanford, explain that this pattern of attention helps keep the anxiety going, because it gets in the way of the ordinary learning that would otherwise let fear fade. Their earlier brain-imaging work had found that adults with social anxiety recruit attention- and control-related brain networks less than other people when trying to reappraise their reactions to social threats and to negative beliefs about themselves.

:term{id="mindfulness"} training seemed a natural fit. :term{id="mbsr"} (MBSR) teaches people to notice thoughts, emotions and sensations as passing events, and much of its formal practice is :term[focused attention]{id="focused_attention" case="lower"} on the breath. The authors framed this in terms of :term[emotion regulation]{id="emotion_regulation" case="lower"}, the ways people influence which emotions they have and how strongly. One family of strategies is attentional deployment: shifting where your attention goes. If MBSR works by training that skill, people should get better at calming themselves by turning their attention to the breath, while a strategy the course never teaches should not change.

Only two studies of MBSR for social anxiety existed. A randomized trial had found that MBSR and group cognitive–behavioral therapy (CBGT) improved mood, functioning and quality of life about equally, but social anxiety scores were significantly lower after CBGT. The authors' own earlier study had found less anxiety and a more positive view of the self after MBSR. Neither showed what happens in the brain when someone with social anxiety faces the kind of harsh self-beliefs at the heart of the disorder.

## What They Did

Sixteen right-handed adults (nine women, average age 35) with a primary diagnosis of generalized social anxiety disorder took part. They were diagnosed with a structured clinical interview, and some had other current conditions, such as :term[generalized anxiety disorder]{id="generalized_anxiety_disorder"} (three) or a specific phobia (three). None were taking psychiatric medication or had meditated before.

Everyone took the standard 8-week MBSR course, developed by Kabat-Zinn: a 2.5-hour class once a week in a group of eight, a half-day meditation retreat, and CDs for practice at home. On average, people attended 7.07 of the 8 classes and logged 2.26 hours of home practice a week. The course was taught by the first author, who the paper says had spent six years living and studying in Buddhist monasteries in Nepal and India and had led MBSR courses for ten years, though he was not board certified in MBSR.

Before and after the course, each person was scanned with :term{id="fmri"} while doing an emotion regulation task. On each trial, a negative self-belief appeared on the screen, such as "I am ashamed of my shyness" or "People always judge me," and they read it over and over for 12 seconds. Then, with the statement still on screen, a cue told them either to "Shift attention to the breath," attending to the sensation of breathing at the nostrils, or to "Count backward from 168," a distraction strategy that MBSR doesn't teach. After 12 more seconds, they rated how negative they felt, from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much). There were 18 statements in all, chosen by the researchers, half followed by each strategy. A simple task of counting asterisks on the screen served as a baseline.

People also filled in questionnaires on social anxiety (the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale), depression, rumination, :term[state anxiety]{id="stai" case="lower"} and self-esteem. Two of the 16, one man and one woman, declined the second scan because they found scanning distressing, so the results after the course come from 14 people.

There was no comparison group. Every result is a comparison of the same people before and after the course.

## One Big Result: Turning to the Breath Upset People Less After the Course, but Counting Backward Didn't Change

Before the course, both strategies worked. Compared with just reading the statements, attending to the breath and counting backward both lowered people's negative emotion ratings, and the two strategies worked about equally well.

After the course, one of them worked better. When people turned to the breath, their negative emotion ratings were significantly lower than before the course (t(14) = 3.25, p < .01). Their ratings after counting backward did not change (t(14) = 0.41), and neither did their ratings for simply reacting to the statements (t(14) = 1.21). The paper reports each of these as a separate before-and-after test, without a direct test of whether the breath strategy changed more than the distraction strategy did. Its text doesn't give the average ratings; they are shown only in a bar chart. And a figure caption notes that the ratings for reacting were collected after the scan, rather than during it like the other ratings.

The brain scans followed the same pattern. When people turned to the breath, compared with just reading the statement, areas that the authors link to visual attention were more active after the course than before: the :term[inferior parietal lobule]{id="inferior_parietal_lobule" case="lower"} on both sides and the left superior parietal lobule, the :term{id="cuneus" case="lower"} and :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"}, the right middle occipital gyrus, and small parts of the right :term{id="parahippocampal_gyrus" case="lower"} and the :term{id="precentral_gyrus" case="lower"}. These results came from a whole-brain analysis with a correction for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}. No area became less active, and for counting backward nothing changed at all. The authors suggest these changes may reflect alerting to a stimulus.

The people whose social anxiety scores fell the most also showed the biggest increases in three of these regions, all in the :term[occipital lobe]{id="occipital_lobe" case="lower"} at the back of the brain: the medial cuneus (r = .68, p = .03), left cuneus (r = .65, p = .04) and right middle occipital gyrus (r = .65, p = .01), among the 14 people with scans from both times. The authors offer two explanations for the extra visual activity, and the study can't tell them apart:

> First, MBSR may have helped adults with SAD be more visually engaged in (i.e., less avoidant of) negative self-beliefs. Alternatively, greater neural recruitment of attention-related brain regions may be due to more refined visualization of the movement of the breath at the nostrils, which also involves better allocation of attention to the task.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Symptoms Eased

From before the course to after it, scores fell for social anxiety, depression, rumination and state anxiety, and self-esteem rose. All five changes were statistically significant.

![Average scores on five questionnaires for the 16 patients with social anxiety disorder before MBSR and the 14 reassessed after it. After the course, social anxiety fell from 68.7 to 49.3 on the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, depression from 8.7 to 3.4 on the Beck Depression Inventory-II, rumination from 26.4 to 19.3, state anxiety from 41.5 to 29.6, and self-esteem rose from 22.7 to 27.2 on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale. There was no comparison group. Drawn from the values in Table 1 of Goldin & Gross, 2010.](/assets/article_images/mbsr-social-anxiety-negative-self-beliefs/symptom-scores.jpg)

The paper gives each change an :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} (partial eta squared, the share of variation explained): .59 for social anxiety, .84 for state anxiety, .53 for rumination, .51 for self-esteem and .27 for depression. The authors write that the changes in anxiety, depression and self-esteem were similar to the moderate effects found in a meta-analysis of 38 studies of behavioral and drug treatments for social anxiety. Without a comparison group, though, it's impossible to know how much of the improvement came from MBSR, and how much from time, from expecting to improve, or from the attention of being in a study.

### A Late Dip in the Amygdala, Before the Breath Cue

The abstract lists reduced activity in the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}, the brain's threat-detection hub, as one of the changes during the breath-focused task. That result needs a closer look. The whole-brain comparison of breath-focus with reacting found no amygdala change at all. Instead, the authors took the right amygdala region that had responded to the statements before the course and followed its activity second by second through each trial.

Before the course, the amygdala's response took about 6 seconds to start, then climbed to a peak at the end of the 12 seconds of reading the statement. After the course, its activity was significantly lower *before* the cue to turn to the breath appeared. So the drop happened while people were still just reading the statement, not while they were attending to their breath. The paper gives no test statistic for it; a figure marks it p < .05. The authors also describe a larger initial spike in amygdala activity after the course, followed by the drop. They suggest that people may have begun turning to the breath on their own, before being asked:

> This highlights the possibility that one potential outcome of MBSR is to change specific aspects of attention regulation from explicit (i.e., more effortful) to an implicit (i.e., more automatic) process.

That is an intriguing idea, but the study didn't test it, and the paper doesn't report asking people what they were doing while they read the statements.

An earlier study on this site found that after an MBSR course, [people whose stress fell the most also showed the largest drops in amygdala gray matter density](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/). That study measured the amygdala's structure rather than its activity, in stressed but healthy adults, and it too had no control group.

### What Reading the Statements Did in the Brain

Before the course, reading the negative self-beliefs (compared with counting asterisks) activated a set of midline areas that the authors link to thinking about oneself, the :term[ventromedial]{id="vmpfc"} and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and the :term[posterior cingulate]{id="pcc"}, along with the right amygdala, visual areas and memory areas. This baseline result is where the amygdala region tracked after the course came from. Among its evidence that MBSR changes how people relate to themselves, the paper cites [a study of MBSR and two modes of self-focus](/narrative-experiential-self-focus/), which found less narrative, conceptual self-focus and more experiential, sensory self-focus after the course.

### The Authors Are Clear About the Limits

The paper's limitations section begins with the most important one:

> This study is limited by the lack of a control group or active comparison clinical intervention that would provide a basis for making a stronger inference about how MBSR might modify the behavioral and neural bases of different types of emotion regulation.

The authors add that future studies should be randomized trials with at least two groups, to address "potential confounds such as practice effects and habituation to the scanner environment." Both matter here, since everyone did the same task in the same scanner twice. The authors also note that the statements were chosen by the experimenters rather than by the patients, and that the study tested only one MBSR practice, attending to the breath at the nostrils, so its results don't extend to other practices such as the :term{id="body_scan" case="lower"}.

## Final Reflection

This small study asked a precise question: if MBSR works through attention, does it improve the specific attention skill it trains? For 14 people with social anxiety disorder, the answer looked like yes. After the course, turning to the breath took more of the sting out of harsh statements about themselves than it had before, and visual and attention areas engaged more, while a strategy the course never taught, counting backward, changed neither in feeling nor in the brain. That specificity, which the authors predicted, is the study's most interesting feature, since a general improvement from time or familiarity might be expected to show up in both strategies.

But it is a single group of 14 people, scanned twice, with no comparison group and no direct test of the two strategies against each other. Symptoms improved, but the randomized trial the authors cite found group :term{id="cbt"} reduced social anxiety more than MBSR. And the amygdala result in the abstract came from a follow-up look at one region and appeared before the breath cue, not during breath-focused attention itself.

It fits with broader evidence that [mindfulness therapies help with psychiatric symptoms about as much as established treatments](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/), and with a [randomized trial in generalized anxiety disorder](/mbsr-anxiety-decentering/) in which MBSR's benefit was linked to learning to step back from one's thoughts. Read alone, it is best taken as an early, carefully reasoned pilot, one whose authors call for randomized trials comparing MBSR with another course to test what it suggests.
