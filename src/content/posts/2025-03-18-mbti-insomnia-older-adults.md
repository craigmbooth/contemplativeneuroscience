## Background Information

Sleep tends to get worse with age, and poor sleep is linked to heart disease, cognitive decline and earlier death. The standard non-drug treatment, :term{id="cbt_i"}, works well for many people, but the authors note that up to 40% don't respond to it. That has made researchers look for alternatives.

One candidate is Mindfulness-Based Therapy for Insomnia (MBTI), an eight-week group program that weaves :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} practice together with behavioral techniques borrowed from CBT-I, such as sleep restriction and stimulus control. Instead of challenging unhelpful thoughts about sleep head-on, as CBT-I does, mindfulness aims to change how people relate to those thoughts and to calm the mental arousal that keeps them awake. In an earlier pilot trial in adults with chronic insomnia, MBTI reduced symptoms more than simply tracking sleep, and by six months had outdone a general mindfulness program ([Ong et al., 2014](/mindfulness-chronic-insomnia/)). Jason C. Ong, first author of that pilot and author of the MBTI manual the instructors followed, is a co-author of this paper.

The authors wanted to address three weaknesses in earlier research on mindfulness and sleep: mindfulness programs that weren't designed for sleep, a reliance on questionnaires rather than objective sleep measures, and comparisons with no-treatment groups rather than an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"} that gives people the same time and attention.

## What They Did

The team recruited adults aged 50 to 80 in Singapore through advertisements, a meditation center, sleep clinics and word of mouth. To qualify, people needed a score of at least 5 on the :term[Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)]{id="psqi"}, a questionnaire on which higher scores mean worse sleep, plus at least one specific complaint: usually taking more than 30 minutes to fall asleep, lying awake for more than 30 minutes during the night, or sleeping less than six and a half hours. A clinical diagnosis of insomnia wasn't required. People with major neurological or psychiatric conditions, cognitive impairment or long-term use of sleep medication were excluded, and none had taken a mindfulness course before.

In this :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, 127 people (average age 61, 58% women, 95% Chinese) were randomly assigned to one of two eight-week courses, each meeting once a week for two hours in groups of 6 to 15:

- **MBTI (65 people)**, taught by certified mindfulness instructors with about 2,000 hours of personal practice on average. Each class after the first opened with a formal practice such as sitting meditation, a body scan, mindful eating or mindful movement, followed by discussion of how the week's practice applied to people's sleep. :term[Sleep hygiene]{id="sleep_hygiene"} was introduced in week 2, and two behavioral techniques from week 3: sleep restriction, which limits time in bed, and stimulus control, which strengthens the link between bed and sleep. Home practice started at 20–30 minutes a day and built up to 45.
- **SHEEP (62 people)**, a Sleep Hygiene, Education, and Exercise Program developed by psychologists at Singapore General Hospital and taught by instructors with at least a master's degree in clinical psychology. It covered sleep biology, tracking one's own sleep, and changes to habits and the bedroom, and taught diaphragmatic breathing, morning and evening stretches and :term{id="progressive_muscle_relaxation" case="lower"}. Class time and homework time were matched to MBTI's.

The two main questionnaires were the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), a seven-item scale covering daytime and nighttime insomnia symptoms, and the PSQI. For objective measures, participants wore a movement-sensing wrist device (actigraphy) for at least five nights and had one night of :term{id="polysomnography" case="lower"} at home, which records brain activity to score sleep and wake. From these the researchers took :term[sleep onset latency]{id="sleep_onset_latency"} (how long it took to fall asleep) and :term[wake after sleep onset]{id="wake_after_sleep_onset"} (WASO, total time awake after first falling asleep). That made six primary outcomes. Everything was measured before and after the course; the PSQI and a pre-sleep arousal questionnaire were also given at week 4 and, by phone, six months later.

Participants knew which course they were taking, though both were presented as credible sleep treatments. The researchers who collected data were blind to group, and sleep recordings were shuffled before scoring so that scorers didn't know whose they were or when they were made. The main analysis was by :term[intention to treat]{id="intention_to_treat"}, filling in missing data statistically so that everyone randomized was counted. To allow for testing several primary outcomes, a difference between the groups had to reach p < 0.0125 to count.

## One Big Result: Insomnia Severity Fell More With Mindfulness, but Sleep Quality Improved Equally

Of the 127 people randomized, 113 completed the course, attending 7.4 of the 8 classes on average.

On the Insomnia Severity Index, the MBTI group improved significantly more than the SHEEP group (p = 0.010). MBTI participants' average score fell from 14.9 to 10.0, a large :term[effect size]{id="effect_size"} (d = −1.27), while the SHEEP group's fell from 14.2 to 11.2 (d = −0.69). On the sleep quality questionnaire, though, the two groups moved together. Both improved by week 4 and further by the end of the course, MBTI from 11.0 to 7.3 (d = −1.19) and SHEEP from 10.9 to 7.6 (d = −1.02), and both kept their gains six months later.

Why would the groups differ on one questionnaire and not the other? The authors point out that the ISI gives more weight to how people feel and function during the day, and offer a tentative explanation:

> Given the large and equal improvements in PSQI scores between the groups, the result on the ISI suggests that MBTI participants may have been more accepting of sensations of sleepiness or fatigue on nights with poor-quality sleep.

The study didn't test this idea directly.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Objective Sleep Changed a Little, and Not Clearly More With Mindfulness

None of the four objective primary outcomes improved significantly more in the MBTI group than in the SHEEP group. Time awake at night measured by the wrist device came closest (p = 0.019), but that fell short of the stricter threshold the authors set for multiple outcomes.

Looking within each group, the MBTI group showed small improvements in three of the four measures. On the wrist device, its time awake at night fell from 75 to 65 minutes (d = −0.30) and its time to fall asleep from 23 to 17 minutes (d = −0.25). On the home sleep recording, its time awake at night fell from 95 to 80 minutes (d = −0.26). The SHEEP group's changes on these measures weren't significant. Time to fall asleep on the sleep recording didn't change in either group.

The MBTI group also started with more time awake at night than the SHEEP group on both devices. The paper's text says the groups did not differ at baseline on clinical measures, but its Table 2 lists p = 0.01 for the difference in time awake on the sleep recording (95 vs. 72 minutes).

Some objective gains were shared. On the home recording, both groups slept longer after the course (MBTI from 348 to 373 minutes, SHEEP from 344 to 376) and spent a larger share of their time in bed asleep (:term[sleep efficiency]{id="sleep_efficiency"}). On the wrist device, the MBTI group's sleep efficiency rose slightly as its time in bed fell, while total sleep time didn't change in either group.

### The Control Program Was Unusually Effective

SHEEP was not a token comparison. Its participants improved about as much on sleep quality as the MBTI group did, a bigger change than earlier studies of sleep hygiene programs have found. The authors think its extra ingredients explain why:

> A likely explanation for this difference was that SHEEP contained many active components (e.g. exercise, breathing training, and relaxation) compared with typical sleep hygiene courses.

A strong control group makes any advantage for mindfulness more convincing, but also harder to find. The authors sum up the comparison plainly: "The incremental benefit of MBTI over active control was, as such, limited to only a few outcomes, with insomnia severity being the most notable."

### Racing Thoughts at Bedtime Calmed in Both Groups

Both groups reported less mental arousal before sleep by week 4, more by the end of the course and more again at six months, with no difference between them. Physical arousal before sleep didn't fall significantly during the course in either group, but was lower at the six-month follow-up. Unhelpful beliefs about sleep also eased equally in both groups.

### Mindfulness Scores Didn't Rise, but Tracked Improvement in the MBTI Group

Neither group scored significantly higher on the :term[Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire]{id="ffmq"} after the course. The authors suggest a ceiling effect, since older adults tend to score high to begin with, and note that many mindfulness trials find no change on such questionnaires. Still, in an exploratory analysis, MBTI participants whose mindfulness scores rose the most tended to improve most in sleep quality (r = −0.59) and insomnia severity (r = −0.38), and no such link appeared in the SHEEP group. The authors take this as a hint that the two courses worked through different routes, though a :term{id="correlation" case="lower"} can't show that one change caused the other.

### No Harms Reported

No adverse events related to the courses or the study visits were reported, and nobody withdrew because of adverse effects. Satisfaction with the courses and instructors didn't differ between the groups. Of the people who dropped out of MBTI, one said it was because they were unhappy with the course content, as did two in SHEEP.

### Who Was Studied

The authors describe their sample as older and mostly Han Chinese, with much broader sleep complaints than the earlier pilot, which recruited only people with diagnosed primary insomnia. Because people were recruited through advertisements, the sample did not represent Singapore's population, and results may not apply to younger adults. Sessions weren't recorded, out of concern that recording would make participants less willing to share, so there was no independent check on how faithfully instructors followed each program. Objective sleep was measured only before and after the course, not at six months.

## Final Reflection

This is a careful trial: a sleep-specific mindfulness program, a well-matched active comparison, blinded scoring and both questionnaire and objective sleep measures. Its results are more modest than the paper's abstract suggests, which concludes that MBTI "is effective at improving subjective and objective sleep quality in older adults." Both groups improved substantially, and the paper's own discussion is more measured:

> In summary, of our six primary comparisons, only one (ISI) showed a significant time × group interaction.

That one difference matters, though. MBTI reduced insomnia severity with a large effect, by more than a demanding control program did, and the authors note that the size of that drop is comparable to what CBT-I studies report, although this trial didn't compare the two directly. They suggest MBTI could suit people who haven't responded to CBT-I or can't get it, and that it can be taught in community groups of up to 15. They also call for the trials that would settle that question: a head-to-head comparison with CBT-I, or a trial in people for whom CBT-I has failed. For older adults with everyday sleep troubles, this study suggests a structured sleep course with relaxation and exercise may improve how they rate their sleep about as much as mindfulness does, with mindfulness offering an extra edge on how much insomnia bothers them.
