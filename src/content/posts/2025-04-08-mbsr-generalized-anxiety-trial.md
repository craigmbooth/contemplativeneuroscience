## Background Information

:term{id="generalized_anxiety_disorder"} is defined by worry that is hard to control, along with symptoms such as poor sleep, muscle tension and irritability. The authors, a team at Massachusetts General Hospital led by Elizabeth Hoge, note that it affects 5.7% of people at some point in their lives, and that 30 to 60% of patients don't reach remission with standard drug or talk therapy. Some people with GAD may also not seek psychiatric care because of perceived stigma.

Mindfulness courses looked like an appealing alternative: low-cost, low-stigma and accessible. But the authors point out that most earlier studies of mindfulness for GAD were small or lacked a proper comparison group. An early study of :term[Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)]{id="mbsr"} for anxiety disorders had no comparison group at all. Another compared MBSR with a wait-list, which can't rule out the benefits of simply expecting to get better and getting attention. Others combined mindfulness with cognitive therapy, which makes it hard to tell which part did the work.

What was missing, they argue, was a comparison with an :term{id="active_control_group" case="lower"}: a course that offers the same group support, instructor attention and expectation of benefit, without the meditation. To their knowledge, this was the first :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial" case="lower"} to compare mindfulness meditation, without added cognitive therapy, against such a control in GAD. They also wanted to know whether meditation training would change how people respond to stress in the moment, so they put participants through a standard laboratory stress test before and after the course.

## What They Did

Between December 2008 and February 2011, the researchers recruited adults for a study on "Stress Reduction" through referrals and media advertisements. To take part, people had to have GAD as their main problem, confirmed in a structured diagnostic interview, and score at least 20 on the :term[Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A)]{id="hamilton_anxiety_scale"}. They were excluded if, among other things, they were in psychotherapy for GAD, had taken more than four classes of meditation, yoga or tai chi in the past two years, or had been on a psychiatric medication at a stable dose for less than 4 weeks. People on a stable dose could take part if they stayed on it throughout.

Ninety-three people were randomly assigned to one of two 8-week group courses. The analysis included the 89 who attended at least one class: 48 in MBSR and 41 in the control course. (Three people assigned to the control course dropped out before it began, and one more turned out not to be eligible.) Their average ages were 41 and 37; about half were women, most were white, and 17 were on a stable dose of an antidepressant (SSRI) or a benzodiazepine.

- **MBSR** followed the standard program, with breath awareness, the :term{id="body_scan" case="lower"} and gentle hatha yoga, plus daily home practice guided by recordings. It was trimmed to make it easier to keep up: weekly classes ran 2 hours instead of 2.5, the all-day retreat became 4 hours, and home practice was cut from 45 to 20 minutes a day. The researchers also added :term[loving-kindness (metta)]{id="loving_kindness"} practice in the first class, with a recording for home. An instructor with over 8 years of MBSR experience taught it.
- **Stress Management Education (SME)** contained no mindfulness. It was matched to MBSR minute for minute: eight 2-hour classes, 20 minutes of homework a day (listening to audiobook recordings) and a 4-hour weekend "Special Class." The lectures covered stress and its effects on the body, time management, sleep, nutrition, caffeine, exercise, and things that buffer stress, such as humor, altruism and volunteering. To match MBSR's yoga, a physical therapist led gentle strength and posture exercises for the same number of minutes, and the Special Class included stability-ball exercises, posture assessments, a short massage and a talk by a dietitian.

Both courses were audiotaped, and a fifth of the classes were checked against a list of each week's content.

### What They Measured

Anxiety was measured before the courses and at week 8, the end of treatment:

- **The main outcome**, chosen in advance, was the HAM-A, a 14-item scale that a clinician scores in an interview.
- **The secondary outcome** was the :term[Clinical Global Impression]{id="clinical_global_impression"} of Severity (CGI-S), a clinician's overall rating of how ill a patient is. At the end, clinicians also rated how much each person had improved (CGI-I).
- **Self-rated anxiety** was measured with the 21-item :term[Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)]{id="beck_anxiety_inventory"}, and sleep with the :term[Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)]{id="psqi"}.

The HAM-A and CGI were rated by independent evaluators who didn't know which course each person had taken. Participants were asked not to discuss their class with them.

Before and after the courses, participants also took the :term{id="trier_social_stress_test"} (TSST): an 8-minute speech followed by 5 minutes of mental arithmetic, in front of two strangers in white lab coats holding clipboards, introduced as "the evaluators," and a large video camera. To keep people from simply getting used to it, the second test used new evaluators, a different room and a different arithmetic task, and participants were told that their first speech had scored in the low range and that this was their chance to improve. During each test, people rated their anxiety on the :term[State Anxiety Inventory]{id="stai"}, their distress on a 0 to 100 scale (the Subjective Units of Distress Scale, SUDS), and how much they agreed with positive and negative statements about their speech, such as "I can handle everything" and "I'll probably 'bomb out' anyway."

The clinical outcomes were analyzed for everyone who attended at least one class, a modified :term[intention-to-treat]{id="intention_to_treat"} analysis. For anyone missing a final score, their last available score was carried forward. The key test was whether anxiety fell more in one group than the other.

## One Big Result: On the Main Anxiety Scale, MBSR Did No Better Than the Stress Class; on Several Others, It Did

Both groups improved on the HAM-A. Scores fell from 21.46 to 13.65 in the MBSR group and from 22.12 to 16.27 in the SME group, significant drops in both (p < 0.0001). MBSR's drop was somewhat larger, but the difference between the groups was not significant (p = 0.244). On the outcome chosen in advance as the main one, MBSR did not beat the control course.

Other measures favored MBSR:

- **Overall severity (CGI-S)**, rated by the same blinded clinicians, fell from 4.54 to 3.15 with MBSR and from 4.38 to 3.58 with SME, a significantly larger drop with MBSR (p = 0.037).
- **Response to treatment**, defined as a CGI-I rating of "much" or "very much improved," was more common after MBSR: 66% (29 of 44) against 40% (14 of 35), p = 0.025. That is a :term{id="relative_risk" case="lower"} of 1.65 (95% CI 1.04 to 2.60) and a :term{id="number_needed_to_treat" case="lower"} of 3.9, though its :term{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} is wide, running from 2.2 to 26.2. The paper doesn't say why these rates are based on 44 and 35 people rather than the 48 and 41 analyzed elsewhere.
- **Self-rated anxiety (BAI)** fell from 16.01 to 9.10 with MBSR and from 14.31 to 11.33 with SME, a significantly larger drop with MBSR (p = 0.041). BAI scores weren't available for the first 7 participants.

So the clinicians' scale of anxiety symptoms showed no difference, while their overall judgment of how ill people were, and people's own reports, did. The authors suggest the main scale may have been the wrong tool:

> One possible explanation is that the HAM-A, which heavily weighs somatic symptoms, was not sensitive to the change in psychological symptoms

As support, they point to analyses done after the fact, splitting the scales into their psychological items. On the HAM-A's psychological items, scores fell by 4.8 points with MBSR and 3.9 with SME, a difference that was not significant. On the BAI's psychological items, they fell by 3.12 and 1.51 points (p = 0.07). Both lean the way the authors suggest, but neither difference reached significance, so the explanation remains a reasonable guess rather than a finding. They also offer a second possibility: that MBSR reduced people's overall distress and impairment in ways that a count of GAD symptoms on the HAM-A didn't reflect. They compare this with pain research, which distinguishes how severe a symptom is from how distressing it is, and where mindfulness training has been linked to a drop in "pain unpleasantness."

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Stress Test Felt Less Stressful After MBSR

Both groups found the second stress test less upsetting than the first, but the MBSR group's ratings fell further:

- **State anxiety** fell from 53.9 to 40.8 with MBSR and from 52.2 to 45.2 with SME (p = 0.040 for the difference).
- **Distress (SUDS)** fell from 53.2 to 28.7 with MBSR and from 50.5 to 39.4 with SME (p = 0.025).
- **Positive self-statements** about the speech rose from 15.9 to 18.6 with MBSR but didn't rise with SME (16.9 to 16.5; p = 0.004 for the difference). Negative self-statements fell in both groups alike (p = 0.26 for the difference).

The authors read the larger gains as better coping, or "resilience," under stress. They suggest a reason rooted in the practice itself:

> it is possible that participants who learned MBSR were less likely to ruminate over negative thoughts about the speech and their performance, and treated themselves with more kindness and less self-judgment, an inherent part of the practice.

A few caveats apply. These analyses included only people who completed the stress test protocol, and they left out three people in the MBSR group who started an antidepressant or benzodiazepine during the study, because those drugs affect the stress response. (Leaving the same three out of the main clinical analyses didn't change those results.) All of these were participants' own ratings: this paper reports no physiological measures of the stress response. And since both groups' ratings fell at the second test, which was changed in several ways from the first, the comparison between the groups matters more than either group's drop.

### Sleep Improved More After MBSR, in People Not on Medication

Because the allowed psychiatric drugs affect sleep, the researchers looked at sleep only in the 61 participants who weren't taking them. Sleep quality improved in both groups, more so with MBSR: PSQI scores, where lower means better sleep, fell by 2.6 points on average with MBSR and by 1.0 with SME (p = 0.035).

### A Large Effect Within the MBSR Group, but Not a Measure of MBSR's Edge

The authors report that, on the HAM-A, the MBSR group's improvement from before to after amounted to an :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} (Cohen's d) of 1.06, in line with studies of :term[cognitive behavioral therapy]{id="cbt"}, where they cite values of 0.73 to 1.37. That figure compares the MBSR group with itself before the course. It doesn't measure MBSR's advantage over the stress class: on this scale, scores fell substantially in both groups, and the difference between them wasn't significant.

### The Conclusion Leans on the Secondary Measures

The abstract reports the null result on the HAM-A plainly. The conclusion is a little more confident:

> In conclusion, our findings suggest that mindfulness meditation training, in the form of MBSR, can reduce anxiety symptoms in patients with GAD even when compared to an active control condition, and is a treatment option worth pursuing in larger investigational trials.

That "even when compared to an active control" claim rests on the CGI ratings, the BAI and the stress test, not on the scale the trial chose in advance as its main test. The second half of the sentence, calling for larger trials, is the safer reading.

### Other Limits

The authors list several: the sample was relatively small; rates of other conditions alongside GAD were lower than in large population surveys, so the results may not apply to all people with GAD; some participants took psychiatric medication, too few to test whether it affected the results; and there was no diagnostic interview at the end of the trial, so it can't say how many people no longer met criteria for GAD. In addition, anxiety was measured only at the end of the 8 weeks, so the trial says nothing about whether the gains lasted, and the paper doesn't describe any adjustment for testing many outcomes. On the other hand, only one person in each group reported an adverse event (muscle soreness after MBSR, disrupted sleep after SME).

### The Same Trial, Looked at Again

Two years later, the same group returned to this trial to ask *how* MBSR helped. In [a smaller analysis of 38 of its participants](/mbsr-anxiety-decentering/), gains in :term{id="decentering" case="lower"}, the ability to see thoughts and feelings as passing events, statistically accounted for MBSR's larger drop in self-rated anxiety on the BAI.

### Who Ran the Study

The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01033851) and funded mainly by a grant from the U.S. National Institutes of Health's National Center for Complementary and Alternative Medicine, with extra support from the Highland Street Foundation. One author reported advisory and consulting roles and equity in several companies, and royalties on the interview guide used to administer the HAM-A. The others reported no conflicts.

## Final Reflection

This trial did what the authors said earlier studies hadn't: it compared MBSR with a course that matched it for time, group, teacher attention and even gentle movement. Against that control, the result is mixed in a way that is easy to blur. On the clinician-rated anxiety scale chosen in advance as the main test, both courses helped and MBSR's edge wasn't significant. On clinicians' overall ratings, on people's own reports of anxiety and sleep, and on how distressing a public speaking test felt, MBSR came out ahead.

That pattern is consistent with mindfulness easing how much anxiety bothers people more than the bodily symptoms a clinician's checklist picks up, which is the authors' reading. But the post-hoc checks behind it fell short of significance, and with 89 people, modest differences can show up on some measures and not others. Either way, a matched health course did a good share of the work, much as it did in [another trial on this site that pitted MBSR against a carefully matched program](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/). Larger trials with follow-up beyond the last class, as the authors themselves call for, would be needed to say how much mindfulness adds for people with GAD.
