## Background Information

By the late 2000s, mindfulness-based therapies such as mindfulness-based stress reduction (:term{id="mbsr"}) and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (:term{id="mbct"}) had become popular in clinics and psychotherapy. Reviews of them were plentiful, but most were narrative summaries that didn't put a number on how much the therapies helped. The authors of this review were blunt about it: "it could be argued that the field has become saturated with qualitative reviews."

The two reviews that had focused on anxiety and depression disagreed. One, by Baer (2003), read the evidence as suggesting mindfulness therapy may help with anxiety and mood disorders. The other, by Toneatto and Nguyen (2007), concluded that it had no reliable effect on them.

Stefan Hofmann and colleagues at Boston University set out to settle the question with numbers. They noted that some programs, MBCT in particular, were designed to prevent depression from coming back rather than to treat current symptoms (a later [pooled analysis of MBCT for relapse prevention](/mbct-depression-relapse-meta-analysis/) looks at that use). Their question was different: how much does mindfulness-based therapy reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression that people have now, and does it help most in people diagnosed with an anxiety disorder or depression?

## What They Did

The team searched three research databases (PubMed, PsycInfo and the Cochrane Library) for studies published up to April 1, 2009, and checked the reference lists of relevant papers by hand. Of 727 potentially relevant articles, 39 studies qualified. To be included, a study had to:

- test a mindfulness-based therapy, delivered in person and of roughly standard length (studies with two sessions instead of the usual eight were excluded, as were taped or internet programs);
- enroll adults aged 18 to 65 with a diagnosable psychological or medical condition;
- not combine mindfulness with :term[Acceptance and Commitment Therapy]{id="act"} or Dialectical Behavior Therapy;
- measure anxiety or depressive symptoms both before and after treatment.

Crucially, a study did **not** need a comparison group. The 39 studies included 1,140 people who received mindfulness therapy, in groups ranging from 6 to 103 people. Cancer was the most common condition (9 studies), followed by :term{id="generalized_anxiety_disorder" case="lower"} (5), depression (4), chronic fatigue syndrome, panic disorder and :term{id="fibromyalgia" case="lower"} (3 each), chronic pain and social anxiety disorder (2 each), and single studies of conditions ranging from ADHD and binge eating disorder to heart disease, stroke and traumatic brain injury. Most programs were MBSR, MBCT or close adaptations of them.

For each study, the team measured how much anxiety and depression scores changed from before to after treatment within the mindfulness group. They expressed this as an :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} called Hedges' g, a version of Cohen's d corrected for small samples, where 0.2 is conventionally called small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. Calculating a before-and-after effect size requires knowing how closely people's scores before and after are correlated, which the studies didn't report, so the authors assumed a value of 0.7, which they describe as conservative. They then pooled the studies in a :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"} using a random-effects model, which allows for real differences between studies.

They also:

- calculated between-group effect sizes for the 16 studies that had a comparison group;
- checked for :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"} with the :term{id="fail_safe_n"} and :term{id="funnel_plot" plural};
- compared results across four kinds of patient: those with anxiety disorders, depression, cancer and pain;
- tested whether the effect depended on the type of program, the year of publication, the number of sessions, or the study's quality, rated on a modified :term{id="jadad_scale"}.

## One Big Result: Symptoms Fell Moderately After Mindfulness Therapy, Most of All in People With Anxiety Disorders or Depression

Across all 39 studies, symptoms fell by a moderate amount from before to after treatment:

- **Anxiety**: g = 0.63 (95% :term{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} 0.53 to 0.73);
- **Depression**: g = 0.59 (0.51 to 0.66).

The changes were larger in people being treated for the problem being measured. In seven studies of people with anxiety disorders, anxiety fell by g = 0.97 (0.72 to 1.22). In four studies of people with depression, depressive symptoms fell by g = 0.95 (0.71 to 1.18). Both are large by the usual convention. People with anxiety disorders also improved in depressive symptoms (0.75, six studies), and people with cancer (0.64 for anxiety, 0.45 for depression) and pain conditions (0.44 and 0.51) improved by smaller but still significant amounts.

The headline numbers come with a major caveat: they measure how much people changed, not how much more they changed than they would have without mindfulness therapy. Only 16 of the 39 studies had a comparison group at all. Symptoms can ease over a few months for many reasons: natural recovery, the attention and hope that come with joining any program, and :term{id="regression_to_the_mean" case="lower"}, since people often enroll when they feel at their worst. A before-and-after design can't separate these from the effect of the therapy itself.

The authors acknowledge this, and offer a benchmark. An earlier meta-analysis co-written by Hofmann found that psychological :term[placebo]{id="placebo"} conditions in anxiety disorder trials produced a before-and-after effect of g = 0.45, so they argue that mindfulness therapy's effects are larger than what a placebo would produce. That comparison draws on different studies of different people, though, so it is a rough guide rather than a direct test.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Controlled Studies Gave Smaller, Less Certain Answers

In the 16 studies with a comparison group, eight compared mindfulness with a :term{id="wait_list_control" case="lower"}, three with treatment as usual and five with an :term{id="active_control_group" case="lower"}. Because people on wait-lists generally kept receiving their usual treatment, the authors pooled the wait-list and usual-care studies together. Against these 11 studies, mindfulness therapy reduced depressive symptoms by g = 0.41 (0.23 to 0.59) and anxiety by g = 0.33 (0.11 to 0.54) more than the comparison condition, smaller effects than the before-and-after figures.

The five studies with an active comparison compared mindfulness with a healing-arts program, education programs, a support group with relaxation training, and, in one study of social anxiety disorder, cognitive behavioral group therapy. Against these, mindfulness did better on both depression (g = 0.50) and anxiety (g = 0.81, with a wide confidence interval of 0.35 to 1.27). The paper doesn't explain why the effects came out larger against active treatments than against wait-lists, and with only five studies, the estimate is imprecise.

The fail-safe N tests suggested that only one of these four controlled results, anxiety against active treatments, was robust. The authors conclude:

> However, the effect sizes for the controlled studies are unreliable and should be considered preliminary.

### How This Fits With Later Reviews

Two later meta-analyses on this site took the opposite approach and kept only :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial" case="lower" plural}, comparing people who got mindfulness therapy with people in a comparison group. Their numbers measure something different from this review's headline figures, so they can't be compared directly.

- [Goyal and colleagues (2014)](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/) included only trials with a program matched for time and attention. Mindfulness programs improved anxiety, depression and pain slightly more than these programs, with effect sizes of 0.22 to 0.38.
- [Goldberg and colleagues (2018)](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/) pooled 142 trials in people with psychiatric disorders. Mindfulness therapies beat no treatment (d = 0.55) and other therapies (d = 0.23), and did about as well as established treatments such as cognitive behavioral therapy (:term{id="cbt"}) and antidepressants (d = −0.004).

Read together, the three reviews tell a consistent story. People's symptoms improve substantially over a course of mindfulness therapy, as this review shows. But people in comparison groups improve too, so mindfulness therapy's advantage over them is smaller than the before-and-after change, and in Goldberg's review it shrank further as the comparison got more demanding.

Hofmann and colleagues noted that conventional CBT is also quite effective for depression and anxiety disorders and called for studies that compare it directly with mindfulness therapy. Goldberg's review later pooled 28 such comparisons with evidence-based treatments and found no difference.

### Benefits Held Up at Follow-Up

Nineteen studies measured symptoms again after treatment ended, on average 27 weeks later (median 12 weeks). Compared with before treatment, anxiety was still lower by g = 0.60 (17 studies) and depression by g = 0.60 (18 studies), about the same as at the end of treatment. These are still before-and-after comparisons, without a control group. Six studies also reported :term{id="intention_to_treat" case="lower"} data, counting people who dropped out; the effects were similar or larger, but the authors caution that so few studies make these results preliminary.

### Starting Levels, Program Type and Number of Sessions

- **Starting symptom levels.** In people with medical conditions such as cancer or pain, there might be little room for improvement if they weren't very anxious or depressed to begin with. The authors checked each study's starting scores against standard clinical cut-offs. Studies whose participants started with elevated anxiety improved by g = 0.67, compared with 0.53 in studies whose participants didn't; for depression the figures were 0.53 and 0.50. The authors note the elevated groups did not improve significantly more, and suggest mindfulness therapy helps across a wide range of severity.
- **MBCT and MBSR.** MBCT studies showed larger before-and-after effects (0.85 for depression and 0.79 for anxiety) than MBSR studies (0.49 and 0.55). The authors conclude only that both are effective. The two weren't compared head to head: according to the paper's table of studies, MBCT was mostly tested in people with depression or anxiety disorders, and MBSR mostly in people with cancer, pain and other medical conditions.
- **Year, length and quality.** The effects didn't depend on the year a study was published, the number of treatment sessions, or study quality. Quality scores were low throughout, ranging from 0 to 3 out of 5, with a median of 1.

The authors read the similar improvement across disorders as a sign that mindfulness works on something many conditions share:

> This pattern of results suggests that MBT may not be diagnosis-specific, but, instead, may address processes that occur in multiple disorders by changing a range of emotional and evaluative dimensions that underlie general aspects of wellbeing.

### Checks for Publication Bias

The fail-safe N for the overall results was very large: 4,150 unpublished studies with no effect would have been needed to make the anxiety result non-significant, and 4,302 for depression. The funnel plots told a more modest story. The trim-and-fill method estimated that 7 studies were missing from the anxiety analysis and 10 from the depression analysis, and adding them lowered the estimates from 0.63 to 0.51 for anxiety and from 0.59 to 0.50 for depression. The authors concluded the before-and-after estimates were unbiased; the adjusted figures show the effects remained moderate, if a little smaller.

### The Authors Expected Less

Meta-analyses can be swayed by what their authors expect to find, so the team stated their prior view:

> At the outset of our review, we were rather critical toward the efficacy of MBT. We expressed our personal view in an earlier theoretical article (Hofmann & Asmundson, 2008) and were fully prepared to report non-significant or only small effects of MBT. We were surprised to find these effects to be rather robust and strong.

They also note that their conclusions depend on their selection criteria, which were deliberately broad. Including studies without control groups and studies of many different conditions let them use almost all of the research available, at the cost of studies that varied widely in design and quality.

## Final Reflection

This review was one of the first to put numbers on what mindfulness-based therapy does for anxiety and depression, and its headline finding is clear: across 39 studies, people's anxiety and depressive symptoms fell by a moderate amount over a course of mindfulness therapy, by a large amount in people with anxiety disorders or depression, and the gains held up for the few months most studies followed people. The authors argued that it showed the earlier conclusion that mindfulness had no reliable effect on these symptoms was premature.

The figures need careful reading, though. They describe how much people improved, not how much of that improvement was due to mindfulness. In the minority of studies with a comparison group, the effects were smaller, and the authors themselves called those results preliminary and ended by calling for randomized controlled trials to establish the therapy's efficacy. The many trials that followed, pooled by [Goyal](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/) and [Goldberg](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/), largely bore out the direction of this review's findings, with a more modest size: mindfulness therapy helps with anxiety and depression, beats doing nothing and matches established treatments, but its advantage over a comparison group is smaller than the before-and-after improvement seen here would suggest.
