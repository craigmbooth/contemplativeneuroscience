## Background Information

In the 1970s, the cardiologist Herbert Benson described what he called the :term{id="relaxation_response"}: a state of physical rest that is the opposite of the fight-or-flight stress response. In his account, many practices bring it on, from meditation, yoga and repetitive prayer to tai chi and :term{id="progressive_muscle_relaxation" case="lower"}, as long as they involve two steps: focusing on a word, sound, phrase, prayer or movement, and letting everyday thoughts go. It is marked by slower breathing and heart rate and lower oxygen use, and the paper cites studies in which practices that elicit it helped with high blood pressure, anxiety and insomnia.

What happens inside the body's cells is less clear. One way to look is to measure :term{id="gene_expression" case="lower"}. Every cell carries the same DNA, but each one uses only some of its genes at a time. When a gene is in use, the cell copies it into a messenger molecule (mRNA), and measuring mRNA shows how active the gene is at that moment. Immune cells in the blood turn genes up and down in response to hormones, infection and stress, so they are an accessible place to look for the body's response to a mental practice. None of this involves the DNA sequence itself, which doesn't change.

The team, from the Benson-Henry Institute for Mind Body Medicine at Massachusetts General Hospital and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, had earlier compared gene activity at rest between long-term practitioners and novices before and after training. This time they asked a narrower question: does gene activity in blood shift within a single 20-minute session, and does the shift depend on how long a person has practiced? As the authors put it:

> No study so far, however, has examined the acute changes in gene expression within one session of RR-eliciting practice and the impact of the length of previous practices on these immediate effects.

## What They Did

The study had three sets of measurements from two groups of healthy adults, recruited through newspaper, online and posted advertisements in the Boston area:

- **Novices (26 people).** They had no prior experience of practices that elicit the relaxation response, and were tested once before training.
- **The same novices after 8 weeks of training.** They had eight weekly one-to-one sessions with an experienced clinician, who guided them through a sequence of :term{id="diaphragmatic_breathing" case="lower"}, a :term[body scan]{id="body_scan"}, repeating a :term{id="mantra" case="lower"} and :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation, "while passively ignoring intrusive thoughts." They also listened to a 20-minute recording of the same sequence at home once a day.
- **Long-term practitioners (26 people).** They had practiced regularly for 4 to 20 years, using various kinds of meditation, yoga or repetitive prayer, and had no training as part of the study.

Each lab session took place in the morning. After 15 minutes of rest, participants listened to a 20-minute recording and then rested for 15 more minutes. Blood was drawn just before the recording, right after it, and 15 minutes later. The long-term practitioners, and the novices after training, heard a recording that guided them through the relaxation sequence. At their first visit, before training, the novices heard a 20-minute health-education recording instead, which served as the control condition:

> The healthy subjects served as their own controls since the identical temporal transcriptome analysis was performed on the novice subjects listening to a health education CD at baseline.

From each blood sample, the team isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cells, white blood cells with a single round nucleus (mainly lymphocytes and :term{id="monocyte" case="lower" plural}), and measured the activity of more than 22,000 gene transcripts on microarrays, chips that measure thousands of mRNAs at once. They also measured the nitric oxide in each person's breath at the same three times. Nitric oxide widens blood vessels and can affect immune responses, and an earlier study by the group had found preliminary evidence that it rises with relaxation practice.

The analysis came in several layers:

1. **Gene by gene.** Each gene was compared between groups at each time point, and between time points within each group. A difference counted if its p-value was below 0.01, computed from 1,000 random reshufflings of the data.
2. **Patterns.** Genes that differed were sorted by the shape of their changes. A "progressive" pattern meant a gene rose or fell step by step from novices to trained novices to long-term practitioners. A "long-term" pattern meant only the long-term practitioners stood out.
3. **Pathways.** Because many biological effects involve small, coordinated shifts in many genes, the team also used :term[gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)]{id="gene_set_enrichment_analysis"}, which tests whether the genes in a predefined set, such as one biological pathway, tend to move together. They tested 880 such pathways. A difference between groups counted if its :term[false discovery rate]{id="false_discovery_rate"} was below 25% (along with a nominal p-value below 0.05). A change across time points within a group counted at p < 0.05.
4. **Networks.** Finally, they mapped how the genes in the flagged pathways interact, using databases of known protein interactions, and picked out the 20 most connected "hub" genes in each network.

## One Big Result: Inflammation-Related Pathways Were Least Active in Long-Term Practitioners and Fell During Their Session

The clearest pattern involved genes linked to :term{id="inflammation" case="lower"}. Several pathways tied to inflammatory signaling, including those for the pro-inflammatory switches :term{id="nf_kb"} and RELA, a receptor for :term{id="tnf_alpha"} (TNF R2), the immune messenger IL-7 and the receptor CCR5, along with T-cell signaling pathways, were most active in the novices, less active after training and least active in the long-term practitioners. In the long-term practitioners, their activity also fell across the session.

A second set of pathways was lower in the long-term practitioners than in the novices at two or three of the time points, and fell significantly from before the recording to 15 minutes after it. These included many immune pathways, among them antigen processing and presentation, Toll receptor signaling, and pathways for the inflammatory messenger :term{id="il_6"} and for IL-10, as well as pathways for programmed cell death and stress responses (the "stress pathway" and p38 MAPK).

When the team mapped how the genes in the downregulated pathways interact, many of the most connected hubs were molecules that act upstream or downstream of NF-κB. The authors conclude that "reduced NF-κB activity may be associated with RR elicitation," and note that it fits other studies of mind-body programs that found less NF-κB-related gene activity in blood cells.

Long-term practitioners also showed the most change over the session in individual genes. Comparing the three time points within each group, more genes differed in the long-term practitioners than in either set of novice measurements, and more differed 15 minutes after the recording than right after it, which the authors read as a delayed effect. Trained novices had fewer consistent changes, which the authors suggest may reflect how differently people get on with the practice after only eight weeks.

These results describe the activity of genes, measured as mRNA in blood cells. The study didn't measure NF-κB activity itself, inflammatory proteins in the blood, or any sign of health. A lower-active pathway in this sense means its genes, taken together, tended to show less mRNA.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Energy Metabolism and Insulin Genes Were Higher in Practitioners, but Didn't Rise During the Session

The paper's title and abstract highlight genes linked to energy metabolism and insulin secretion. The abstract says that relaxation practice "enhanced expression" of these genes, and the network analysis picked out ATP synthase, an enzyme in the cell's :term[mitochondria]{id="mitochondrion"} that makes the energy molecule ATP, and the insulin gene (INS) as top hubs among the upregulated pathways.

The results paint a more mixed picture. The pathways for energy metabolism (including the electron transport chain) and insulin secretion were more active in the long-term practitioners than in the novices before and right after the recording, that is, already before the session began. Within the session, they drifted the other way:

> Although there was a slight downregulation in gene expression across the three time points in M, the gene expression values remained higher in the M group than the N1 and N2 groups.

Pathways linked to telomere maintenance, which helps protect the ends of chromosomes, behaved similarly: higher in the long-term practitioners than in the novices, but some of them fell within the session. Other upregulated pathways, such as the cytochrome P450 enzyme family, steroid hormones, retinol metabolism and cell adhesion, were higher in both trained novices and long-term practitioners, more so in the long-term practitioners, and most of them also changed significantly during the session, particularly in the long-term practitioners.

So the energy and insulin findings mostly describe differences between groups of people, not changes brought on by the 20-minute session. And a hub in a gene network is a gene with many known connections to the other genes in the flagged pathways. The study did not measure insulin, blood sugar, ATP production or mitochondrial function.

### How the Pathway Results Were Tested

Testing 880 pathways, across three pairs of groups, three time points and three pairs of time points, raises the problem of :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}: some results will cross a threshold by chance. The team used a false discovery rate below 25% for differences between groups. By definition, that threshold allows that up to about a quarter of the flagged differences could be false positives. The authors describe the criteria differently, writing that they "ensure that there is minimal chance of identifying false positives." For changes within a session they used p < 0.05, and the paper doesn't report a correction for those. The authors also argue that looking for changes that appear at several time points "reduces the likelihood of identifying false positives."

The text doesn't report how large any of the gene changes were, and the pathway results are given mostly as lists of names and as dots and asterisks in the figures, some of which mark results at p < 0.09. The authors themselves describe the findings as preliminary and close with a call for confirmation:

> To confirm this molecular mechanism of RR, validation of the results using secondary biochemical testing will be necessary.

### The Control Condition Changed Two Things at Once

Using the novices as their own controls means their before-training and after-training sessions differed in two ways: what they heard in the lab (health education, then the relaxation recording) and whether they had trained for eight weeks. The health-education session also always came first. The design can't separate the effect of training from the effect of the recording, or from getting used to the lab. No one heard the health-education recording after training or as a long-term practitioner, so it doesn't show how the experienced groups' genes would have behaved during an ordinary 20 minutes of sitting and listening.

The long-term practitioners are compared with the novices at a single point in time. They chose to practice for years, and could differ from the novices in age, lifestyle, health or other ways; the paper doesn't report the groups' characteristics. The numbers are also small, and they aren't stated consistently: the main text reports enrolling 26 novices, while the supplementary methods list 27 at the first visit and 26 after training.

### Nitric Oxide in the Breath Rose After Relaxation

The amount of nitric oxide in the breath rose from before to right after the recording in both the long-term practitioners (by an average of 1.93, p < 0.01) and the trained novices (by 3.24, p < 0.001). In the novices at their first visit, it rose by 1.52, which wasn't significant. Fifteen minutes later, it stayed up in the long-term practitioners but dropped back significantly in both novice measurements.

In some groups and time windows, larger rises in nitric oxide went with larger falls in a few inflammation-related pathways, including RELA, TNF R2 and IL-7. The authors read these negative :term{id="correlation" case="lower" plural} as support for the biological relevance of the pathways they identified. They are associations between two measures taken in the same people, drawn from 10 selected pathways, and don't show that one caused the other.

### A Corrected Conflict-of-Interest Statement

When the paper was published, it stated that the authors had no competing interests. In 2017, the journal published a correction. Five of the authors held or had held positions at the Benson-Henry Institute, which is paid for running relaxation and mindfulness programs, sells related books, DVDs and CDs, and holds a patent pending, filed in 2012, titled "Quantitative Genomics of the Relaxation Response." That doesn't make the findings wrong, but it is relevant context for a study that interprets its results as a mechanism for the health benefits of the institute's approach.

## Final Reflection

This was one of the first studies to track gene activity in blood minute by minute through a single session of a mind-body practice. Its most consistent finding is that inflammation-related gene pathways, especially those connected to NF-κB, were less active in people who practiced techniques that elicit the relaxation response, most of all in those with years of practice, and that in the long-term practitioners they dipped further over a 20-minute session.

It sits alongside other gene-activity studies on this site. In the [study by Kaliman and colleagues](/meditation-day-gene-expression-inflammation/), experienced meditators had lower activity in two inflammation-related genes and three genes that help control other genes after an 8-hour day of practice than non-meditators who spent the day at leisure. Another comparison found that long-term meditators had [longer telomeres and more active telomere-maintenance genes](/telomere-dynamics/) than non-meditators. Like this one, both were small and not randomized.

The authors go further, proposing that relaxation practice builds "mitochondrial resiliency" by boosting energy production and dampening NF-κB, and that this may explain its health benefits. The data here can't carry that much weight. The energy and insulin pathways differed between groups but didn't rise during the session; nothing beyond gene activity and breath nitric oxide was measured; the comparison session for the novices used a different recording; and the pathway results were judged at a false discovery rate of up to 25%. Taken on its own terms, the study is a hypothesis-generating map of which gene pathways might be worth testing, ideally in randomized trials with a matched comparison session and with measures of the proteins and health outcomes the genes are thought to affect.
