## Background Information

The :term[jhanas]{id="jhana"} are a sequence of eight states of deep concentrative absorption from the Theravada Buddhist tradition. The first four, the "form" jhanas, move from joy and bliss toward ease and equanimity. The last four, the "formless" jhanas, are harder to describe: attention becomes expansive, spontaneous thought falls away and awareness of the body fades.

This site has already covered two brain-imaging studies of jhana, each with a single meditator. A [2013 study](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/) found the brain's reward system active during the jhanas. A [2025 study](/jhana-brain-networks/) from Matthew Sacchet's lab at Harvard found that the brain's large-scale networks became less compartmentalized and more interconnected during jhana, especially in the formless states.

That 2025 study, like most connectivity research, measured :term{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"} averaged over whole runs of meditation. But the brain doesn't hold one pattern for minutes at a time. Its regions fall in and out of step with each other from moment to moment, in step with shifts in attention and arousal. This paper, from the same lab, asks what those moment-to-moment patterns look like during jhana, using a method called :term{id="dynamic_functional_connectivity" case="lower"}. The authors say it is the first such analysis of any advanced meditation.

**The same meditator, and the same scans.** The participant is the meditation teacher described in the [earlier post](/jhana-brain-networks/): a 52-year-old man with more than 25 years of practice and an estimated 20,000 hours or more. The 27 jhana runs recorded in a 7-tesla scanner over five days match too, and the paper takes its list of brain regions and its table of jhana durations from the team's earlier analysis of these recordings. So this is a fresh analysis of existing data from one person, not a new experiment or a second meditator.

## What They Did

In each run, the meditator began in "access concentration," moved through the eight jhanas in order and finished with an "afterglow" period, with eyes closed. He pressed a button at each transition up to the sixth jhana, but not between the sixth, seventh and eighth, which he said would disrupt his practice. So those three were analyzed together as "J6–8." After every run, he rated his experience on 1–10 scales, including the stability and width of his attention, how intense the jhanas were, joy and bliss, narrative thought and physical sensations.

Because an expert may slip into meditation during ordinary rest, the comparison was two tasks meant to keep his mind busy: silently recalling the events of the past two weeks, and counting backward from 10,000 by fives. Each was run twice, for eight minutes each time, and the four runs were combined.

The team tracked :term{id="fmri"} activity in 49 brain networks and regions: 33 cortical networks, 10 regions below the cortex, 5 in the brainstem, and the cerebellum. They measured how strongly each pair rose and fell together within sliding windows of 16 scans (46.4 seconds), then used a clustering algorithm to sort all the windows, from jhana and control runs alike, into a handful of recurring patterns, or "brain states." For each run they counted how much time the brain spent in each state, how long each visit lasted and how often it switched between states.

The algorithm found five states. Two were dropped because they appeared only in the 27th jhana run, which contained nothing else, and looked like noise. That left 26 runs and three states:

- **State 1, "DMN-anticorrelated."** Activity in the left-hemisphere core of the :term{id="dmn"}, around the :term{id="mpfc"}, ran *opposite* to much of the rest of the brain, as did two right-hemisphere control and salience networks. Regions below the cortex were mostly quiet, apart from the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} and the periaqueductal gray, a midbrain area.
- **State 2, "hyperconnected."** Connectivity was high almost everywhere, especially between the DMN, the attention networks, the somatomotor network (which handles body sensation and movement) and the rest of the brain. The :term{id="thalamus" case="lower"} and reward-related regions such as the nucleus accumbens were in step with the cortex, and the cerebellum with the :term{id="dorsal_attention_network" case="lower"}.
- **State 3, "sparsely connected."** Few strong connections at all.

## One Big Result: Deeper Jhanas Brought More of the DMN-Anticorrelated State and Less of the Hyperconnected One

First, jhana looked different from the control tasks. During jhana, the brain spent about 31% of the time in State 1 and 27% in State 2, compared with 19% and 16% during the control tasks. The sparsely connected State 3 took up 42% of jhana but 65% of the control runs, and each visit to it was much longer in the control runs (about 35 scans, or roughly 100 seconds, versus about 21 scans, or 60 seconds).

Within the jhanas, the two main states moved in opposite directions. The odds of being in the DMN-anticorrelated State 1 rose as the meditator moved through the jhanas and were higher in the formless jhanas than in the form jhanas. The hyperconnected State 2 did the reverse, becoming less common through the sequence and in the formless jhanas. The sparsely connected state didn't change. State 1 then fell back to a low level in the afterglow.

The rise wasn't smooth. As the figure shows, State 1 dipped in the fourth jhana before jumping in the fifth, and State 2 rebounded somewhat in J6–8. It also wasn't the pattern in every run. When the team grouped runs by their sequence of states, only 11 of the 26 showed State 1 building through the jhanas. The other 15 were dominated by the sparsely connected state throughout, with State 1 low and unchanging.

What might State 1 mean? Earlier studies have linked the DMN running opposite to other networks with focused attention and less mind-wandering. Because the anticorrelation here involved the left frontal part of the DMN, an area involved in language and thinking about others, the authors lean toward a more specific reading:

> This suggests that the frontal DMN characteristics of this brain state may reflect suppression of linguistic thought more than "focused attention" per se.

That fits the traditional description of the formless jhanas as free of spontaneous thought. But the authors also point out that the meditator's own ratings of narrative thought didn't correlate with how much time he spent in this state, so the link remains an interpretation.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Brain Switched States More Often, Not Less

Meditators describe the jhanas as increasingly still and stable, and the authors suggest that this might show up as fewer switches between brain states. They found the opposite: switches happened at about 5% of time points during jhana versus 4% during the control tasks, with no difference between form and formless jhanas. The authors compare this with anesthesia, where unconsciousness brings *fewer* switches and slower dynamics:

> ACAM-J states are clearly not inert, unconscious states.

(ACAM-J, short for "advanced concentrative absorption meditation – jhana," is the authors' term for these states.) They suggest stillness might show up in some networks and not others, which their method couldn't detect.

### The Hyperconnected State May Track Attention to the Body

The meditator said he used the breath and bodily sensations to enter the form jhanas, and that sensory objects fade in the formless ones. The hyperconnected state, with its strong somatomotor, thalamus and cerebellum connections, faded in step. Runs where he rated his attention as wider had less of it (r = −0.43). And in the formless jhanas, longer visits to this state went with stronger ratings of physical sensations (r = 0.60). The authors read State 2 as a sign of sensory processing and narrow, object-focused attention, while noting that hyperconnected brain states have also been reported with :term[psychedelic drugs]{id="psychedelic_drug"}. These correlations come from 26 runs and weren't corrected for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}, so they are best treated as leads.

### No Brain State for Bliss

The early jhanas are known for joy and bliss, and State 2 even included strong connections between reward regions and the cortex. Yet none of the three states was related to the meditator's ratings of bliss, joy or equanimity, or to his ratings of intensity, width of attention or stability in any single jhana.

> We were surprised to find no relationship between self-report ratings of these experiences and the brain states.

The authors note that the team's earlier analysis of the same scans did find activity in reward regions tracking bliss. Bliss may be tied more to activity in those regions than to their moment-to-moment connections with the rest of the brain.

### No One-to-One Map From Jhana to Brain State

In sleep research, the same kind of analysis gives each stage of sleep its own dominant brain state. The jhanas didn't work that way. Instead, the three states gradually waxed and waned across the sequence, with a good deal of variation between runs.

### Signs of Change Over the Scanning Days

Across the runs, the meditator's time in jhana grew longer (r = 0.60), and in later runs he spent less time in the hyperconnected state and more in the sparsely connected one. The authors take this as a hint of practice effects inside the scanner, which they say needs more study.

### The Averaged Picture Fits the Earlier Study

Averaged over whole runs, connectivity between cortical networks was higher during jhana than in the control tasks, while connectivity between brainstem regions (such as the locus coeruleus) and the rest of the brain was lower. That fits the [more interconnected networks](/jhana-brain-networks/) found when the same scans were analyzed for network structure. A check using all 526 brain regions instead of the 49 chosen in advance also found higher cortical connectivity and more switching during jhana.

## Final Reflection

This study adds time to the picture of jhana. Rather than one fixed "jhana brain," it describes a brain moving among a few recurring states, switching more often than during the control tasks, with the balance tilting through the sequence toward a pattern in which the frontal DMN runs against the rest of the brain, and away from a hyperconnected pattern that may reflect attention to the body.

It is still one person, moving through the states in a fixed order, compared with four short control runs during which he reported some meditative experience of his own. The rising DMN-anticorrelated state showed up clearly in fewer than half the runs, and most of the hoped-for links to experience didn't appear. The authors themselves say it is unclear whether the findings generalize to other jhana practitioners. What the study shows is that this kind of analysis can follow an expert's movement through altered states as it unfolds, which makes it a promising tool for studying more practitioners.
