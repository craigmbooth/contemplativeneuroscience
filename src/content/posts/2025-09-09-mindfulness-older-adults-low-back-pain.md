## Background Information

Back pain is one of the most common problems of old age. The paper cites a national survey of Americans 65 and older in which 52.9% reported pain, most often in the back, and those in pain had poorer physical function than those without. Treating it is hard at that age. Anti-inflammatory drugs and opioids carry side effects that older adults tolerate poorly, and with few options, doctors may turn to invasive procedures that may be "of limited benefit." More treatments that don't rely on drugs are needed.

:term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation is one candidate. A 2014 review of randomized trials found that mindfulness programs eased pain a little more than programs matched for time and attention, but mostly visceral pain, such as that of irritable bowel syndrome; for musculoskeletal pain, the benefit was not clinically significant ([Goyal et al., 2014](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/)). As the authors point out, that verdict rested on only two small studies, one of them their own pilot trial in older adults with back pain.

The team's earlier pilot studies had shown that an 8-week mindfulness program was workable for older adults and hinted that it might reduce pain and improve function. This trial was the full-size test. The team predicted that, compared with a health education course, mindfulness would bring a clinically meaningful improvement in physical function by the end of the program, with less pain and better psychological well-being, and that these gains would still be there 6 months later.

## What They Did

Between 2011 and 2014, the researchers screened 1,160 people in and around Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, and enrolled 282. To take part, people had to be 65 or older, live independently, pass a test of memory and thinking, and have had moderate back pain daily or almost daily for 3 months or more. The pain also had to limit their daily lives: a score of 11 or more out of 24 on the Roland and Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ), which asks about everyday tasks that back pain makes harder. People were excluded if they had done a mindfulness program before, if a serious illness such as cancer was causing their pain, if they had worse pain elsewhere in the body, or if they had moderate to severe symptoms of depression.

The participants averaged 74.5 years old and had lived with back pain for about 11 years on average. Two-thirds were women, and 80 (28.4%) were African American, which the authors note is unusual for mind–body studies that don't specifically set out to recruit minorities.

In this :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, 140 people were assigned to the mindfulness program and 142 to an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"}. The paper calls the mindfulness program a "mind-body program." Modeled on :term[Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)]{id="mbsr"}, it met in groups for 90 minutes once a week for 8 weeks and taught four practices: the :term{id="body_scan" case="lower"}, sitting meditation, walking meditation and mindful stretching. Six monthly 60-minute booster sessions followed, each with a meditation and a discussion.

The control group took an 8-week group course based on the 10 Keys to Healthy Aging, a curriculum on health topics for older adults, such as managing high blood pressure. It was matched to the mindfulness program for time, group size, attention, homework and instructor time, and it had its own monthly booster classes. Both groups learned the same chair stretches. The course included nothing about pain, which the researchers deliberately left out so that the control group wouldn't be getting a treatment for pain of its own.

The main outcome was the RMDQ score. On this scale, an improvement of 2.5 to 5.0 points counts as a :term[clinically meaningful change]{id="minimal_clinically_important_difference"}, and the trial was sized to detect a difference of 2.5 points between the groups. Participants also rated their current pain, their average pain over the past week and their worst pain over the past week, on a scale from 0 to 20 (rather than the more common 0 to 10). Secondary measures included quality of life, depressive symptoms, :term[pain self-efficacy]{id="self_efficacy"} (confidence in being able to manage pain), pain catastrophizing (a tendency to dwell on pain and feel helpless about it) and a questionnaire measure of everyday mindfulness. Everyone was assessed before the programs, when they ended at 8 weeks, and again 6 months after that, by staff who didn't know who was in which group. The analyses were :term[intention-to-treat]{id="intention_to_treat"}.

## One Big Result: A Small Edge in Daily Function at 8 Weeks That Was Gone by 6 Months

Both groups started with similar RMDQ scores (15.6 in the mindfulness group and 15.4 in the control group), and both improved. By the end of the 8-week programs, the mindfulness group's score had fallen by 3.5 points and the control group's by 2.3. In the trial's adjusted analysis, the mindfulness group had improved 1.1 points more (95% :term[confidence interval]{id="confidence_interval"} −2.1 to −0.01), a statistically significant but small difference (:term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} −0.23, where negative favors mindfulness). That is less than half of the 2.5-point difference the trial was designed to detect, the low end of what counts as clinically meaningful.

The difference didn't last. Six months after the programs ended, the mindfulness group had improved by 3.4 points from the start and the control group had caught up, at 2.8 points, leaving a gap of 0.4 points (−1.5 to 0.7; effect size −0.08), too small to rule out chance. (The paper's abstract gives this difference as −0.04, but its table and results section give −0.4.) The authors put it plainly:

> Learning mindfulness resulted in a clinically meaningful mean RMDQ improvement of −3.4 points, but by 6 months, the comparison group had also improved by a mean of −2.8 points.

Counting people rather than averages tells the same story. At 8 weeks, 56.8% of the mindfulness group and 44.9% of the control group had improved by at least 2.5 points, a difference that just missed statistical significance (P = .051). At 6 months the shares were almost identical, 49.2% and 48.9%.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Current Pain Was Lower in the Mindfulness Group at 6 Months

The pain results followed a different course from function: the clearest differences came later. Current pain in the mindfulness group dipped from 7.4 to 6.8 (on the 0–20 scale) and stayed there, while in the control group it rose from 7.1 to 7.9 and then 8.6. At 8 weeks the difference between the groups (−1.0 points, −2.3 to 0.2) could have been chance. By 6 months it was 1.8 points (−3.1 to −0.5), an effect size of −0.33. Part of that gap, then, came from the control group's pain getting worse.

The paper's abstract and key points say worst pain in the past week also improved significantly. That rests on the trial's overall test of whether the groups changed differently over time (P = .04). At each assessment, the difference in worst pain was small, and its confidence interval included zero (−0.4 points at 8 weeks, −1.0 at 6 months). Average pain did not differ significantly overall (P = .08), though at 6 months it was 1.1 points lower in the mindfulness group (−2.2 to −0.01).

Counting people who improved by a meaningful amount gives the pain results more weight. More people in the mindfulness group reduced their current pain by at least 30%, both at 8 weeks (40.9% vs 24.6%) and at 6 months (44.4% vs 25.2%). The same was true of worst pain (36.4% vs 21.7% at 8 weeks, 35.9% vs 22.2% at 6 months), though not of average pain. The authors treat a 30% reduction as clinically meaningful, in line with what patients themselves count as successful pain relief.

### Participants Felt Helped, but the Comparison Wasn't Even

At the end of the programs, everyone was asked: "How much have your back symptoms changed as a result of the treatment provided in this study?" In the mindfulness group, 80.3% said their back symptoms had improved at least a little, compared with 37.0% in the control group, and 40.9% said they were much or very much better, compared with 6.5%. Six months later, 76.1% and 42.2% reported at least minimal improvement.

That is a large gap, but the question favored the mindfulness group: only its members had been taught a way of dealing with pain, while the control course never mentioned it. The authors acknowledge this:

> Intervention participants received information on a mind-body approach to pain reduction, whereas the comparison group did not receive education about pain, which may have biased responses to measures.

At the start, both groups had rated their program as equally credible and equally likely to help their back pain. Still, everyone knew which program they were in, and every outcome in the trial was self-reported, so :term[demand characteristics]{id="demand_characteristics"}, people answering the way they sense is expected, can't be ruled out.

### Confidence in Managing Pain Rose, Then Evened Out

The biggest difference anywhere in the trial was in pain self-efficacy. At 8 weeks, the mindfulness group's confidence in managing pain had risen 9.8 points more than the control group's on a 0–100% scale (5.3 to 14.3), a medium effect size of 0.51. By 6 months the gap had shrunk to 3.5 points (−1.2 to 8.2), because the control group's confidence had also risen. Quality of life and pain catastrophizing showed the same pattern on a smaller scale: slightly better scores in the mindfulness group at 8 weeks, none statistically significant on its own, and almost no difference by 6 months. The authors judged these changes not clinically meaningful. Depressive symptoms didn't differ between the groups, but they were low to begin with.

### Mindfulness Scores Didn't Budge

The mindfulness questionnaire (the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale, scored 1 to 6) barely moved in either group: 4.6 at the start and 4.6 at 6 months in the mindfulness group, and 4.4 and 4.6 in the control group. The authors think the scale missed a real change:

> In addition, mindfulness scores indicated a high level of self-reported mindfulness at baseline. This level of mindfulness is likely the reason why we did not see a significant difference between groups at program completion, despite qualitative reports by participants of increased mindfulness after learning to meditate.

That may be so, but it means the trial can't show that the pain benefits came from people becoming more mindful.

### Who Came and Who Stayed

Attendance at the weekly classes was good: an average of 6.6 of 8 sessions in each group. The monthly boosters were another matter, with people coming to an average of 2.4 (mindfulness) and 2.5 (control) of the 6. Nearly everyone was assessed at 8 weeks, but at 6 months, 84.3% of the mindfulness group were assessed, compared with 95.1% of the control group. The intention-to-treat analysis kept everyone, and the authors report that handling the missing data differently didn't change the conclusion about function, but the uneven dropout is worth keeping in mind for the 6-month pain results. No harms related to either program were reported.

### What the Design Can and Can't Show

This was a carefully run trial by the standards of meditation research: large, publicly registered, with a control course matched for time and attention, assessors who didn't know who was in which group, and good attendance. Funding came from the National Institutes of Health, and the authors report no conflicts of interest, though they developed the program in their own earlier pilot studies.

Its limits are those of most behavioral trials. Every outcome was self-reported by people who knew which program they had, and only the mindfulness group learned anything about pain. Three pain measures and several secondary outcomes were tested at two time points, and the paper doesn't mention adjusting for the number of comparisons (:term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}), so the borderline results deserve caution. Partway through the trial, after 155 people had been assigned, the researchers noticed that the groups had become unbalanced by sex and switched to a randomization method that corrected for it. And the participants, mostly women from one region, with few depressive symptoms, may not represent all older adults with back pain.

## Final Reflection

This trial asked a practical question: can a mindfulness course help older adults with long-term back pain do more in their daily lives? Its main outcome gives a modest answer: a little, and only briefly. Both groups improved by a clinically meaningful amount, and the mindfulness group's extra improvement at 8 weeks was small and had disappeared by 6 months. The clearer differences were in pain: current pain was lower in the mindfulness group 6 months after the program, more of its members had their pain drop by 30% or more, and most of them said their back symptoms had improved, though the comparison course never addressed pain at all.

It makes an instructive contrast with a smaller trial in older adults, in which a six-week mindfulness course [improved sleep more than a sleep hygiene course](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/) measured right after the program. Here, with nearly six times as many participants and a longer follow-up, the advantage on the main outcome was modest and short-lived. In people whose chronic pain is complicated by opioid misuse, a mindfulness program that added reappraisal and savoring [eased pain more than a support group](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/), by similarly modest amounts.

Taken together with the earlier review, this trial suggests that mindfulness can help older adults with chronic back pain feel somewhat less pain, but not that it is a strong treatment for the disability that back pain causes. The authors suggest that future versions focus on making the gains in function last; they note that few participants came to the booster sessions meant to keep up their practice.
