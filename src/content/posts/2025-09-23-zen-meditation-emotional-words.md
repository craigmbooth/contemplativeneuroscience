## Background Information

Many studies have found that meditation can take the edge off emotional reactions, mostly using pictures. After eight weeks of mindful-attention training, for example, the [amygdala reacted less to emotional photos](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/). Words are a different kind of emotional material: their feeling comes through their meaning, which the authors call a higher-level representation. Larissa Lusnig, Markus Hofmann and colleagues at the University of Wuppertal in Germany found no earlier study of whether meditation changes how people process emotional words.

They chose :term{id="zen"} meditation, which the authors group with mindfulness as :term[open monitoring]{id="open_monitoring"} practice. In the Japanese Soto Zen tradition, practitioners sit facing a wall with their eyes half open, alternating seated meditation (zazen) with short periods of walking meditation (kinhin). The authors describe a central part of the practice:

> Thoughts, which naturally cross the mind during Zen meditation, are not judged as “good” or “bad” by the meditator.

If a meditation session made people less inclined to sort experience into good and bad, it might show up in how they judge the emotional tone of words. The researchers tested that in two ways. One was explicit: simply asking people how positive or negative each word was, its :term{id="valence" case="lower"}. The other was implicit. In a :term[lexical decision task]{id="lexical_decision_task"}, people only decide, as fast as they can, whether a string of letters is a real word. Nobody asks about emotion, but a word's emotional content still nudges how quickly it is recognized. In an earlier study using the same word set, Hofmann's group had found that calm negative words (low in :term{id="arousal" case="lower"}) were recognized more slowly than neutral words, while positive and highly arousing negative words were recognized faster.

## What They Did

Twenty Zen practitioners were recruited at meditation centers in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. They were 19 to 53 years old (average 33), 9 were women, and they had meditated for 7.9 years on average (from half a year to 28 years), for about 4.3 hours a week. Twenty people of similar age and gender, recruited at the university or online, formed the comparison group (9 women, 18 to 56 years old, average 34.5). None had any meditation or yoga experience. All were native German speakers.

The words were 200 German nouns in four groups, carefully matched for features that affect how fast words are read, such as length and how common they are: highly arousing negative, calm negative, neutral, and positive words. (The word list didn't have enough highly arousing positive nouns to form a fifth group.) Each participant did the task twice, once before and once after a session, with a different half of the words (100 words and 100 nonwords) each time, the halves swapped for half of the people. On each trial a letter string appeared for one second, and participants had to press a button within that second to say whether it was a word. About five minutes after each task, they rated every word they had just seen on a scale from −3 (very negative) through 0 (neutral) to +3 (very positive).

Between the two rounds, the groups did different things. The meditators took part in a 1.5-hour Zen session: two 25-minute periods of zazen, each followed by 5 minutes of kinhin, plus about 30 minutes of recited :term[mantras]{id="mantra"}, such as the Heart Sutra. The comparison group watched a 90-minute nature documentary, *Unsere Erde* ("Our Earth"), chosen as interesting but calm and not arousing.

Everyone also took a personality questionnaire, a concentration test and a vocabulary-based intelligence test. The groups didn't differ in concentration or intelligence, but the meditators scored higher on :term[openness to experience]{id="openness_to_experience" case="lower"} (6.35 vs. 4.45) and lower on "need for achievement and performance" (4.00 vs. 5.20), so the researchers included both scores in every analysis. The data were analyzed with :term[mixed-effects models]{id="mixed_model" case="lower"} that account for differences between people and between words.

## One Big Result: After Meditating, Emotional Words Were Rated Closer to Neutral

After the Zen session, the meditators rated the emotional words as less emotional than before. Negative words, both highly arousing and calm ones, were rated as less negative, and positive words as less positive. The comparison group's ratings were essentially the same after the film as before.

Error bars show standard errors. Adapted from Lusnig et al., 2020 (CC BY 4.0).](/assets/article_images/zen-meditation-emotional-words/valence-ratings.jpg)

In statistical terms, the change from before to after differed between the groups for all three kinds of emotional word (p ≤ .003 for each). Within the Zen group, the shift toward neutral was significant for each kind of word (p < .001), while none of the comparison group's changes came close to significance. The paper reports these results as regression estimates rather than average ratings, so the size of the shift is best seen in the figure. Leaving out the personality scores, or adding two others on which the groups differed slightly, didn't change which results were significant.

The authors' explanation is emotion regulation: Zen practice trains people not to judge thoughts as positive or negative, and after a session the meditators judged words less strongly. But the implicit measure told a different story:

> Even though the explicit evaluation of words was affected by meditation, we found no effects of meditation in the lexical decision task, where affective evaluation was not explicitly required.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Meditators Got Faster at Recognizing Every Kind of Word

Although the words' emotional tone affected recognition no differently after meditation, the meditators recognized all the words faster after their session (p < .001), whatever their emotional tone. The comparison group's speed didn't change significantly after the film (p = .275). The authors suggest that the session freed up attention, perhaps because the meditators were less distracted by the words' emotional content, were more focused, or let their minds wander less.

There is a wrinkle, which the authors raise themselves:

> The descriptive data might appear to suggest that meditators demonstrated, before the interventions, generally slower RTs than comparison subjects did.

In their chart of response times, the meditators look slower than the comparison group before the session and about as fast afterward. The authors tested this, and the groups' response times didn't differ significantly either before or after the sessions. So the meditators sped up relative to their own starting point, but they didn't end up faster than people who hadn't meditated.

### Calm Negative Words Slowed Everyone Down

Both groups recognized calm negative words more slowly than neutral ones, before and after the sessions, repeating the result of Hofmann's earlier study. The other two earlier findings, faster recognition of positive and highly arousing negative words, didn't appear in either group. The authors argue that the gap isn't just a matter of the small sample, since the test statistics didn't even lean that way, and suggest that the earlier study's setup (participants were having EEG electrodes attached, which may be mildly stressful) might have strengthened those effects.

### Why Would Meditation Change One Measure but Not the Other?

The authors' answer is time. In the rating task, people could take as long as they liked to weigh each word's feeling. In the lexical decision task, they had one second to respond, which may have limited the task to the earliest, automatic stage of processing a word's emotional meaning, before the slower, more evaluative stage that meditation may influence. They propose testing Zen meditators on emotional words with less time pressure, in a task that draws on those later, evaluative stages. On this account, a single session changed how people judge emotional words when they stop to think, not the split-second emotional pull of the words themselves.

### Zen Meditators and Words, Another Look

This isn't the only study of Zen practitioners judging letter strings. In an earlier fMRI study, Zen meditators doing a lexical decision task while focusing on their breath showed a [shorter-lived response to word meanings](/zen-meditation-default-mode-words/) in parts of the default mode network than non-meditators did, a finding the authors cite when suggesting that less mind-wandering may have sped up their meditators. A different kind of evidence comes from students taking a meditation course, who came to [recall more positive words](/meditation-course-positive-word-recall/) than students in other courses.

### Some Questions the Design Leaves Open

The two groups differed in two ways at once: who they were (experienced meditators vs. people who had never meditated) and what they did between the two rounds (a Zen session that included chanting vs. a film). That means the study can't say whether the shift came from the meditation itself or from something else about the meditators' 90 minutes, or whether beginners would show it after a first session. The groups weren't randomly assigned, and they differed in personality; the researchers handled that statistically, but other unmeasured differences could remain. The ratings are self-reports, and the meditators knew they had just meditated, so their expectations can't be ruled out as part of the effect. With 20 people per group, a single session and no later follow-up, it's also unknown how long the shift lasted.

## Final Reflection

This small study asked a precise question: does a meditation session change how people respond to emotional words, and if so, at which stage? Its answer splits in two. When experienced Zen meditators stopped to rate how positive or negative words were, they rated them more neutrally after 90 minutes of practice, while non-meditators who watched a film didn't change. When the same words flashed up for a one-second word-or-not decision, their emotional tone affected the meditators just as much as before, though the meditators responded faster overall.

The authors read this as the session changing a later, evaluative stage of processing rather than the earliest, automatic one, which echoes the Zen instruction to notice thoughts without labeling them good or bad. But this is one session in 20 experienced meditators compared with 20 non-meditators doing something else entirely, so the clearest conclusion is a modest one: right after their practice, these meditators judged emotional words less strongly, and whether that comes from the practice itself or would be seen in newcomers remains to be tested.
