## Background Information

Worrying about a difficult conversation or replaying an argument can feel almost as stressful as the real thing. The authors of this study think they know why: when we imagine an upsetting event, we tend to step inside it. They call this immersion, a strong sense of the self being engaged in the imagined scene, so that its sights, sounds, feelings and bodily sensations seem vivid and real, as if it were happening now. Once a stressful scenario feels real in this way, they argue, negative emotion, a bodily stress response and :term[repetitive negative thinking]{id="repetitive_negative_thinking"} tend to follow.

:term{id="mindfulness"} offers a possible counter to this. Many accounts of mindfulness include a shift in perspective known as :term{id="decentering" case="lower"}, which the authors describe this way:

> An important outcome of shifting perspective is an insight known as decentering: The realization that thoughts, feelings, and reactions are transitory patterns of mental activity, that they are not necessarily true representations of the self and events, and that they are not actually happening…

In the authors' view, decentering works by disengaging a person's sense of self from the imagined situation, so the situation stops feeling real. Earlier studies had found that brief instructions in this kind of "mindful attention" could change how people responded to tempting foods and to members of other social groups. But most of that work measured only behavior, so little was known about what decentering does in the brain, and none of it had focused on stressful thoughts. Most research on mindfulness had also looked at experienced meditators or people who had completed a course of several weeks. This study asked what happens when people with no meditation experience are simply taught the decentering part of mindfulness.

## What They Did

The researchers recruited 30 students aged 18 to 23 (15 women) from Emory University and Georgia Tech. Anyone with meditation experience was excluded, as were people taking psychiatric medication. Four other people were replaced: three moved too much in the scanner and one admitted in the exit interview to not following instructions.

The material was 120 one-sentence scenarios written in the second person, about 15 words long. Sixty were stressful situations from student life, such as "You have to tell your parents you failed a class and need to take summer school." Each had a matched non-stressful version with the same setting and characters, such as "You tell your parents you're considering taking a class over the summer to free up your fall schedule." In an earlier rating study, the stressful scenarios averaged 5.86 for stressfulness on a 7-point scale and the non-stressful ones 1.34.

Training took place in a session of about 90 minutes, which also included some questionnaires. Participants learned two strategies:

- **Immersion:** become completely absorbed in the scenario as if it were happening now, mentally traveling into it and vividly experiencing its sensory details, physical sensations, feelings and emotions.
- **Mindful attention:** stay aware of where you physically are while thinking about the scenario, and notice the reactions you would normally have, but instead of "living" the event, simply observe your thoughts and reactions as they arise in the present moment, as passing mental states that are your responses to the scenario, not part of it.

One to two days later, participants reviewed both strategies, did one more practice run and went straight into the scanner. They were not asked to practice at home. There was no separate control group: in this within-person design, every participant used both strategies, each on different scenarios, in alternating blocks. The authors chose this after pilot participants said the two strategies were easier to learn by contrasting them with each other.

In the scanner, which recorded brain activity with :term{id="fmri"}, each trial had two parts of 6.9 seconds each. First the scenario appeared and participants simply read and understood it (the reading period). Then the text dimmed, cuing them to apply the current block's strategy (the strategy period). They then rated how well they had managed the strategy from 1 to 5. Between trials, participants did a simple task of spotting the word "left" or "right" hidden in a sentence, which served as the baseline that brain activity was compared against. A share of "catch" trials that stopped after the reading period let the team separate brain activity in the two periods. After the scan, participants rated how stressful each scenario was.

## One Big Result: Mindful Attention Engaged Control and Perspective-Taking Areas More, and Self-Related Areas Less, Than Immersion

The clearest test in the paper compared the two strategies directly during the strategy period, combining stressful and non-stressful scenarios. Several areas were more active during mindful attention than during immersion: the lateral prefrontal cortex, mostly on the left (linked to executive and attentional control), part of the medial prefrontal cortex associated with inhibitory control, the :term[angular gyrus]{id="angular_gyrus" case="lower"} and neighboring :term[inferior parietal lobule]{id="inferior_parietal_lobule" case="lower"}, and areas of the visual cortex, cerebellum and brainstem.

Only one cluster was more active during immersion: a region on the lower inner surface of the frontal lobes spanning the :term[ventromedial prefrontal cortex]{id="vmpfc"} (vmPFC), the medial orbitofrontal cortex, and the ventral and subgenual parts of the :term[anterior cingulate]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"}. Other research has linked these areas to sensing the body's internal state, processing reward and tagging things as relevant to oneself. The authors read this as a sign that immersion engaged a felt, bodily sense of self:

> Thus, immersion appeared to engage stronger self, bodily, and affective responses than did mindful attention, consistent with engaging oneself in events physically, becoming immersed in them, and experiencing them as subjectively real.

Put together, they argue, mindful attention used regulatory and perspective-shifting resources to pull an embodied sense of self out of the imagined event, which in their account is what decentering is. They also note that the angular gyrus peak sat closest to coordinates reported in studies of attributing actions to an outside agent rather than oneself.

Two things temper this. First, direct comparisons between the strategies turned up few differences overall. In the reading period, only one small cluster in the visual cortex differed, and immersion's extra activity in the strategy period was a single cluster of 75 voxels (each voxel a 3 × 3 × 3 mm cube of brain). The authors explain that the two strategies often raised activity in the same areas, just not always by enough to pass the significance threshold for both. Second, describing the vmPFC and subgenual cingulate as a "visceral sense of self" relies on what these regions have been linked to in other studies. They do many things, so the label is an interpretation, not something this experiment measured directly.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Mindful Attention Seemed to Start While People Were Still Reading

The authors also counted how much of the brain became active above the baseline task in each condition, after removing all the activity the conditions shared. For stressful scenarios, the two strategies followed opposite paths over the two halves of a trial.

With mindful attention, a lot of activity appeared while people were only meant to be reading, including in visual, body-related (somatosensorimotor) and limbic networks, and less during the strategy period. With immersion, little appeared during reading and much more once people immersed themselves, including in somatosensorimotor, limbic, :term[default mode]{id="dmn"} and ventral attention networks. Because each block used one strategy throughout, participants knew which strategy was coming, and the authors suggest that with mindful attention they began applying it while reading, heading off strong emotional and bodily responses before they developed. Immersion, by contrast, seemed to wait until the strategy period to build up the scene. On this basis the abstract says mindful attention "down-regulated" stressful events while immersion "up-regulated" them. That conclusion rests on these counts of above-threshold voxels and on chi-square tests of them, a looser kind of evidence than the direct comparisons above, which found almost no difference in the reading period.

For non-stressful scenarios, both strategies showed more activity in the strategy period than in reading. The authors suggest that bland events give mindful attention little to work with, so people may have had to work harder to produce reactions to observe.

### Stress Ratings Barely Moved

After the scan, scenarios that participants had met during mindful attention trials were rated slightly less stressful than those met during immersion trials: 3.57 versus 3.66 on a 1-to-7 scale, averaged across stressful and non-stressful scenarios. The difference was statistically significant (p = .031), and the authors describe it as "a small sustained effect." It amounts to less than a tenth of a point, whereas the stressful scenarios were rated far more stressful than the non-stressful ones (5.66 versus 1.56). Stress was not rated during the scan itself, and heart rate, breathing and skin conductance were recorded in the scanner but left for a later paper, so this study has little to say about whether mindful attention eased stress, felt or physical, in the moment.

### Both Strategies Felt Doable, but Mindful Attention Took More Effort

Participants rated how well they had carried out each strategy at between 3.75 and 3.89 on the 5-point scale, with no difference between the strategies or scenario types. In exit interviews, most said immersion made the scenarios more vivid, especially with bodily tension, and most found mindful attention more effortful. Some who struggled with immersion said it was unpleasant to project themselves into stressful situations. That fits the brain results: the authors suggest beginners had to work at the unfamiliar perspective, which would explain the extra activity in prefrontal control areas.

### No Quieting of the Default Mode Network

Studies of [experienced meditators](/reduced-dmn-active-task/) often find reduced activity in the :term[default mode network]{id="dmn"} (DMN), the brain system most associated with self-related thinking and mind wandering. Here the direct comparisons showed no difference in its core hubs between the two strategies, and mindful attention to stressful scenarios produced plenty of default mode activity above baseline. The authors note that reduced default mode activity is reported mainly in experts, and suggest these regions may be heavily engaged when people are first learning. Nor did the strategies differ in the :term{id="insula" case="lower"} or :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"}, which some meditation studies have highlighted. The authors suggest that brief instruction may simply not engage the hippocampus, and that the insula differences seen in meditators may come from body-focused practices such as Vipassana, which this training left out.

## Final Reflection

This study isolates one idea from the large bundle that is mindfulness: stepping back to see a thought as a passing event in the mind rather than a situation you are living through. After a single session of instruction and a short review, people who had never meditated could apply it in a scanner. Compared with plunging into the same kind of scenario, it went with more activity in control and perspective-shifting areas and less in a frontal region tied to a felt, bodily sense of self. The authors take this to mean that everyone has a basic capacity for decentering that mindfulness programs can build on.

It is a much milder shift than the ones studied in [long-term meditators who let go of the sense of self](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/) altogether. The self isn't dissolved here, only taken out of the imagined scene. The authors are clear that this is a starting point:

> We hasten to add, however, that the decentering skill demonstrated here for non-meditators may lie on the earliest part of the learning curve.

There are other reasons for caution. The study had 30 students and imagined one-sentence scenarios, not real stressors. The comparison condition, immersion, was an instruction to engage as fully as possible, so "less self-related activity" here means less than during deliberate immersion, not less than during ordinary thinking, and there was no group that received no training. The brain maps used a common cluster-based correction for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"} with a fairly lenient voxel threshold (p < .005), and the strongest claims about down- and up-regulation come from comparing counts of above-threshold voxels rather than from direct comparisons. The one practical outcome measured, how stressful the scenarios seemed afterward, shifted by less than a tenth of a point. The authors themselves say it remains for future research to establish whether perspective shifting actually causes the reduced self-engagement. As a carefully designed look at what decentering might involve in the brain, the study is a useful first step; whether the skill lowers stress in daily life is a question it leaves open.
