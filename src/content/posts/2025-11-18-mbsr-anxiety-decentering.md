## Background Information

:term{id="mindfulness"} courses have been found to ease anxiety and stress in many groups of patients. A [meta-analysis of 142 trials](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/) found that, for anxiety, mindfulness-based treatments beat no treatment and did about as well as other therapies. The harder question is *how* they work. What changes in a person over eight weeks of meditation that then makes them less anxious?

The obvious candidate is mindfulness itself, as measured by questionnaires about how attentive and non-judgmental people are toward their experience. Another is :term{id="decentering" case="lower"}. The authors, a team at Massachusetts General Hospital led by Elizabeth Hoge, define it as a "metacognitive capacity" to observe what arises in the mind (thoughts, feelings, memories) "as mere psychological events." They add that it means seeing those events as temporary, rather than as part of who you are:

> This ability also involves recognizing that one’s thoughts, feelings, and urges are transient internal events rather than inherent, permanent aspects of the self or accurate representations of reality

That is what links decentering to the question of how meditation changes the sense of self. It describes a shift in one's relationship to one's own mind: the anxious thought is still there, but it is no longer taken to be *me* or the truth. The authors note that decentering and mindfulness overlap in theory, but distinguish them: mindfulness is heightened awareness with an attitude of non-judgment, while decentering is more specifically the ability to take a psychologically distanced stance.

Earlier work had linked decentering to recovery from depression. Gains in decentering during :term[cognitive behavioral therapy]{id="cbt"}, or during :term[mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT)]{id="mbct"}, a program designed to [prevent depressive relapse](/mbct-depression-relapse-meta-analysis/), predicted more lasting improvement. But to the authors' knowledge, no study had formally tested whether decentering *mediates* the benefits of a mindfulness program, or pitted it against mindfulness as competing explanations.

They chose to test this in :term[generalized anxiety disorder]{id="generalized_anxiety_disorder"} (GAD), a condition defined by chronic worry about many topics, along with physical symptoms such as restlessness, poor concentration and disturbed sleep. People with GAD are often caught up in worries about the future and ruminations about the past, so the ability to step back from those thoughts seemed a plausible route to relief.

## What They Did

The data came from an earlier :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial" case="lower"} by the same group, in which adults diagnosed with GAD through a structured clinical interview were randomly assigned to one of two 8-week group courses:

- **:term[Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)]{id="mbsr"}**: breath awareness, :term{id="body_scan" case="lower" plural} and gentle hatha yoga, with daily home practice guided by recordings. It was slightly shortened for this clinical group: the usual all-day retreat became 4 hours, and home practice was cut from 40 to 20 minutes a day.
- **Stress Management Education (SME)**: an :term{id="active_control_group" case="lower"} with no meditation or other mind-body practice. It taught healthy living through education about diet, exercise, sleep, time management and resilience, and had its own half-day session with a stability-ball exercise class and a dietitian's lecture. Its purpose was to match MBSR on group support, instructor attention and expectations of benefit.

The decentering questionnaire was added after the trial had started, so only part of the trial's sample completed it. This paper analyzes that subgroup: 38 people, 19 in each course, with an average age of 37.6. The authors report that they did not differ from the full trial sample in demographics.

Everyone filled in four questionnaires before and after the course:

- **Anxiety**: the 21-item :term[Beck Anxiety Inventory]{id="beck_anxiety_inventory"} (BAI), scored 0 to 63.
- **Worry**: the 15-item Penn State Worry Questionnaire–Past Week (PSWQ-PW).
- **Decentering**: the 11-item decentering subscale of the Experiences Questionnaire, scored 11 to 55, with higher scores meaning more decentering. The paper doesn't list its items.
- **Mindfulness**: the 39-item :term[Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire]{id="ffmq"} (FFMQ), which also yields scores for five facets: observing, describing, acting with awareness, non-judging and non-reactivity to inner experience.

The key analysis was a :term{id="mediation_analysis" case="lower"}. It asks whether the difference between the courses in how much anxiety fell can be traced, statistically, through how much each course changed decentering or mindfulness. The authors used :term{id="bootstrapping" case="lower"}, resampling their data 4,000 times to build a :term{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} around each indirect effect. An indirect effect counted as significant if its 95% confidence interval did not include zero. They tested each mediator on its own and then both together, to see which one still carried the effect when the other was accounted for.

## One Big Result: Gains in Decentering Statistically Accounted for MBSR's Extra Drop in Anxiety

Both groups improved, but the MBSR group changed more. Decentering rose by 8.0 points on average after MBSR and by 2.6 points after SME, a clear difference between the groups (p < 0.001). Anxiety fell by 7.5 points after MBSR and by 2.0 after SME, a smaller and more borderline difference (more on that below). Mindfulness scores rose by 17.2 points after MBSR and 5.5 after SME, a difference that fell short of significance (p = 0.079).

Across everyone, people whose anxiety fell most also tended to gain most in decentering (r = −0.53) and in mindfulness (r = −0.54). The two gains were themselves correlated (r = 0.56), so the question was which of them better explained MBSR's advantage.

Tested one at a time, both counted as mediators: decentering (indirect effect 5.84; 95% CI 2.04 to 13.44) and mindfulness (2.69; 95% CI 0.01 to 8.97, only just clear of zero). Tested together, only decentering remained:

- **Decentering**: indirect effect 3.66, 95% CI 0.68 to 8.38.
- **Mindfulness**: indirect effect 1.89, 95% CI −0.06 to 7.17, not significant.

Swapping the overall mindfulness score for its five facets didn't change the picture: decentering was still the only significant mediator. The authors conclude that MBSR's improvements in GAD symptoms are "in part explained" by increased decentering.

The abstract also reports that once decentering was in the model, the remaining direct effect of MBSR on anxiety was no longer significant, which it reads as decentering "fully" mediating the effect. In a sample of 38, though, a non-significant remainder is weak evidence that nothing else is going on. The "in part" wording is the safer reading.

Why decentering would matter most for this measure is a matter of interpretation. The BAI mainly measures what the authors call physiological anxiety, the bodily arousal that comes with it. They suggest:

> It could be that having a psychologically distanced stance buffers individuals from the distress associated with physical signs of anxiety.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Worry Followed Mindfulness, Not Decentering

For the second outcome, worry, the pattern reversed. Gains in mindfulness mediated the drop in worry (indirect effect 2.66; 95% CI 0.23 to 6.06), and decentering did not (3.66; 95% CI −0.54 to 8.76). Among the five facets of mindfulness, only non-reactivity to inner experience (letting thoughts and feelings come and go without reacting to them) and acting with awareness (as opposed to acting on autopilot) were significant, both with lower confidence limits just above zero.

There is a catch. In this subgroup, the two courses did not differ significantly in how much worry fell (8.8 points after MBSR, 6.7 after SME; p = 0.26). An indirect effect can still be tested when the overall difference between groups isn't significant, but it makes the worry finding more tentative than the anxiety one. The authors nonetheless read the split, decentering for anxiety and mindfulness for worry, as evidence that the two are distinct skills.

### The Groups Weren't Far Apart, and the Statistics Are Borderline

The authors note that comparing MBSR with an attention control yields a smaller difference between groups, which they put down to the control course's own benefits of expectation, attention and group support. MBSR's advantage on anxiety was significant only with a one-sided test, which the authors justified because they were checking, in a smaller subgroup, a result already found in the full trial (t = −1.83, p = 0.038). A conventional two-sided test would not have reached the usual 0.05 threshold. The MBSR group also started out more anxious (18.1 vs 13.8 on the BAI; d = 0.49), though the difference was not statistically significant. A group that starts higher has more room to fall, for example through :term{id="regression_to_the_mean" case="lower"}.

The mediation results lean on confidence intervals, as is standard for this method. The paper's table also gives conventional p-values "for completeness," and by those, several of the effects called significant would not be: the mindfulness-only mediator of anxiety has p = 0.21, and decentering in the joint model p = 0.051. Several lower confidence limits sit barely above zero. None of this makes the result wrong, but it is a fragile one.

### What This Kind of Mediation Can't Show

Randomization means the difference between the groups in anxiety can be credited to the courses. It doesn't randomize decentering. Decentering and anxiety were measured at the same two moments, before and after, so the data can't say which changed first. Feeling less anxious could make it easier to see one's thoughts as passing events, rather than the other way round. The authors acknowledge this directly:

> A third limitation is that the nature of the assessments (pre- and post-treatment only) precludes us from establishing that change in decentering and mindfulness preceded change in anxiety.

Every measure was also a self-report questionnaire, so a general shift in how people described themselves after taking a course could move several scores together. And since the BAI emphasizes bodily arousal rather than the worry at the core of GAD, the authors note it may not capture GAD distress precisely. The findings may not apply to other conditions.

### Who Ran the Study

The work was funded mainly by a U.S. National Institutes of Health grant. One author listed consulting and research grants, and spousal equity in several companies. The others declared no conflicts of interest. One co-author, David Fresco, led the paper that validated the decentering questionnaire used here, and co-authored the papers on :term{id="emotion_regulation" case="lower"} therapy, which the discussion offers as an example of a treatment designed to build decentering. That doesn't undermine the analysis, but it is useful context.

## Final Reflection

This small study puts a number on an idea at the heart of mindfulness-based therapy: that what helps may be less a matter of becoming more attentive than of changing one's relationship to one's own mind, coming to treat thoughts and feelings as passing events rather than as truths about oneself. In this sample, that shift tracked MBSR's advantage over a well-matched health course better than general mindfulness did.

The evidence is suggestive rather than decisive. With 38 people, borderline group differences and only before-and-after questionnaires, the study shows that decentering and anxiety moved together in the way a causal pathway would predict, not that one drove the other. Testing it properly would take larger trials that measure decentering week by week, to see whether it shifts before anxiety falls.

For the question of how meditation changes the sense of self, decentering may be an everyday relative of what other studies explore at the extremes. [Experienced Vipassana meditators asked to experience the present without being "in the center"](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/) are deliberately loosening the sense of self. Decentering asks for less: not that the self disappears, but that a worry be seen as a worry rather than as part of who one is. In this trial, that more modest shift went with feeling less anxious.
