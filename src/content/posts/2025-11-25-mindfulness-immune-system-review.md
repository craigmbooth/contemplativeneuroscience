## Background Information

Most evidence that mindfulness programs help health rests on what people say about themselves: less stress, less pain, better sleep. Self-reports are useful, but they can be biased, and they don't show whether anything has changed in the body. The immune system is an obvious place to look. Chronic :term{id="inflammation" case="lower"} and weakened immune defenses have been implicated in illnesses from asthma and rheumatoid arthritis to metabolic disorders, some cancers and depression, and chronic stress can weaken immune defenses.

By 2015, a scattering of randomized trials had measured immune markers before and after mindfulness training, but nobody had pulled them together. David Black and George Slavich set out to do that. They limited themselves to :term[randomized controlled trials]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, where people are assigned to mindfulness or a comparison group by chance, so that differences afterwards are more likely to come from the training itself.

## What They Did

This is a :term{id="systematic_review" case="lower"}: the authors searched for every trial that met rules set in advance, then described what each one found. It is not a :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"}. They didn't combine the results statistically into an overall effect size; they tallied, marker by marker, how many trials found a change.

The search covered papers from January 1966 to July 2015 in the American Mindfulness Research Association's database, which collects mindfulness studies from PubMed, PsycINFO, Web of Science and other sources, using terms such as immune, inflammation, cytokine and telomere. The authors also checked bibliographies, journal contents pages and suggestions from other researchers. A trial counted if it was randomized, made mindfulness meditation the main part of the program, measured an immune marker objectively and was published in English. Six non-randomized studies were set aside.

Twenty trials qualified, with 1,602 participants in all. They were small: 80 people on average, ranging from 21 to 201. Half recruited people with a disease, such as breast cancer, HIV, ulcerative colitis or rheumatoid arthritis. Five recruited people with a risk factor, such as older age, poor sleep, loneliness, overweight or cardiovascular risk, and five recruited healthy volunteers. Women outnumbered men in most of them: 14 of the 20 samples were at least 60% female.

The programs were similar. All but three were :term{id="mbsr"} or a course built on it, met as a group once a week for 6 to 10 weeks, and in eight cases included a day-long retreat. The exception in length was a three-month residential meditation retreat. Nineteen of the 20 trials measured immune activity in blood, one of them also in nasal washes; the twentieth used fluid from a small blister raised on the skin.

The authors grouped the results into five kinds of marker:

- **Inflammatory proteins**, such as :term[C-reactive protein (CRP)]{id="c_reactive_protein"}, :term{id="il_6"} and :term{id="tnf_alpha"}.
- **Transcription factors**, switches inside immune cells that turn inflammatory genes on, chiefly :term{id="nf_kb"}.
- **Immune cell counts**, such as :term[CD4+ T cells]{id="cd4_t_cells"}.
- **Immune cell aging**: the length of :term{id="telomeres" case="lower"}, the protective caps on chromosomes, and the activity of :term{id="telomerase" case="lower"}, the enzyme that maintains them.
- **Antibody responses**, for example to a flu vaccine.

## One Big Result: Four Markers Shifted in Three or More Trials; Most Others Didn't

The authors set a bar for calling an effect replicated: at least three trials finding it, where a trend that fell short of statistical significance, or an effect seen only in people who practiced more, still counted. Four markers cleared it:

> When using the criteria of three or more RCTs showing that mindfulness meditation has at least a trend-level or dosage-dependent effect on immune parameters, we identified … effects for the following four parameters: reductions in the activity of the cellular transcription factor NF-kB, reductions in circulating levels of CRP, increases in CD4+ T cell count (in HIV-diagnosed individuals), and increases in telomerase activity

Looking at each of the four more closely shows how tentative the support is:

- **NF-κB.** All three trials that measured it found lower activity after training. Two compared mindfulness with a wait-list group: one in breast cancer patients, the other in lonely older adults. In the third, a [sleep trial in older adults](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/) led by Black himself, NF-κB also fell significantly in the sleep hygiene comparison group.
- **CRP.** Six trials measured it and three found reductions. One of those was only in ulcerative colitis patients whose illness hadn't flared, and the other two were trends that fell short of significance. The other three trials found no change.
- **CD4+ T cells.** Four trials counted T cells in blood, and all found more of them, more activated ones, or a smaller decline than in the comparison group. Three of the four were in people with HIV, for whom CD4+ T-cell counts are a standard measure of how far the disease has progressed.
- **Telomerase.** All three trials that measured it found higher activity, at least at the trend level. They were a three-month retreat ([Jacobs et al., 2011](/immune-telemorase-psychological-mediators/)), a trial in women with overweight or obesity, and a trial in breast cancer patients. In the overweight trial, the rise was a trend seen only among the women who stuck most closely to the program.

Everything else, the authors write, either showed no effect or wasn't replicated. That covers IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α, interferon, antibodies and most cell counts. Not every single result pointed toward less inflammation. One trial found relatively *higher* IL-8, an inflammatory signal, in nasal fluid from older adults with acute respiratory infections after mindfulness training than after exercise.

The authors' own summary is cautious:

> Considered together, these data point to promising areas of future investigation, but we caution against exaggerating the positive effects of mindfulness meditation on immune system dynamics until these effects are further replicated and additional studies are performed.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### IL-6 Mostly Didn't Budge

Along with CRP, IL-6 was the inflammatory protein these trials measured most often. Six trials measured it, and only one found a reduction, in breast cancer patients, where it depended on how often they practiced. The other five found no change relative to the comparison group.

### Mixed Results for Antibodies

Two trials looked at the antibody response to a flu vaccine. One, [Davidson et al. (2003)](/brain-and-immune-function/), found a greater rise in antibodies after MBSR than in a wait-list group of company employees. The other, in older adults, found no difference. A third trial gave older adults a harmless test substance (keyhole limpet hemocyanin) to provoke an antibody response and found a greater increase in one antibody type, IgG, right after MBSR. The paper isn't consistent about the later result: its text describes a smaller increase at 24 weeks, while its table marks it as a decrease.

### Several Effects Depended on Practice

The review counted results that appeared only in people who practiced more. Besides the IL-6 and telomerase results above, it counts a TNF-α reduction from a skin inflammation trial that compared MBSR with a matched health program ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)). There, TNF-α in blister fluid didn't differ between the groups, but among MBSR participants more practice went with a bigger drop. The authors take such results as a reason for future trials to record attendance and home practice and to report effects both overall and by amount of practice.

### What the Trials Couldn't Show

The authors list the weaknesses of the field plainly. Only 8 of the 20 trials (40%) compared mindfulness with a program matched for time, attention and group support, such as exercise, sleep hygiene or health education. Half used a wait-list or usual care, which can't separate mindfulness from the general effects of joining a group. Many trials were designed around something else, such as stress, sleep or pain, and added immune markers on the side:

> immune biomarkers were often included as ancillary measures and, consequently, hypothesis tests were frequently underpowered.

That means a trial could easily miss a real effect. The trials also differed widely in who took part, how they were designed and how blood samples were processed, and several described their lab procedures too thinly for others to repeat them. The replicated findings, the authors add, are restricted to particular populations, subgroup analyses and disease types. The CD4+ T-cell result, for example, applies to people with HIV.

## Final Reflection

This review is a useful map of what had been tested by 2015, and an honest one. Twenty trials sounds like a lot, but they were spread across a dozen different markers, so no marker was measured in more than six of them, and several were measured in only two or three. The four "replicated" effects rest on three or four small trials each, counting trends and subgroups, and the review didn't combine the trials statistically to estimate how large any effect was.

The authors' conclusion is accordingly modest: mindfulness *may* nudge the immune system toward a healthier profile, with less inflammatory signaling, stronger cell-mediated defenses and more of the enzyme that protects chromosomes. Whether those shifts are large or lasting enough to matter for health is, they write, a question for future trials that make immune markers their main outcome, recruit enough people and compare mindfulness with an equally engaging program. Some later trials covered on this site use the kind of matched comparison the authors call for: [Creswell et al. (2016)](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/), for example, compared a three-day mindfulness retreat with a matched relaxation retreat and measured IL-6 four months later.
