## Background Information

By the late 2010s, more than two dozen MRI studies had reported that meditators' brains differ in structure from other people's: more :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"} here, a thicker cortex there. A [2014 meta-analysis by Fox and colleagues](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/) found that the differences recurred in a handful of regions, including the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"} and the hippocampus, but judged their average size modest and probably inflated by unpublished null results.

Cyril Pernet and Alison Zammit at the University of Edinburgh, with Nikolai Belov (Otto von Guericke University Magdeburg) and Arnaud Delorme (Paul Sabatier University, Toulouse, and the University of California San Diego), took another look with studies published up to 2018. They asked two questions: how strong are the reported links between meditation and gray matter, and is there anywhere in the brain where they consistently show up?

Their review differs from Fox's in a few ways that matter for reading it:

- **A narrower definition of meditation.** They counted only "the practice of focused attention or awareness with the aim of cultivating a calm and stable mental state," which they equate with mindfulness. They left out any program that adds movement such as yoga, because the movement could itself affect the brain and confound the results. That excluded :term{id="mbsr"}, which includes yoga, and with it the eight-week MBSR training studies, such as [Hölzel et al. (2011)](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/), that Fox and colleagues had included.
- **Mindfulness questionnaires counted too.** Unlike Fox's review, this one included studies that related gray matter to people's scores on a mindfulness questionnaire rather than to meditation practice, among them the two largest samples in the review (247 and 155 people).
- **Gray matter only.** White matter and cortical folding were left out.
- **The strongest result from each study.** For effect sizes, they took each study's single largest reported effect, wherever in the brain it was.

What it shares with Fox's review is the kind of evidence underneath. It does not pool training studies. Most of the 25 studies compared people who already meditated with people who didn't, at a single point in time.

## What They Did

The team searched five databases (MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, AMED and Global Health) on October 10, 2018, for MRI studies of meditation or mindfulness that measured gray matter volume, density or :term{id="cortical_thickness" case="lower"}. Of 698 unique records, 143 were read in full and 25 qualified. Together they included 1,646 participants, or 1,406 once people who appeared in more than one study were counted once. Studies ranged from 19 to 247 people (56 on average), and participants from 12 to 87 years old.

Most compared meditators from various traditions, from Zen and loving-kindness meditation to Sahaja Yoga Meditation, with non-meditators at a single point in time. A few related gray matter to years of practice or to mindfulness questionnaire scores. One looked at 19 patients receiving meditation as part of their treatment for opioid dependence (Fahmy et al., 2018). The authors describe the majority as :term[cross-sectional]{id="cross_sectional_study"} and observational, and only two as longitudinal. Some studies included people with pain disorders or other illnesses, or adolescents.

The team combined the studies in three ways:

- **How large were the effects?** From each study, they took the strongest reported result anywhere in the brain and converted it to Hedges' *g*, an :term[effect size]{id="effect_size"} like Cohen's *d* (the difference between groups in standard deviations; by convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large). For three studies the value had to be read from a published figure. They then pooled these in a random-effects :term[meta-analysis]{id="meta_analysis"}.
- **Where were the effects?** Sixteen studies that searched the whole brain reported the coordinates of their significant results. A technique called activation likelihood estimation (ALE) tested whether those points clustered in the same place more than chance would predict, using a strict correction for testing the whole brain.
- **What is that place connected to?** Around the region the ALE found, they took the coordinates of nearby insula findings and looked them up in published :term[resting-state]{id="resting_state_fmri"} :term{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"} maps from 1,000 people (not meditators), to see which other regions normally work in step with that spot.

They also rated each study for four kinds of bias using a standard appraisal checklist.

## One Big Result: Only the Right Insula Showed Up Consistently

The reported locations were scattered across the brain. Only one cluster survived the ALE's correction: a patch of the right anterior insula (the short insular gyrus) extending into the claustrum, a thin sheet of gray matter beneath it. Three studies contributed to it: patients in the opioid study who received meditation versus those who didn't, Sahaja Yoga meditators versus controls, and a study linking higher mindfulness questionnaire scores to more gray matter there. The authors note that this front part of the insula is typically associated with emotion.

Six studies reported right insula effects, but only three fell within the cluster; the others lay further back or higher up in the insula. (Lazar et al., 2005, the first study to report a right insula difference, gave no coordinates and couldn't be included.) The authors acknowledge that 16 studies is fewer than the roughly 20 recommended for a reliable ALE, so the analysis was likely underpowered. They conclude the insula finding is a true effect rather than a false positive because the other insula results lay close by and because of the connectivity analysis.

That analysis showed that the spot in the right insula normally works in step with the left insula, the lower part of the sensory strip (the inferior postcentral gyri) on both sides, and the anterior cingulate and neighboring paracingulate cortex. About a third of the studies had reported differences around the anterior cingulate, but too spread out to reach significance on their own. The authors recommend that future studies test the left and right insula and the anterior cingulate as :term[regions of interest]{id="region_of_interest"} decided in advance. Note that the connectivity maps come from people in general, not meditators, so they show what the insula is wired to, not that meditators differ in those regions.

Two of these regions match Fox's review, which also found differences recurring in the insula and anterior cingulate cortex. Fox's longer list of regions came partly from counting regions reported in three or more studies, an approach this review didn't use. The authors compare their map instead with a 2015 ALE meta-analysis by Boccia and colleagues, and note that their newer software applies a stricter correction, so they did not expect many similarities.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Large Average Effects, Which the Authors Say Are Overestimated

Pooling the strongest result from each of the 23 studies with usable data gave an average effect of *g* = 0.82 (95% :term[confidence interval]{id="confidence_interval"} 0.45 to 1.20). In the 18 studies that directly compared meditators with non-meditators (395 meditators and 454 controls in total), it was *g* = 1.03 (0.61 to 1.44). By the usual conventions, those are large. For the seven studies with right insula findings, the abstract puts the effect at about *d* = 0.8 and the discussion at somewhere between 0.2 and 1.4.

The authors do not take these numbers at face value. Each study contributed its single best result, picked from across the whole brain, and only significant results were reported. On the :term{id="funnel_plot" case="lower"}, the more precise studies showed weaker effects and the less precise studies stronger ones, the pattern of :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"}. Every study but one found an effect, and the one that didn't reported no effect size. The effects also varied widely between studies (:term[heterogeneity]{id="study_heterogeneity"} I² of about 78%). The authors conclude that the "population effect size is overestimated."

So, they suggest, future studies should be planned around the low end of the confidence interval. For 95% :term[statistical power]{id="statistical_power"} to detect a whole-brain difference between meditators and controls, a study would need 62 people per group, more than most of the reviewed studies had. A study focused on the insula might need up to 417 per group.

Even so, the abstract states that "mindfulness meditation practice does induce grey matter changes." That causal wording goes further than one-time comparisons can, as the authors themselves acknowledge later in the paper (see below).

### Different Studies, Different Places

Usually, each study's strongest result was in a different part of the brain:

> More often than not, the strongest effects were observed in different locations in the brain which may be partly attributed to a lack of statistical power.

The authors' explanation is that underpowered studies produce false positives, and those turn up at random locations. Differences in design and analysis between studies could add to the scatter.

### Biases in Nearly Every Study

The bias ratings were sobering. The authors judged 24 of the 25 studies at risk of selection bias and 24 at risk of information bias, with the remaining study unclear on the latter. Their summary:

> The systematic review revealed design issues with selection, information, attrition and confirmation biases, in addition to weak statistical power.

The problems they describe include:

- **Unrepresentative samples.** Examples include healthcare workers as controls, samples made up only of volunteers or college students, and recruitment limited to one area; some studies didn't describe recruitment at all. Controls drawn from databases of healthy adults may have meditated themselves, which the authors note around 8% of people in Western societies do.
- **Mixed traditions.** Some studies pooled meditators from different or unspecified traditions, as if all practices had the same effect.
- **Self-reported experience.** Most studies measured meditation experience as hours or years recalled by participants. Mindfulness questionnaires such as the :term{id="ffmq"} have known problems with validity, the authors note, and one study split participants into high and low meditation/prayer groups without an established method.
- **Dropouts and measurement.** Several studies didn't account for participants who dropped out, and some measured gray matter by hand without saying whether the person measuring knew who was a meditator.
- **Missing details.** Fewer than half reported adjusting for age, sex and head size, and none shared the full statistical maps that would let others recompute effect sizes in any region.

### What One-Time Comparisons Can and Can't Show

Most of the evidence here compares people who already meditate with people who don't. Such studies can't separate meditation from everything else that differs between those groups, including whatever led people to take up long-term practice. The authors put it this way:

> Although this is enough to show the presence of an effect, there is a need for more randomised controlled trials and longitudinal studies to demonstrate therapeutic benefits.

They add that experienced meditators have often practiced in several traditions over the years, so the practice named at the time of the scan may not be what produced any difference.

Randomized trials published since have tested some of these ideas directly. In the largest randomized trial of MBSR and brain structure, a program this review excluded, [eight weeks of training changed gray matter no more than a matched wellness course or a waitlist](/mindfulness-brain-structure/). And in older adults, [18 months of meditation left gray matter volume in the insula declining no differently than English lessons did](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/). An earlier one-time comparison by the same lead author, of six older expert meditators and 67 controls (Chételat et al., 2017), is one of the 25 studies here.

## Final Reflection

This review narrows the question rather than settling it. Looked at with a strict correction, the many regions reported in meditators' brains mostly failed to line up, and the one place that did, a small patch of the right anterior insula, rested on three studies of quite different kinds: opioid patients given meditation, long-term meditators and a mindfulness questionnaire. The large average effect sizes come from each study's best result and, by the authors' own account, overstate the true size.

The review's most useful contribution may be practical. It names the insula and the anterior cingulate as regions to test in advance, and it estimates how many people such studies would need: far more than most of these studies had. The randomized trials summarized on this site that have since looked for such changes found none beyond their comparison groups. Whether the insula differences seen in experienced meditators come from practice or from who chooses to practice remains open.
