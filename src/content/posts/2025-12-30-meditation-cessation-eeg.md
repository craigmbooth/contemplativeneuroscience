## Background Information

Most meditation research looks at states in which experience changes: attention sharpens, thoughts quiet down, the sense of self loosens. Buddhist texts more than two thousand years old describe something more radical. In an event called :term[cessation]{id="cessation"} (*nirodha* in Pali), a very advanced meditator reports that consciousness itself briefly switches off, and then comes back.

The authors describe what is reported this way:

> During cessation, consciousness is momentarily absent (as reported by the meditator), and there may also be subsequent awareness of this absence having occurred. However, unlike sleep, there is almost no awareness of time having passed.

In the Theravada Buddhist tradition, cessations come at the end of a sequence of meditative "stages of insight," at a final stage called fruition. Teachers describe them as lasting only seconds, and say meditators come out of them with a sudden sense of mental clarity.

For neuroscience, this is an unusual opportunity. Scientists usually study the loss of consciousness through general anesthesia or sleep. A drug-free loss of consciousness that a person can steer toward from within, and that starts and ends abruptly, might reveal which brain activity consciousness depends on. But cessations are hard to study. Few people report them, they can't be produced on cue, and capturing one means recording the brain continuously while relying on the meditator to say when it happened. Before this study, the authors found only one neuroimaging study of cessation: two meditators, with three cessations each.

The research team, from the Meditation Research Program at Massachusetts General Hospital and collaborators in Australia and the Netherlands, took a different approach. Instead of a few cessations from several people, they recorded many from one person, the same strategy the program later used for [an expert meditator's jhana states](/jhana-brain-networks/). Based on earlier work, they predicted the main changes would show up in :term[alpha waves]{id="alpha"}.

## What They Did

The participant was one of the paper's authors, Daniel Ingram, a meditation teacher who was 51 at the time and had practiced for 26 years. Based on about an hour of daily practice plus roughly a year of retreat at 14 hours a day, the team estimated his lifetime practice at 23,110 hours. He had trained in several approaches, including Mahasi-style noting, the :term[jhanas]{id="jhana"} and :term{id="vipassana"}, and said he could deliberately incline his meditation toward cessations.

Between November 2019 and March 2020 he meditated with eyes closed during 29 :term{id="eeg"} recordings, each lasting from about 7 minutes to an hour, wearing a cap with 20 dry electrodes. He did one of two practices: a form of insight meditation called "review practice," aimed specifically at cessation, or fire kasina, a traditional Theravada practice of concentrating on visual sensations generated inside the mind.

**Finding the cessations.** Each cessation came with an involuntary flutter of the eyes and a large blink, which stood out clearly against the otherwise still, eyes-closed EEG. The meditator and another author independently marked these blinks in the raw recordings and picked the same moments. In one session, he also pressed a button after each of seven cessations. The button press came a median of 3.6 seconds after the blink (range 2.1 to 7.2 seconds), which supports the idea that the blink marks the event and that cessations are very brief. In the other sessions he didn't press a button, so that monitoring for one wouldn't affect his meditation.

**Grading them.** After every session, he rated each cessation on its build-up, entry, the event itself, the exit and the aftereffects. He reported 46 grade A cessations, 12 grade B and 11 grade C. The team kept only grade A events, which they took to be the clearest, and only those at least 80 seconds from another cessation and 40 seconds from the start or end of a session. After one recording could not be read because of a technical glitch, 37 cessations remained.

**The comparison.** For control data, a computer placed 27 markers at random points in the same sessions, each at least 160 seconds from any cessation. The team then analyzed the 80 seconds around every cessation and every control marker, split into four windows of about 18 seconds each: two before the event and two after. The 3 seconds on either side of the cessation were dropped, because the eye movements swamp the EEG there.

In each window they measured brain-wave power in standard frequency bands, using a method that separates true rhythmic activity from the brain's non-rhythmic background activity. Alpha was the prediction; differences in the other bands were treated as exploratory and weren't corrected for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"}. They set aside the fastest band, :term{id="gamma" case="lower"}, because at those frequencies scalp recordings pick up a lot of muscle activity.

## One Big Result: Alpha Waves Faded for 40 Seconds Before Each Cessation and Were Lowest Just After

Compared with the control stretches, alpha power was already lower over the back of the head in the first window, 22 to 40 seconds before the cessation. It kept falling as the cessation approached, and in the window just after the event (3 to 22 seconds afterward) it was at its lowest and lower across the whole scalp. In the last window, 22 to 40 seconds after, it was recovering toward its earlier level but was still lower than in the control stretches. Alpha power at the control points stayed steady across the four windows, so the change was tied to the cessations.

A second analysis tracked power in seven brain regions, two seconds at a time. Over the regions at the back of the head (occipital and parietal), alpha fell steadily across the whole 40 seconds before a cessation and climbed steadily across the 40 seconds after it. In the other regions, alpha only showed the steady climb back after the event.

The differences were large in statistical terms. The :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} for the window just after a cessation was a Cohen's d of 3.79. That number compares moments within one person's recordings, though, not groups of people, so it can't be set beside the effect sizes from typical studies.

Alpha waves are thought to reflect top-down processing: the brain using its expectations to predict and filter incoming sensory information. Alpha over the back of the head is also linked to turning attention away from the outside world. The authors connect the decline to a theory two of them have proposed: that deep meditation gradually takes apart the brain's layered, top-down predictions about the world, and that if this goes far enough, consciousness itself can drop out.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Theta Changed Only a Little

:term[Theta waves]{id="theta"}, often linked to relaxed alertness in meditation, were slightly higher than control in the window just before a cessation, mostly over the right side toward the back of the head. Just after the cessation, theta was lower in a small area at the top of the head. The regional analysis told a somewhat different story: theta rose over the 40 seconds before the event in three regions until it was close to the control level. These theta results were modest, exploratory and not fully consistent between the two analyses. Delta and :term{id="beta" case="lower"} power didn't differ around cessations.

### Unlike Anesthesia, and Unlike Most Meditation

Under drugs such as propofol, alpha tends to shift from the back of the head toward the front as people lose consciousness. Here, alpha simply dropped, most of all just after the event. The authors suggest that drop could reflect the brain becoming newly alert:

> the suppression in post-cessation global alpha power may be an indication of renewed wakefulness … which some meditators (including the participant in the current study) describe as a kind of inner “reset”

They also note that many studies of :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation find alpha *increases*, the opposite of this pattern. Falling alpha is also seen in disorders of consciousness such as coma, so the authors suggest cessations may resemble unconsciousness more than they resemble other meditative states. That is a suggestion, not something this study tested.

### Not Just the Eyes

A well-known EEG effect is that alpha drops sharply when people open their eyes. Since each cessation came with eye movements and a blink, could that explain the result? The authors argue it can't fully: the decline began about 40 seconds before the blink, which points to a gradual change in the brain rather than a reflex. They also acknowledge that the control stretches had no blinks, but argue that adding deliberate blinks would have created a different problem, since the cessation blinks were involuntary.

### A Different Result From the Only Earlier Study

The earlier study of two meditators found that cessations came with more long-range synchronization in gamma waves, the brain's fastest rhythms. This study didn't look at synchronization, and it set gamma aside because muscle activity contaminates it. The authors suggest the different methods, the much larger number of cessations here, the different ways the meditators signaled a cessation, and the different meditators may explain why the results don't line up.

## Final Reflection

This is a careful attempt to study something most meditation science hasn't touched: not a change in what a person experiences, but a reported gap in experience altogether. The design is unusual in a useful way. Thirty-seven events from one person give enough repetitions to see a consistent pattern, and the brain changes began well before the eye blink that marked each event, so they aren't simply a side effect of it. The power values were also fairly consistent across the 37 cessations, which suggests they weren't wildly different events.

There are real limits, and the authors name the main one:

> such a protocol simultaneously reduces the generalizability of the current results, that is, it may be that the alpha suppression effect observed may be idiosyncratic to the participant.

Other limits matter too. The EEG can't confirm that consciousness was absent; that rests on the meditator's own account. The events were located by a blink that may be peculiar to him, and checked against button presses in only one session. And the participant is not an outside volunteer: he is a co-author who helped design and run the study, and has provided research funding to the program that ran it, all of which the paper discloses. The paper's discussion says the results "validate" experienced meditators' ability to change their state of consciousness at will, while the abstract more cautiously calls them "evidence." What the study shows is a distinctive, repeatable brain pattern around the moments one meditator reports as cessations. Whether it holds for other practitioners, and how it compares with other deep states studied in single expert meditators, such as [content-free awareness](/content-free-awareness-expert-meditator/) and [jhana](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/), are questions for studies with more meditators.
