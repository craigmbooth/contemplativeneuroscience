## Background Information

:term[Chronic pain]{id="chronic_pain"}, usually defined as pain lasting more than three months or past the normal time for tissues to heal, is one of medicine's most stubborn problems. The paper cites an Institute of Medicine estimate that pain costs the United States at least $560–635 billion a year in health care and lost productivity. Chronic pain often comes with depression and with dependence on pain medication, which has pushed interest toward treatments that can be used alongside drugs or instead of them.

Mindfulness meditation is one of those treatments, most often taught through 8-week courses such as mindfulness-based stress reduction (:term{id="mbsr"}). Earlier reviews had found encouraging results, but their authors kept running into the same problem: many trials were small or poorly run, and they concluded that better research was needed before mindfulness could be recommended for chronic pain.

Lara Hilton and colleagues at the RAND Corporation, in a review funded by the US Department of Defense, set out to bring the evidence together with a registered protocol and a formal rating of how much it could be trusted.

## What They Did

The team searched four research databases for English-language :term[randomized controlled trials]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"} published up to June 2016. To qualify, a trial had to enroll adults with chronic pain, test a program that involved formal mindfulness meditation, and report pain or the use of pain medication. Programs such as yoga, tai chi and transcendental meditation were excluded unless they referred to mindfulness, and so was :term[acceptance and commitment therapy]{id="act"}, which teaches mindfulness skills without requiring formal meditation. Two reviewers independently screened the studies and extracted the data, and each trial's quality was rated good, fair or poor.

From 755 records, 38 trials met the criteria. Together they randomized 3,536 people, with trials ranging from 19 to 342 participants. The most common conditions were :term{id="fibromyalgia" case="lower"} and back pain (eight trials each), followed by headache or migraine, arthritis, irritable bowel syndrome and cancer. Twenty-one trials tested MBSR, six tested mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (:term{id="mbct"}), and eleven tested other mindfulness programs. Most programs lasted 8 weeks.

The comparison groups varied. Nineteen trials compared mindfulness with treatment as usual, thirteen with passive controls such as a :term{id="wait_list_control" case="lower"}, and ten with education or support groups; a handful used other treatments such as massage, stress management or cognitive behavioral therapy (:term{id="cbt"}). Some trials had more than one comparison group.

The team then ran a :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"}, pooling each trial's result at its longest follow-up (a median of 12 weeks, ranging from 4 to 60 weeks). The results are given as standardized mean differences, a kind of :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} that puts different pain scales on a common footing; by common rules of thumb, 0.2 is a small difference and 0.5 a medium one. Finally, they rated the overall quality of the evidence for each outcome as high, moderate, low or very low using the :term{id="grade_evidence"} system.

## One Big Result: Mindfulness Reduced Pain Slightly, but the Evidence Was Rated Low Quality

Eight of the 38 trials couldn't be pooled because they didn't report usable pain data. Among them was the largest trial in the review, a 342-person study of MBSR for back pain that reported only adjusted results. The remaining 30 trials, with 2,292 participants, showed that mindfulness reduced pain more than the comparison groups did, with a standardized mean difference of 0.32 (95% :term{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} 0.09 to 0.54). That is a small effect.

The authors put it in more familiar terms by averaging each group's percentage change in pain from the start of the trial. Pain fell by about 19% in the mindfulness groups and about 8% in the comparison groups. (The paper prints these as −0.19% and −0.08%, but they appear to be proportions, since individual trials' changes in pain ranged up to nearly 48%.)

The figure shows why the authors were cautious. Most trials found little or no difference, while a few found very large ones, and the gap between them was far bigger than chance would explain (:term{id="study_heterogeneity" case="lower"}, I² = 77.6%). The four largest effects ranged from 1.23 to 2.50, and three of those four trials had been rated poor quality. When the team kept only the 19 trials rated fair or good, the benefit was still statistically significant but shrank to 0.19 (0.03 to 0.34), and the trials agreed more closely.

Timing mattered too. At follow-ups of 12 weeks or less, the effect was not statistically significant (0.25, 15 trials); beyond 12 weeks it was (0.31, 14 trials). The main analysis showed no sign of :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"}, but in these timing analyses one of two tests suggested it. Taking inconsistency, heterogeneity and possible publication bias together, the authors rated the evidence for pain as low quality at both short and long follow-up. Their summary:

> In sum, mindfulness meditation was associated with a small effect of improved pain symptoms compared with treatment as usual, passive controls, and education/support groups in a meta-analysis of 30 randomized controlled trials. However, there was evidence of substantial heterogeneity among studies and possible publication bias resulting in a low quality of evidence.

Low quality, in GRADE's terms, means the true effect could be substantially different from the estimate.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Depression and Quality of Life Also Improved

The secondary outcomes told a similar story, with some differences in how far they could be trusted:

- **Depression** (12 trials): a small improvement of 0.15 (0.03 to 0.26). The trials agreed closely, so the authors rated this evidence high quality. It is good evidence for a small effect.
- **Mental health-related quality of life** (16 trials): 0.49 (0.22 to 0.76), rated moderate quality.
- **Physical health-related quality of life** (16 trials): 0.34 (0.03 to 0.65), rated low quality, with results again varying widely between trials.
- **Disability** (4 trials): 0.30 (−0.02 to 0.62), which fell just short of statistical significance and was rated low quality because there were so few participants.

### The Type of Program and Condition Didn't Seem to Matter

The authors checked whether the effect on pain depended on the kind of program, the condition, the length or frequency of sessions, whether mindfulness was given alone or alongside other treatment, or the type of comparison group. None of these made a statistically significant difference. MBSR did no better than MBCT or the other programs, and back pain, headache and fibromyalgia did no better or worse than other conditions. With only a few trials in some of these groups, though, real differences could have gone undetected.

### Most Control Groups Weren't Matched for Time and Attention

Because the main analysis pooled usual care, waitlists and education or support groups, it can't separate the effect of meditation itself from the general benefits of joining a weekly group, getting attention from an instructor and expecting to improve. The authors make this point in their call for better trials:

> In order to detect intervention specific effects, studies need to have attention-matched controls.

An earlier review by [Goyal and colleagues](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/) did restrict itself to trials with an :term{id="active_control_group" case="lower"} and reached a similar figure for pain (0.33), but that estimate came from just four trials, and the benefit varied by condition. A later and much larger review by [Goldberg and colleagues](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/) found mindfulness better than no treatment for pain conditions but no different from other therapies. Hilton's review cites Goyal's only in passing, for stress, and doesn't say how far the trials overlap.

### Little Is Known About Pain Medication or Side Effects

Only four trials reported on the use of pain medication, and the results were mixed. One trial of MBSR for pain after failed back surgery found that participants cut their use of painkillers more than the control group did, while two back-pain trials found no difference. The fourth was an early trial of Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (:term{id="more"}) in people with chronic pain who were misusing prescribed opioids. Right after treatment, MORE participants were more likely to no longer meet criteria for :term{id="opioid_use_disorder" case="lower"}, but the difference had faded three months later. A [later, larger trial of MORE](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/) followed people for nine months.

Side effects were barely measured. Four trials said no adverse events occurred. In one, two participants felt temporary strong anger toward their pain condition and two felt more anxious, and two others recorded mild side effects from yoga and progressive muscle relaxation. The authors are blunt about what this does and doesn't show:

> However, only 7 of the 38 included RCTs mentioned whether adverse events were monitored and collected. Thus quality of evidence for adverse events reported in RCTs is inadequate for a comprehensive assessment.

They note published reports of adverse events during meditation, including psychosis, and they ask future trials to collect this information actively.

### The Trials Had Many Weaknesses

Thirteen of the 38 trials were rated poor quality, mostly because they didn't analyze everyone who was randomized (:term{id="intention_to_treat" case="lower"}), lost too many participants to follow-up or described their methods poorly. Fifteen trials randomized fewer than 50 people, ten described themselves as underpowered pilot studies, and ten more didn't report a power calculation. Pain was measured with scales and questionnaires that participants filled in themselves. The review itself relied on published data only, without contacting trial authors, and included only English-language papers.

## Final Reflection

This review gives a fair snapshot of mindfulness for chronic pain as of 2016. Across 30 trials, mindfulness programs reduced pain a little more than usual care, waitlists or education groups did, and they improved depression and mental health-related quality of life too. But the pain effect was small, it shrank when the weakest trials were set aside, and it rested on trials that disagreed widely with one another. The authors' own verdict is that the quality of evidence for pain is low.

That doesn't mean mindfulness fails people in pain. The evidence that it eases depression, if only slightly, was rated high quality, many trials gave it alongside usual care rather than instead of it, and, as the authors note, people with chronic pain increasingly seek it out. What the review can't yet say is how much of the benefit comes from meditation itself, rather than from joining a supportive weekly group, or whether mindfulness does better than established psychological treatments for pain. Answering those questions needs the large, well-controlled trials with long follow-up that the authors call for.
