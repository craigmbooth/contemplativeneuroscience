## Background Information

Many contemplative traditions, especially in Buddhism, aim to see through the feeling of being a fixed, separate self. Some experienced meditators describe moments in which that feeling seems to fall away. Classic :term[psychedelic drugs]{id="psychedelic_drug"}, such as psilocybin and LSD, are also known for dramatic changes in the sense of self at high doses, a phenomenon researchers call :term[ego dissolution]{id="ego_dissolution" case="lower"}. Raphaël Millière, Robin Carhart-Harris, Leor Roseman, Fynn-Mathis Trautwein and Aviva Berkovich-Ohana noticed that the two fields rarely talk to each other, and asked whether "losing the self" in meditation and on psychedelics is the same thing.

This paper is a review, not a new study: it has no participants and no new data. When it was published, the first author was in the Faculty of Philosophy at the University of Oxford, Carhart-Harris and Roseman were in the Psychedelic Research Group at Imperial College London, Trautwein was at the Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences in Leipzig, and Berkovich-Ohana was at the University of Haifa. Several studies the review relies on are their own, including meditation studies summarized on this site.

This site is about meditation, so this summary covers the psychedelic research only as far as it sheds light on meditation. Nothing here is a suggestion to take these drugs. One point of vocabulary: by "self-consciousness" the authors mean a person's awareness of themselves, the sense of self, not the social awkwardness of feeling watched.

The authors start with two warnings. First, neither "meditation" nor "psychedelic states" is a single thing: the style of practice, the drug and dose, the person's experience and personality all shape what happens. And dramatic changes are the exception:

> In particular, dramatic disruptions of self-consciousness seem to occur mostly for highly experienced meditators or with high doses of psychedelics.

Second, they suspect that experiences described in the same words, "selflessness" or "ego dissolution," may differ a great deal.

## What They Did

This is a narrative review mixing philosophy of mind with neuroscience. The authors don't say how they chose the studies they cite, don't rate their quality and don't pool their results. Their aim is conceptual: to break the sense of self into parts, then ask what the evidence says about how meditation and psychedelics affect each part.

They begin by summarizing the brain findings for each.

**Meditation.** A :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"} of 78 brain-imaging studies found distinct patterns for four common styles: focused attention, mantra recitation, open monitoring and loving-kindness. Focused attention went with reduced activity in two hubs of the :term[default mode network (DMN)]{id="dmn"}, a set of regions most active during self-reflection, :term{id="mind_wandering" case="lower"} and thinking about one's past and future. Many studies of mindfulness meditation have found reduced activity in key DMN nodes, the :term[medial prefrontal cortex]{id="mpfc"} and the :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"}, and one found the drop larger during meditation than during an active thinking task ([Garrison et al., 2015](/reduced-dmn-active-task/)). The :term{id="insula"}, a region central to :term{id="interoception" case="lower"} (the sense of the body's internal state), turned up across styles, which the authors read as a sign that attention to the body, especially the breath, is central to most practice.

**Psychedelics.** :term[fMRI]{id="fmri"} studies of psilocybin and ayahuasca found reduced activity across many regions, including DMN hubs. Psilocybin, ayahuasca and LSD all made the DMN less internally coherent (its regions' activity rose and fell together less) while increasing :term[connectivity]{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"} between brain regions and between networks. When volunteers rated their ego dissolution, higher ratings went with several of these changes, such as a less coherent DMN under LSD. In many of these studies, ego dissolution was measured with a single questionnaire item.

The authors then take apart the sense of self.

## One Big Result: "Self-Loss" Is Not One Thing, and Meditation and Psychedelics Reach Different Versions of It

The review's central claim is that the sense of self has several components that can be disrupted separately. Meditation and psychedelics can each disrupt several of them, but not the same ones to the same degree. The authors sort the components into two families.

### The narrative self: thoughts, memories and plans about "me"

The narrative self is the running story of who we are: thoughts about ourselves, memories of our past and plans for our future, what researchers call mental time travel. The authors describe two ways it can be switched off.

**Self-related thinking stops.** Much of ordinary waking life is spent in self-involving thought, often about the future, and much of it goes unnoticed. Meditation trains :term{id="meta_awareness" case="lower"}: noticing when the mind has wandered and coming back. The authors suggest that once attention steadies, experienced meditators can have long stretches with no self-related thought at all, and they connect this to the reduced activity in the medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortex. Psychedelics also lower activity in these regions, and under psychedelics a less coherent DMN has gone with ego dissolution and with less mental time travel to the past. On this first route, the two seem to converge.

**Access to facts about oneself goes missing.** This is more dramatic: a temporary, amnesia-like loss of knowledge about oneself. Here the evidence is anecdotal. Respondents to an unpublished online survey by Millière, of experienced psychedelic users, described forgetting they were human. The authors speculate that very large reductions in DMN coherence might explain this, and point to studies linking a weakened DMN to memory loss. They think this second route is mostly specific to psychedelics, since that degree of DMN disruption "does not appear to be such a reliable feature of meditation."

### The bodily self: owning, feeling and locating one's body

The authors also distinguish a more basic, "minimal" or embodied self, which doesn't need any thinking about oneself. Within it, they separate three things.

**Body ownership,** the sense that this body is mine. The authors draw on studies of experienced meditators by Berkovich-Ohana's group. In one ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/)), 12 long-term mindfulness meditators tried to induce a "selfless" mode of awareness during :term[MEG]{id="meg"} recording, and four described losing the sense of ownership. In another, a single meditator with about 20,000 hours of practice, interviewed in depth about the state he could enter at will, said: "There is no sense of mine [and] there is no sense of me." People on psychedelics often report :term[depersonalization]{id="depersonalization" case="lower"}-like experiences, such as looking at a hand and not feeling it as their own. One hypothesis is that psychedelics loosen the brain's top-down expectations about the body.

**Bodily awareness,** simply feeling bodily sensations. Here the two routes may part. Many meditation practices direct attention *toward* the body, so the authors would not expect bodily awareness to vanish. In the expert meditator's deepest state, the body faded but did not disappear: "there is a kind of very light sense of body in this experience," he said. Several mindfulness meditators in another study reported that bodily sensations faded into the background. Psychedelics, DMT and 5-MeO-DMT in particular, may suppress awareness of the body completely. Volunteers given psilocybin or LSD score fairly high on questionnaire items such as "it seemed to me as if I did not have a body anymore," and in a DMT study, then still in preparation, almost all participants reported losing awareness of their body for several minutes at the peak. The authors add that both settings involve sitting or lying still, eyes closed or blindfolded, and that this sensory deprivation may itself loosen the sense of the body.

**Self-location,** the sense of being somewhere in space. Both routes seem able to remove it. The expert meditator said, "there's no real location at all," and several long-term mindfulness practitioners described "spacelessness." On psychedelics, people describe merging with their surroundings and score high on questionnaire items such as "it seemed to me that my environment and I were one." In the brain, both lines of evidence point to the :term[temporoparietal junction (TPJ)]{id="tpj"}, a region that helps work out where the self is. In experienced meditators, :term{id="beta" case="lower"} activity there fell as the boundary between self and world dissolved ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)), and under LSD, stronger ego dissolution went with increased connectivity of the TPJ and the insula. These are different measures in different people, and the authors call the link a hypothesis that needs more data.

The authors leave out the sense of agency, the feeling of being in control of one's actions, because the concept is ambiguous and there is little evidence on how either route affects it.

### "Total" self-loss: the same label for different experiences

Even when every component of the self seems to go, the authors argue, the experiences can differ. Their tentative comparison, below, puts the clearest difference in something that isn't part of the self at all: how much else is in experience. Ego dissolution on psychedelics usually comes with vivid sensations and visions, while the available reports of some selfless states in meditation suggest very sparse experience.

They build this into a model in which an altered state is plotted on several dimensions rather than on a single scale from "self" to "no self." A typical meditation session, for instance, might involve *more* bodily awareness than usual (through attention to the breath) and fewer self-related thoughts, with no loss of self at all. The conclusion of their analysis is direct:

> The upshot of this discussion is that there is no such thing as “self-loss” or “ego dissolution” in absolute terms.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Non-Dual Awareness and "Pure Consciousness"

Some traditions aim beyond any one component of the self. Practices of :term{id="non_dual_awareness" case="lower"}, found in Tibetan Buddhist Dzogchen and Mahāmudrā and in Hindu Advaita Vedanta, hold that the split between a subject and the objects it experiences is an illusion that can be seen through. The authors examine two readings. On one, ordinary experience carries a special, built-in sense of "mineness," which non-dual practice removes. They find that assumption about ordinary experience controversial. On the other, which they prefer, non-dual states are what remains when self-related thought, and perhaps the bodily components, all switch off, so that nothing in experience marks a boundary between self and world. In one brain-imaging study, the usual opposition between the DMN and attention networks weakened during non-dual meditation, though similar changes have been seen in other kinds of meditation and after psilocybin.

"Pure consciousness," a state said to be entirely empty of content, raises a philosophical problem: if there is something it is like to be in a state, it has *some* content. As the authors put it, "the experience of absence is not equivalent to the absence of experience." They suggest a more modest reading: a state of extreme absorption lacking ordinary content. :term{id="samadhi"} practice, which moves through stages called :term[jhanas]{id="jhana"} toward a "formless" or "objectless" state, is often described this way, but the brain evidence is "still extremely sparse." The review cites [a single-participant study of the jhanas](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/) that found reduced activity in parietal areas that may represent the body's orientation in space, matching the meditator's experience of losing the body's boundaries. A [later case study of one expert](/jhana-brain-networks/), published after this review, found brain networks becoming less compartmentalized and more interconnected, most of all in the formless jhanas; it too followed a single person. Anecdotal reports suggest one psychedelic, 5-MeO-DMT, can produce similarly empty states, which is why the authors flag it as an exception.

### How to Study Self-Loss Better

The review's recommendations are mostly about measurement:

- **Ask about each component separately.** Psychedelic studies have often measured ego dissolution with one item ("I experienced a disintegration of myself or ego"), and the validated Ego Dissolution Inventory doesn't separate narrative from bodily self-loss either. The authors suggest questionnaires with items on self-related thoughts and mental time travel and on body ownership, bodily awareness and self-location, then testing statistically whether these fall into two separate factors.
- **Don't rely on reports alone.** Implicit measures could back them up. The authors suggest measuring peripersonal space, the zone around the body that the brain treats as "near me," which can be measured with an established method and might blur when people report losing their location.
- **Treat reports with care.** They argue the reports are probably not confabulated, since they converge across groups and some come from interviews designed to reduce bias. They still note that most studies are small, and that reporting the *experience of losing* something does not prove it was there to begin with.
- **Mind the design differences.** In a footnote, they point out that meditative self-loss is hard to produce on demand in the lab, while drug effects depend more predictably on dose, and that a true placebo is hard to achieve with drugs whose effects are so obvious.

### A State of Self-Loss Is Not a Selfless Person

The last section, written by Trautwein, Berkovich-Ohana, Carhart-Harris and Roseman, asks whether these experiences leave lasting traces. For meditation, there is some evidence of trait-level change in the narrative self: practitioners show reduced resting DMN activity and connectivity compared with controls, and an 8-week mindfulness course helped people disengage from "narrative self-focus." Trautwein's own study found [hints of a smaller brain bias toward one's own face](/loving-kindness-self-bias/) in long-term loving-kindness meditators. Structural brain findings in the DMN are less consistent ([Fox et al., 2014](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/)). Whether practice lastingly changes the *bodily* self hasn't been systematically studied. For psychedelics, the authors state plainly that there is no evidence ego dissolution has lasting effects on either aspect of the self.

They also contrast how benefits seem to arise. In psychedelic therapy, the intensity of a "mystical-type" experience predicts later improvement. Meditation's long-term benefits, by contrast, "do not appear to be necessarily mediated by intense experiences," but by gradual training of skills such as attention and noticing mind wandering, and perhaps by "decentering," the ability to step back from one's thoughts, which has been found to explain part of the effect of mindfulness programs. The authors speculate that quieting self-related thought might help well-being, given the link between [mind wandering and unhappiness](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/), but say this "remains unsubstantiated by significant evidence."

Nor are changes in the sense of self always welcome. In [the Varieties of Contemplative Experience study](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/) of Western Buddhist meditators who had had difficulties, more than 75% reported changes in their sense of self, with reactions ranging "from neutral curiosity, to bliss and joy, to fear and terror." (Elsewhere on this site, [Castillo, 1990](/depersonalization-and-meditation/) discusses why depersonalization in meditators can feel calm or alarming.)

Finally, the authors warn against equating a temporary experience of selflessness with being selfless, more altruistic or compassionate:

> While it is important to avoid conflating states of self-loss with “selflessness” as a trait or moral construct, it is possible that correlations might exist between these distinct notions, although perhaps only for a subset of highly experienced meditators or drug-users.

## Final Reflection

This review doesn't show that meditation and psychedelics do the same thing to the self, or that either is a shortcut to the other. Its contribution is a set of distinctions. "Losing the self" might mean that self-related thoughts fall silent, that facts about oneself become unavailable, that the body no longer feels like one's own or can't be felt at all, or that the sense of being somewhere disappears, with experience either vivid or nearly empty. Those are different experiences, and studies that lump them together under one questionnaire item can't tell them apart.

For meditators, two points stand out. Dramatic self-loss is reported mostly by highly experienced practitioners, while the authors think meditation's long-term benefits more likely come from steady training than from peak experiences. And the evidence on meditative self-loss is still thin: a handful of small studies, many of single experts, several from the authors' own labs, plus the authors' careful reading of first-person reports. The comparison with psychedelics leans further on anecdotal reports from drug users. The authors are open about this, calling their model "tentative." Read as a map of questions to test rather than a set of findings, it is a useful one.
