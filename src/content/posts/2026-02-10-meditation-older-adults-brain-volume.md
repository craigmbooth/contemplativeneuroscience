## Background Information

Dementia researchers have long looked for everyday habits that might lower risk. Trials of lifestyle programs have tried exercise, diet, brain training and heart-health advice. But depression, stress and anxiety, which are also linked to dementia, had not been targeted directly. Meditation, which trains attention and emotion regulation, seemed a natural candidate.

The brain-imaging case for it was promising but thin. Studies in younger adults have tied meditation to changes in frontal and limbic areas, and a recent meta-analysis named the :term{id="insula" case="lower"} and the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"} (ACC) as the regions most sensitive to meditation training. Both are also especially vulnerable to aging. Two :term[cross-sectional studies]{id="cross_sectional_study"} had found that older expert meditators had better-preserved brains in regions including these two than non-meditators, but one-time comparisons like these can't show that meditation caused the difference. Trials in older adults were scarce, as the authors put it:

> Studies on meditation in elderly populations are sparse, with high risk of bias, and there are no randomized clinical trials (RCTs) with large samples.

The three existing trials with brain scans had used 8-week courses and reported conflicting results. Age-Well, part of the European Medit-Ageing project, was designed to give a far longer course a much tougher test.

## What They Did

The researchers recruited adults aged 65 and over in Caen, France. All were native French speakers, retired for at least a year, living independently, scoring in the normal range on cognitive tests and free of major neurological or psychiatric illness. None had regular meditation experience, and none spoke fluent English. Of 157 people screened, 137 were enrolled between 2016 and 2018 (average age 69.4; 61% women).

In this :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, each person was assigned at random to one of three groups:

- **Meditation:** a secular program designed for the study, combining :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} with :term[loving-kindness]{id="loving_kindness"} and compassion meditation
- **English lessons:** an :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"} of comprehension, writing and speaking exercises, built to match the meditation course in length, class time, homework and number and expertise of teachers
- **No intervention:** a passive control group asked to carry on living as before

Both courses lasted 18 months, with a 2-hour group class each week, at least 20 minutes of practice at home every day, and one 5-hour intensive day. The investigators and the staff who measured the outcomes did not know who was in which group.

Before and after the 18 months, everyone had an MRI scan, used to measure :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"} volume, and a :term{id="pet"} scan. The PET scan used a tracer called florbetapir. Later in the scan it shows deposits of amyloid, one of the Alzheimer disease risk factors the trial tracked, but its first few minutes of signal reflect blood flow (perfusion), which is closely tied to how much energy the brain tissue uses. The authors note that this early signal "remains a surrogate marker" of perfusion and glucose use.

The trial was planned around four main comparisons, each a :term[region of interest]{id="region_of_interest"} test set in advance. Meditation was compared with no intervention on ACC volume and perfusion, because the authors expected both courses to affect the ACC, which is considered a signature of cognitive reserve and has been found to resist aging better in people who speak two languages. Meditation was compared with English lessons on insula volume and perfusion, because they expected only meditation to affect the insula, given its role in emotion and empathy. To guard against false positives across four tests (:term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}), each result had to reach p < .0125 rather than the usual .05. The main secondary outcome was a self-report score combining attention regulation, socioemotional skills and self-knowledge, compared between meditation and English.

## One Big Result: Meditation Didn't Significantly Change Volume or Blood Flow in the Target Regions

Retention was exceptional for an 18-month trial. One participant was excluded after randomization for failing an eligibility rule, and only one other didn't finish: a participant in the no-intervention group who died of a heart attack unrelated to the study. The analysis followed the :term{id="intention_to_treat" case="lower"} principle, keeping everyone in the group they were assigned to.

Over the 18 months, gray matter volume in both regions fell in all three groups, and meditation didn't change that significantly. Compared with no intervention, meditation made no significant difference to ACC volume (difference 0.01, 98.75% :term[confidence interval]{id="confidence_interval"} −0.02 to 0.05; p = .36). Compared with English lessons, it made no significant difference to insula volume (difference 0.01, −0.02 to 0.03; p = .58).

Perfusion came closer. Blood flow held up better in the meditation group in both regions, but neither difference met the trial's threshold: for the ACC against no intervention, 0.02 (−0.01 to 0.05; p = .06), and for the insula against English lessons, 0.02 (−0.01 to 0.05; p = .09). The results looked much the same when the team handled missing data differently, limited the analysis to people who attended at least a fifth of their classes, or split participants by Alzheimer disease risk factors (the APOE ε4 gene, amyloid in the brain, or a family history of dementia).

The authors' summary is plain:

> Findings also show positive behavioral effects of meditation that were not reflected on volume, and not significantly on perfusion, of target brain areas.

This is a well-designed null result. The trial was randomized, long, tested against both an active and a passive control, measured blind, and lost almost no one. It found no evidence that 18 months of meditation preserved the volume of the two regions it targeted, and only a non-significant hint for blood flow.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Older Brains May Be Harder to Change

Why no effect on volume, when these are supposedly the regions most responsive to meditation? The authors' first suggestion is that 18 months may not be enough to alter how these regions age:

> Meditation might alter volume in younger and more plastic brains but not halt the age- and disease-related brain volume decreases at older ages.

They also offer a more sobering reading of the earlier cross-sectional findings. The bigger brains seen in older expert meditators (for example, a [shallower drop in gray matter across ages](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/) in one comparison) might reflect practice when they were younger, decades of intense practice, or other differences between the groups, such as lifestyle. And they note that the null result fits a large, rigorously controlled trial that found eight weeks of MBSR didn't change brain structure in adults aged 25 to 66 ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)).

### The Perfusion Near-Miss, Read With Care

The authors point out that the perfusion differences, though small (0.023 for the ACC and 0.022 for the insula), could represent a substantial gain compared with the decline in the no-intervention group (0.031 in both regions). Assuming a steady decline, they calculate a 74% and 71% reduction in the rate of loss, which "could translate into about 13 months less loss" over the intervention. They suggest a bigger sample or longer follow-up might have produced a significant effect. That is a hypothesis for future trials, not a finding: by the trial's own pre-set standard, the perfusion differences were not significant, and the measure itself is an indirect one.

### The Trial Was Probably Too Small for Subtle Effects

The team sized the trial to detect an :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} of 0.75, drawn from a 2014 meta-analysis of meditation and brain structure ([Fox et al., 2014](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/)). They now think that figure was probably inflated: the meta-analysis mixed before-and-after studies with one-time comparisons of long-term experts, and publication bias, weak methods in earlier studies and general biases in brain imaging all tend to exaggerate effect sizes. In their words, they "may have been underpowered to observe a smaller but still clinically significant effect." In other words, the trial may have lacked the :term{id="statistical_power" case="lower"} to detect a smaller effect. The sample was also unusually healthy and well educated, the authors note, which left little room for improvement.

### Meditators Reported Better Attention and Socioemotional Skills

On the main secondary outcome, meditation beat English lessons. The global self-report score rose more in the meditation group (Cohen's d 0.52, 95% confidence interval 0.19 to 0.85; p = .002), as did the attention regulation subscore (0.38; p = .009) and the socioemotional subscore (0.31). The text gives p = .01 for the socioemotional result and Table 2 gives p = .02; either way it is significant by the usual standard. The self-knowledge subscore fell short (0.28; p = .06).

The pattern differed by subscore. Attention regulation rose only after meditation. Socioemotional scores barely moved in the meditation group but fell in the English group, so the authors read that difference as meditation maintaining these skills rather than raising them. They tie the composite score to a framework of trainable dimensions of well-being ([Dahl et al., 2020](/well-being-four-skills/)).

These results need some caution. The secondary outcomes were defined after the trial began, chosen from a very large set of measures, and they rest on questionnaires filled in by people who knew which course they were taking.

### Older Adults Stuck With It

Over the course, the meditation group attended an average of 62.0 of 72 classes, and the English group 57.9. No serious adverse event was judged to be related to either course. Of 170 adverse events recorded, 41 were serious, spread evenly across the groups (13 meditation, 15 English, 13 no intervention), and the 7 judged related to the study came from procedures such as scans that had to be redone. The paper doesn't report how much home practice people actually did.

## Final Reflection

Small early studies, such as one linking eight weeks of MBSR to denser gray matter in 16 people ([Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)), helped popularize the idea that meditation reshapes the brain. Age-Well is a much sterner test: randomized, 18 months long, with an engaging matched course as a comparison, and almost no dropouts. Its answer for the two regions it targeted was no significant effect on volume, and a perfusion difference that leaned toward meditation without reaching significance.

That doesn't rule out benefits elsewhere in the brain or in other measures, which the team plans to analyze, and the meditators did report gains in attention and socioemotional skills. But for anyone hoping that a meditation course late in life will measurably preserve brain tissue, this careful trial is a reason for caution. If such an effect exists, it is probably smaller than earlier studies suggested.
