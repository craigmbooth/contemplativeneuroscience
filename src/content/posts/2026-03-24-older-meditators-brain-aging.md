## Background Information

As we age, the brain loses volume and uses less energy. Brain scans show both: :term{id="gray_matter" case="lower"} shrinks, and the amount of glucose that brain tissue burns falls, especially in frontal regions, the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex" case="lower"} and the :term{id="insula" case="lower"}. Lower volume and metabolism in certain regions, along with deposits of :term{id="amyloid_beta" case="lower"}, are linked to a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease.

The authors start from the idea that some of this risk can be changed. Stress, depression, anxiety and poor sleep are all risk factors for Alzheimer's disease, and meditation is practiced partly to reduce stress and regulate emotion. So could a lifetime of meditation help protect the aging brain?

Earlier evidence was thin. Studies of younger and middle-aged meditators had found structural differences in frontal and limbic regions and in the insula, areas that are also sensitive to aging. Only one research team had looked at older people, comparing meditators and non-meditators aged 24 to 77, and found that gray matter differed less with age in the meditators ([Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/)). No study had measured brain glucose metabolism in meditators of any age.

This French pilot study set out to take a first look, using two kinds of brain scan in a small group of older expert meditators.

## What They Did

The meditators were six people aged 61 to 70 (three women, three men) who had trained in Buddhist traditions: Korean :term{id="zen"}, Tibetan Buddhism and :term{id="vipassana"}. All practiced :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"}-related, :term[loving-kindness]{id="loving_kindness"} and compassion meditation. To count as experts they needed at least 10,000 hours of formal practice; in fact they had between 15,000 and 30,000 hours over the previous 10 to 40 years, about half of it on retreat.

They were compared with controls from a larger brain-imaging study of aging and Alzheimer's disease in Caen, France. Everyone was screened for neurological and psychiatric illness and scored in the normal range on cognitive tests. The controls served two purposes:

- **67 controls aged 55 to 75**, the direct comparison group. They were the same average age as the meditators (64.8) and scored the same on a standard screening test of thinking and memory, but had less education (12.1 years on average, against 16.2).
- **All 186 controls, aged 20 to 87**, used to map which brain regions change most with age.

Each person had two scans on the same machines at the same center:

- **Structural MRI**, to measure gray matter volume across the brain.
- **:term{id="fdg_pet"}**, a :term{id="pet"} scan that maps how much glucose each brain region uses at rest.

Four of the meditators and 61 of the older controls also had a PET scan for amyloid.

For the main analysis, the team scored each meditator's brain, point by point, against the 67 older controls, adjusting for age and education. They then looked for regions where the six meditators, as a group, stood consistently above the controls, using a standard correction for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"} across the brain. Separately, they mapped where gray matter and metabolism declined most with age across all 186 controls, to see whether the meditators' differences fell in those regions.

## One Big Result: The Meditators Had More Gray Matter and Higher Metabolism, Mostly in Regions That Decline Most With Age

Compared with the 67 older controls, the meditators' scores were mostly higher across the brain, and almost entirely higher for glucose metabolism. Six clusters stood out, three for each kind of scan:

- **More gray matter** in the :term{id="vmpfc"} and anterior cingulate cortex on both sides of the brain, and in the left and right :term{id="tpj"}, an area that includes the back of the insula.
- **Higher glucose metabolism** in the same ventromedial prefrontal and anterior cingulate area, the right insula (mainly its front part), and the :term{id="pcc"} extending into the :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"}.

No region showed less gray matter or lower metabolism in the meditators. Even so, the meditators weren't off the charts: in each region their values fell within the upper range of the controls', except in the prefrontal–cingulate region for metabolism, where they were above the highest control.

The second analysis linked these regions to aging. Across the 186 controls, gray matter and metabolism fell most with age in the insula and in medial frontal, cingulate and temporal areas. The meditators' differences lay mainly in those same areas; the exception was the posterior cingulate cortex, where metabolism declined only moderately with age. Of the regions where meditators differed, 75% (for gray matter) and 60% (for metabolism) overlapped with the 25% of brain points showing the strongest age effects. When the team plotted each region against age, the meditators sat higher than expected for people of their age.

The meditators differed from the controls in more than meditation. Besides their extra education, all four who had an amyloid scan were amyloid-negative, while 10 of 61 older controls (16%) were amyloid-positive. So the team re-ran the comparison in two ways: adjusting for education and every lifestyle measure, and comparing the meditators with a subgroup of 31 amyloid-negative controls who had at least 12 years of education. Both times, all six clusters still differed between the groups, with p-values ranging from 0.00008 to 0.02. These checks re-tested the six clusters the main analysis had already found.

The authors don't oversell what six people can show:

> The findings need to be interpreted with caution given the small sample size of the elderly expert meditators.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### "Age-Associated Changes" From a Single Scan

The paper's title speaks of reduced age-associated brain *changes*, but nobody was scanned twice. The study shows that six older meditators had more gray matter and higher metabolism than their peers in regions that, across other people of different ages, are smaller and less active in older people. That fits less decline, but the meditators may also have started out different. The authors say as much:

> The cross-sectional nature of the study is also a limitation because of possible cohort effects. Only longitudinal studies would allow to test for a causal relationship between meditation and brain volume preservation in elderly populations.

They also note a risk that runs the other way from the usual worry about small studies. Besides missing real effects through low :term{id="statistical_power" case="lower"}, small samples can produce "inflated or spurious effects." They point to a :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"} of brain-structure studies in meditators in which the smallest studies reported the largest effects ([Fox et al., 2014](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/)).

### Why These Regions?

The anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex together form the :term{id="salience_network" case="lower"}, which the authors describe as involved in awareness of one's own body, breathing, heart rate, pain and emotional state. Both regions are among the most consistent findings in earlier brain-structure studies of meditators, and the authors see the result as in line with what meditation practice would be expected to train.

The ventromedial prefrontal cortex, temporo-parietal junction and posterior cingulate cortex belong to the :term{id="dmn"}, which is active during self-related thought and mind-wandering. Brain-imaging studies have found less DMN activity in meditators. The authors offer a speculative link to aging: lifelong high activity in DMN hubs is thought to make them more vulnerable to aging and Alzheimer's disease, so:

> It is thus possible that DMN structures are relatively preserved in elderly expert meditators because they have endured lifelong reduced DMN activity compared to controls.

This is a hypothesis to explain the pattern; the study didn't measure DMN activity.

### Thinking, Lifestyle and Sleep: Mostly Small Samples and Trends

The team also compared cognitive test scores and questionnaires on lifestyle, diet and sleep. They called a difference significant only if p < 0.01, and "marginal" if p < 0.05.

- **Thinking and memory:** no significant differences. On an :term{id="episodic_memory" case="lower"} test, the meditators recalled more words on average (20.3 against 16.5 from two 16-word lists), a marginal effect (p = 0.02) that faded after adjusting for education (p = 0.2). The authors note that six people may be too few to detect a difference.
- **Lifestyle:** the meditators reported taking part more often in mentally stimulating leisure activities before age 30 and tended to score higher on other lifestyle measures, but after adjusting for education only a trend toward closer adherence to a Mediterranean diet remained.
- **Sleep:** on a questionnaire about the previous five years, adapted from the :term{id="psqi"}, the meditators reported much shorter spells awake during the night (5.0 minutes against 34.7). This was significant before adjusting for education (p = 0.009) and marginal after (p = 0.03). The other sleep measures, including the questionnaire's overall sleep quality score, did not differ significantly.

The lifestyle and sleep comparisons used smaller control groups (43, and 50 or 51, people) because not every control had filled in the questionnaires.

### Only Faint Differences in the Hippocampus

The :term{id="hippocampus"}, central to memory, is often reported to differ in meditators, and an early MBSR study reported denser gray matter there after eight weeks of training ([Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)). Here, the team traced its subregions by hand on special high-resolution scans in the six meditators and 53 controls. The differences were weak: the meditators tended to have a larger CA1 subfield (the authors call the effect marginal), and to a lesser degree part of the subiculum. The authors note that CA1 is the subregion most sensitive to Alzheimer's disease in its early stages, but suggest that either the sample was too small or long-term meditation affects the hippocampus less than other regions, at least in older meditators.

### How This Fits With an Earlier Study

The authors describe the earlier study by Luders and colleagues as showing "markedly less age-related brain atrophy" in meditators across extended brain areas. Our [summary of that study](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/) found a clear difference in total gray matter, but its regional differences appeared only without the study's strict statistical correction.

## Final Reflection

This is a first look, and the authors frame it that way. Six highly experienced meditators, scanned once, had more gray matter and higher brain metabolism than their peers, and most of the differences fell in the regions that age hits hardest. It was also the first study to measure brain glucose metabolism in meditators, and the metabolism differences were more marked than the volume differences.

But six people practicing for decades can differ from their peers in many ways besides meditation: education, diet, sleep, health and whatever led them to meditate in the first place. The adjustments for education, lifestyle and amyloid help, but can't rule all of that out, and with a sample this small the size of the differences may be overstated.

The authors call for larger groups of older meditators and for studies that follow meditation newcomers over time. Several of the same researchers, including Chételat and Lutz, went on to run such a test. In the Age-Well randomized trial, 137 adults aged 65 and over, new to meditation, took 18 months of meditation training, English lessons or no training. Gray matter volume in the anterior cingulate and insula declined no differently with meditation, and blood flow there held up slightly better, but not significantly ([Chételat et al., 2022](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/)). Telomeres, a marker of cellular aging, also shortened no less with meditation ([Kaliman et al., 2025](/meditation-older-adults-telomeres/)). Whether a lifetime of practice protects the aging brain, or whether people with well-preserved brains are the ones who keep meditating into their sixties, remains open.
