## Background Information

What happens in the brain when a meditator moves through the deepest states of concentration? In the Theravada Buddhist tradition, these states are called the :term[jhanas]{id="jhana"}: a sequence of eight absorptions reached through sustained, focused attention.

The first four, the "form" jhanas, are traditionally described as moving from bliss toward contentment and then equanimity. The last four, the "formless" jhanas, leave sensory objects behind, and experience takes on qualities such as boundless space, with a growing sense of the self falling away.

Neuroscience has barely looked at these states. The authors count only three earlier neuroimaging studies of jhana, including a [2013 study of a single meditator](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/) already covered on this site. Part of the challenge is that reports of inner experience can be unreliable. So the team used :term{id="neurophenomenology" case="lower"}, an approach that pairs careful first-person descriptions of experience with brain measurements and uses each to help interpret the other.

The study tested a specific idea. Some current theories picture the brain as a hierarchy: high-level networks such as the default mode network (:term{id="dmn"}), active during self-referential thought and mind-wandering, and the frontoparietal network, involved in cognitive control, send predictions down to sensory areas. Focused-attention meditation is thought to loosen this top-down control. If so, jhana might show up as a flattening of that hierarchy.

## What They Did

The participant was a 52-year-old meditation teacher with more than 25 years of practice in several traditions, including :term{id="vipassana"} and jhana. Rather than recruit a group, the researchers studied one expert intensively: five days of :term{id="fmri"} in an ultra-high-field 7-tesla scanner and five separate days of :term{id="eeg"}, with up to three or four hours of recording each day.

They recorded 27 jhana runs in the scanner and 29 with EEG. In each run, which lasted about 14 minutes on average, the meditator moved through all eight jhanas in sequence, pressing a button at each transition up to the sixth. He didn't mark the seventh and eighth, because pressing a button would have pulled him out of those states. The authors describe how the sequence unfolded:

> In general, the participant intended/inclined their mind to begin a progression through the ACAM-J and the mind did as intended in its own time.

(ACAM-J, short for "advanced concentrative absorption meditation – jhanas," is the authors' term for these states.)

Because an expert may drift into meditation during ordinary rest, the team built two control tasks instead: silently counting backward from 10,000 in steps of five, and quietly recalling events from the past two weeks. Each lasted eight minutes with eyes closed. After every run, the meditator rated his experience on scales developed with his input, covering bliss, equanimity, the stability and width of attention, and "grades of formlessness."

## One Big Result: Jhana Was Linked to Less Segregated, More Interconnected Brain Networks

The team looked at two fMRI measures. *Modularity* describes how much a network splits into tight clusters that mostly talk among themselves, a sign of hierarchical organization. *Global functional connectivity* describes how closely a region's activity rises and falls with the rest of the brain (see :term[functional connectivity]{id="functional_connectivity"}).

Compared with the two control tasks combined, modularity was lower during jhana in four of seven large-scale networks: the DMN, frontoparietal, visual and salience networks. Global connectivity was higher in six of the seven (all but the affective network) and across the brain as a whole. The changes were strongest in the formless jhanas, and the DMN in particular became more connected with other networks. The authors read this as support for the hierarchy idea:

> Our results show that advanced meditation practices markedly dysregulate high-level brain systems via practices of enhanced attention to sensations, corroborating recent neurocognitive theories of meditation as the deconstruction of the brain's cortical hierarchy.

The EEG recordings told a compatible story. During jhana, rhythmic electrical activity was weaker across much of the scalp, most clearly in the slower delta, theta and :term{id="alpha" case="lower"} frequency bands. At the same time, brain signals became more varied, as measured by :term{id="lempel_ziv_complexity"}, a count of how many distinct patterns a signal contains.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Bliss Had Its Own Neural Signature

The second jhana is traditionally marked by intense, pleasant bodily bliss. Run by run, higher bliss ratings in this jhana went with higher DMN connectivity and lower DMN modularity. On the EEG, bliss went with weaker :term{id="beta" case="lower"} and :term{id="gamma" case="lower"} activity, rhythms the authors link to the brain's predictions and active thinking, and with a modest *drop* in signal complexity, the opposite of the overall trend.

> This suggests that during ACAM-J2, the brain experiences a state of relaxation and "letting go", which may be related to the pleasant subjective experience of bliss/joy.

### Stable Attention and Wide Attention Looked Different in the Brain

The meditator rated two qualities of attention separately: how steady it was and how wide it was. Steadier attention went with *more* alpha and theta power, while wider attention went with *less* alpha power and more complex signals. The authors take this as a sign that stability and width are distinct, both in experience and in the brain. In the formless jhanas, stronger formlessness ratings also went with more alpha power.

### The Choice of Control Task Mattered

Many fMRI differences were clear against the counting task but weaker against memory recall, where increased connectivity appeared only in the DMN and visual network during the formless jhanas. The EEG results, by contrast, held up against both tasks. The ratings also suggest that some jhana-like experience crept into the first control runs, a reminder of how hard it is to find a truly "neutral" comparison for an expert meditator.

## Final Reflection

This study offers an unusually detailed look at the jhanas, with dozens of runs and the meditator's own ratings tied to each one. In this practitioner, the jhanas were reliably accompanied by a loosening of the brain's usual network divisions, and specific experiences, especially bliss, tracked specific brain measures.

The findings fit the idea that deep concentration relaxes the brain's top-down hierarchy, but they can't establish it. They come from one person, moving through the states in a fixed order, compared with only a few control runs recorded at different times. Whether other practitioners show the same patterns is the obvious next question, and one the authors themselves raise.
