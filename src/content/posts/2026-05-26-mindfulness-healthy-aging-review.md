## Background Information

Getting older brings well-documented changes in attention. Drawing on decades of work by the attention researcher Raja Parasuraman and others, Stephanie Fountain-Zaragoza and Ruchika Prakash of Ohio State University describe how older adults are worse at shifting attention from one place to another, less able to widen or narrow their focus on demand, and worse at some kinds of :term[executive control]{id="executive_function"}, such as juggling two tasks at once. In the brain, older adults tend to recruit attention regions more broadly, and they quiet the :term[default mode network]{id="dmn"}, a set of regions active during rest and self-focused thought, less well during demanding tasks.

Emotion tells a different story. Older adults often regulate their emotions as well as or better than younger people, and according to :term{id="socioemotional_selectivity_theory" case="lower"} they increasingly prioritize emotional goals. In an earlier paper, Prakash's group proposed that mindfulness training is well suited to later life for both reasons: it exercises attention, by repeatedly noticing when the mind has wandered and bringing it back, and it builds on emotion-regulation skills that age tends to preserve.

This review asks how well the evidence supported that idea by 2016. It covers three areas that matter for healthy aging: attention, psychological well-being, and :term{id="inflammation" case="lower"}, which is linked to social isolation and to a range of health problems.

## What They Did

The authors searched PubMed, PsycInfo and Web of Science using keywords such as mindfulness, older adults, aging, attentional control, cognition, well-being and inflammation, and checked the reference lists of the articles they found. They included English-language articles published before September 2016. Rather than pooling results in a :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"}, they described the studies in turn and listed them in a table, grouped by outcome, with each study's design, sample, program and main findings.

That table holds 22 distinct studies (a few appear under more than one outcome). They range widely in design. Some simply asked whether older adults who describe themselves as more mindful, on questionnaires, do better on attention tasks or report better well-being. Some compared long-term meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time, a :term[cross-sectional]{id="cross_sectional_study"} design. Seven were feasibility studies that tested a program, usually without a comparison group, and six were randomized trials. Most of the programs were 8-week courses of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (:term{id="mbsr"}) or Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (:term{id="mbct"}). Samples included healthy community volunteers, but also older adults with anxiety, depression, chronic pain, poor sleep or recent bereavement.

## One Big Result: Mostly Positive Findings, but Few Fair Tests

The authors' overall verdict balances promise against a thin evidence base:

> Although the majority of the reviewed studies provide positive results for mindfulness training in each of these domains, the field is currently limited in its scope and much more work is needed in order to establish the causal impact of mindfulness practice on these outcomes.

The recurring problem is the comparison group. A program that gathers older adults in a group every week, gives them homework and is billed as improving attention or mood can help for reasons that have nothing to do with mindfulness: social contact, practice of any kind, expectations, :term{id="demand_characteristics" case="lower"} and simply getting better at tests taken twice. A :term[wait-list group]{id="wait_list_control"} controls for none of these; an :term{id="active_control_group" case="lower"} doing a similar but non-mindfulness program controls for many of them. Only three of the six randomized trials used one, along with a quasi-randomized trial and a brief lab study. Across the three areas, the pattern looked like this.

**Attention.** Questionnaire studies were mixed: self-rated mindfulness went with better set-shifting (switching between tasks) in one study of 73 older adults but showed no link to working memory, inhibitory control or set-shifting in another of 48. In one-time comparisons, older adults with more than 10 years of meditation did better than non-meditators of the same age on most attention tests, and older meditators showed a smaller :term{id="attentional_blink" case="lower"} than both their peers and young non-meditators. Such comparisons can't show that meditation caused the difference.

The trials were mixed too. In the largest, with 201 older adults, MBSR improved the ratio of Part B to Part A on the :term{id="trail_making_test"}, a measure of mental flexibility, more than waiting did, but the difference was gone 3 and 24 weeks later. In a quasi-randomized trial of 97 older adults, MBSR did no better than a reading and relaxation group on any measure. A study of anxious older adults found improvements on almost every cognitive test after MBSR but had no control group. The authors suggest that benefits seen in the wait-list and uncontrolled studies may have come from these general features of taking part rather than from mindfulness itself. The oldest trial, from 1989, did find that :term{id="transcendental_meditation"} and a "mindfulness" method focused on creative problem solving both improved learning and one measure of flexibility more than relaxation or no treatment, with transcendental meditation ahead.

**Well-being.** MBSR reduced loneliness more than a wait-list in 40 older adults ([Creswell et al., 2012](/mbsr-loneliness-older-adults/)). Uncontrolled studies reported large improvements: after MBSR, the number of older adults with clinically significant depression and anxiety fell by more than half in one study of 141, and worry eased in another. Qualitative studies, built from diaries and focus groups, described better mood, sleep and coping. But when 40 older adults with chronic low back pain were randomly assigned to a mindfulness program or a health education group, both groups improved on quality of life, disability and pain, with no difference between them. The authors point out that the social support participants valued in the qualitative studies is not specific to mindfulness, and that such effects "are undetectable when mindfulness training is not tested against an active comparison condition."

**Inflammation.** In the same loneliness trial, MBSR turned down activity of genes controlled by :term{id="nf_kb"}, a master switch for inflammation, compared with the wait-list. The review describes a significant drop in :term[C-reactive protein (CRP)]{id="c_reactive_protein"}, though the trial's own report describes it as a trend that fell short of significance, and :term[interleukin-6 (IL-6)]{id="il_6"} didn't change. A second MBSR trial also found no effect on IL-6. The most telling result came from [a sleep trial](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/) of 49 older adults with poor sleep, randomly assigned to six weeks of mindful awareness practices or sleep hygiene education. Mindfulness improved sleep quality, insomnia, depression and fatigue more, but NF-κB activity fell significantly in both groups. The authors draw the lesson directly:

> This finding highlights the sizable influences that can result from non-specific factors arising from participation in group-based interventions.

The abstract's statement that improvements in inflammation accompany the gains in well-being therefore rests on few studies: one wait-list trial with a gene-activity result, two trials in which IL-6 didn't change, and an NF-κB drop that a sleep-education class matched.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Brain Studies Were Scarce

Only one randomized trial in older adults had measured brain activity. In it, MBSR participants showed less of a shift toward greater right-sided frontal :term[alpha]{id="alpha"} activity, a pattern linked to withdrawal, than wait-listed participants, and kept the left-sided pattern linked to approach, though the difference had faded by follow-up. Two cross-sectional studies hinted that mindfulness might protect the aging brain: in 25 older adults, higher self-rated mindfulness went with stronger connectivity in the default mode network, centered on the :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"} and :term[precuneus]{id="precuneus"}; and in a comparison of Zen meditators with non-meditators, gray matter volume and attention declined with age in the non-meditators but not in the meditators. The authors note that it's unclear how old the oldest participants in the second study were (the meditators averaged 37), and that both studies were one-time snapshots.

### Mind Wandering Falls With Age

:term[Mind wandering]{id="mind_wandering"} decreases markedly with age, the authors note, and in their own earlier work older adults with higher self-rated mindfulness showed the least task-unrelated mind wandering. They also mention an unpublished study of theirs, then under review, in which four weeks of mindfulness training reduced task-unrelated mind wandering during a cognitive task compared with an active control group. They suggest future studies test whether mindfulness adds to the age-related drop in mind wandering, and whether that helps attention.

### One Practice Was Linked to a Weaker Immune Response

A trial that tracked which parts of MBSR older adults practiced found that more yoga and sitting meditation went with higher levels of IGF-1, a growth factor involved in forming new neurons and preserving cognition that declines with age, and more yoga with a bigger rise in positive mood. But more :term[body scanning]{id="body_scan"} was linked to weaker antibody responses three weeks after the course, an effect the authors call negative, which had disappeared by 24 weeks. These were associations within the MBSR group, not comparisons with a control group.

### A Small Inconsistency

The review is not entirely consistent about one trial of MBCT in bereaved older adults. Its table lists better working memory after training in the mindfulness group than in the wait-list group, while its text says the study "found no significant improvements in attentional control, measured via working memory, compared to a waitlist comparison group."

### What the Authors Want Next

The authors call for randomized trials with both an active comparison group, matched for length, group size and instructor expertise, and a wait-list group; standardized training manuals; attention measures grounded in research on aging, such as tests of shifting and scaling attention and of :term[vigilance]{id="vigilance_decrement"}; fairly homogeneous samples of healthy older adults; and longer follow-up to see whether any benefits last and carry over to everyday life, such as understanding medical information.

## Final Reflection

This review is most useful as a map of how little had been tested. Of 22 studies, seven involved no training at all, looking instead at self-rated mindfulness or at existing meditators; six of the seven feasibility studies had no comparison group; and only three randomized trials pitted mindfulness against an active alternative. In those three, mindfulness came out ahead on some measures (learning and flexibility in 1989; sleep, depression and fatigue in the sleep trial) but not on others, including the pain and quality-of-life outcomes in the back pain trial and the inflammatory gene activity that sleep hygiene classes matched.

The authors end on an optimistic note:

> we assert that mindfulness represents a potential intervention for not only reducing emotional distress in older adults, but for allowing them to flourish.

"Potential" is the key word. The evidence they gathered supports trying mindfulness with older adults, many of whom report finding it helpful, but it doesn't yet show that mindfulness itself, rather than the group, the routine or the expectations around it, sharpens attention or calms inflammation in later life. The authors' own prescription, trials with matched comparison programs, is the right test for anyone weighing these claims.
