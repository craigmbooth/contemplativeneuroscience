## Background Information

Children who can manage their attention, feelings and impulses tend to do better, at school and long afterward. The authors cite long-term studies in which self-regulation in childhood predicted health, financial stability and education in adulthood, and children who could wait longer for a reward at age 4 had better attention and self-control as teenagers. These skills rest on what psychologists call :term[executive functions]{id="executive_function"}, such as cognitive flexibility and inhibitory control, which develop rapidly in early childhood. Yet, the authors point out, they are rarely taught explicitly in school.

Their candidate for teaching them was :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"}. In their description, mindfulness practice trains attention by noticing when the mind has wandered from its focus and bringing it back, a skill that overlaps with self-regulation. The same practice can also be used to cultivate kindness and care. In a curriculum for young children, the paper explains:

> Young children, for instance, engage in setting daily intentions and age-appropriate practices to extend care and well wishes.

The authors point to adult research on compassion training, including a study in which two weeks of practice made people more generous to a stranger ([Weng et al. 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)). Whether something similar works in a classroom of four-year-olds is a different question. Earlier studies of mindfulness with children, they note, were mostly limited by a lack of randomized trials and by relying on questionnaires. This study, from the Center for Investigating Healthy Minds at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, aimed to address both, combining teacher ratings with behavioral tasks the children did themselves.

## What They Did

Seven preschool classrooms in six public elementary schools in a medium-sized Midwestern city took part. In these schools, 37.9% of children were considered socioeconomically disadvantaged. Of 99 children invited, the parents of 68 enrolled them. The children were about four and a half years old on average, half were girls, and 59% were white.

Randomization was by classroom, not by child, which makes this a :term[cluster-randomized trial]{id="cluster_randomized_trial"}. Three classrooms (30 children) got the Kindness Curriculum, and four classrooms (38 children) formed a :term[wait-list control group]{id="wait_list_control"}.

The curriculum was built on mindfulness practice for attention and :term{id="emotion_regulation" case="lower"}, combined with kindness practices such as empathy, gratitude and sharing, taught through children's books, music and movement. Experienced mindfulness instructors taught it, in a secular way, to the whole class during the regular school day: two lessons of 20 to 30 minutes a week for 12 weeks, about 10 hours in all.

Before and after the 12 weeks, the researchers measured:

- **Social competence**, rated by the children's teachers on a 6-point scale, with two parts: prosocial behavior (such as showing empathy for others' feelings) and emotion regulation (such as calming down when upset).
- **Sharing**, with a task designed for this study. In four rounds, each child got 10 stickers to divide between an envelope marked "me" and an envelope for someone else: their most-liked classmate, their least-liked classmate, an unfamiliar child and a sick child. The score was the average number kept.
- **Self-control**, with a :term[delay of gratification]{id="delay_of_gratification"} task of nine choices between a small reward now (one item of food, crayons or tokens) and a bigger one later (two, three or five).
- **Cognitive flexibility**, with a computerized card-sorting task in which children sort pictures by one feature, such as shape, and then switch to another, such as color.
- **Inhibitory control**, with a computerized :term[flanker task]{id="flanker_test"}: children pressed a button to show which way a middle fish or arrow pointed while those on either side pointed the same or the opposite way.

Finally, the researchers collected the children's report-card grades for the second half of the school year, in five areas. These were available only at the end, so they couldn't be compared with grades before the curriculum.

## One Big Result: The Curriculum Group Gained a Little More in Social Skills, and Didn't Become Less Generous

Teacher ratings of social competence rose a lot in both groups over the 12 weeks (within-group effect sizes of d = 1.31 and 1.05). The curriculum group's rose more: from 3.23 to 4.32 on the 6-point scale, against 3.10 to 3.91 for the wait-list group. The difference between the groups was statistically significant for the total score (p = .011) and for both parts, prosocial behavior (p = .041) and emotion regulation (p = .002). Its size was small, an :term[effect size]{id="effect_size"} of d = 0.26 for the total score. When the analysis also accounted for children's gender and age, the prosocial behavior part was no longer significant (p = .105); the total score and emotion regulation still were.

The sharing task gave the result most directly about kindness, though it came from the comparison group changing. Children on the wait-list kept more stickers for themselves at the end than at the start, 6.20 of 10 on average against 5.42, a significant rise (p = .029). The curriculum group's change, from 4.84 to 5.13, was not significant. The difference between the groups' changes was significant (p = .013), with a small effect size (d = 0.33), and it held when gender and age were taken into account. In the abstract's words, "the control group exhibited more selfish behavior over time."

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Better Report Cards in Three of Five Areas

At the end of the year, about three months after the curriculum finished, teachers gave the curriculum children higher grades (on a 4-point scale) in three areas: approaches to learning (3.55 against 3.33, d = 0.54), health and physical development (3.60 against 3.40, d = 0.56) and social and emotional development (3.51 against 3.16, d = 0.97, the largest difference in the study). The groups didn't differ in cognition and general knowledge, where the wait-list group was slightly ahead (d = −0.30), or in language and communication (d = 0.09). Because there were no grades from before the curriculum, these show where the groups ended up, not how much each changed.

### The Thinking Tasks Didn't Show Significant Differences

None of the three tests of self-control and thinking skills showed a significant difference between the groups. On delay of gratification, the curriculum children's choices to wait for the bigger reward rose more than the wait-list group's (within-group d = 0.42 against 0.19), but the difference between them was small (d = 0.23) and not significant (p = .244). On the card-sorting task, the curriculum group improved more on the trials after the switch in sorting rule (d = 0.43, p = .222). Only 44 children were included in that comparison, because many didn't sort accurately enough to reach the switch, and across all trials the wait-list group was slightly ahead (d = −0.13). On the flanker task, both groups improved, the wait-list group a little more (d = −0.17).

The abstract presents the delay and card-sorting results as "small to medium effects favoring the KC group." Those effect sizes are as reported, but neither was statistically significant, and the medium-sized card-sorting result came from the subset of trials and children that reached the switch. The authors' own summary in the discussion is careful:

> Overall, however, inspection of effect sizes lends support to a general pattern of change favoring the intervention group, even though not all outcomes were significant in the omnibus test of the group by time interaction, which highlights the need for larger samples in future studies.

### Children Who Started Lower Gained More

The researchers expected the curriculum to help most the children who began with weaker skills, and in a :term[moderation analysis]{id="moderation_analysis"} it did. Curriculum children with lower teacher-rated social competence, or lower cognitive flexibility or inhibitory control on the computer tasks, at the start gained more in social competence than similar children on the wait-list. The authors note that earlier research has found the same pattern of larger gains for children who start further behind.

### Teachers Knew Which Children Were in the Program

The teacher ratings and report-card grades came from teachers who knew whether their class was getting the curriculum. The authors argue this didn't simply produce a halo effect: the grades were given three months after the program ended, they weren't higher in every area, and the gains in social competence varied with children's starting skills rather than rising evenly. Still, teacher-rated outcomes clustered by classroom. The researchers measured this with the :term[intraclass correlation]{id="intraclass_correlation"}, the share of differences between children that lay between classrooms. It was 0.19 to 0.35 for the social competence ratings and 0.17 to 0.48 for grades, but close to zero for most of the behavioral tasks. The authors concluded that classroom effects alone don't explain the pattern of results, but added a caution:

> However, the sizeable ICCs for some teacher-rated outcomes highlights potential limitations of relying on teacher-based measures when conducting research in schools.

With three classrooms in one group and four in the other, the paper says there were too few classrooms to use multilevel models, the kind of analysis that accounts for children being grouped in classrooms.

## Final Reflection

This was a small, early randomized test of teaching mindfulness and kindness to very young children in ordinary public schools. About 10 hours of lessons over 12 weeks went with slightly larger gains in teacher-rated social skills, better end-of-year grades in three of five areas, and children who held on to their generosity in a sharing game while children on the wait-list grew a little less generous. The behavioral tests of self-control and thinking skills, which didn't depend on teachers' judgments, pointed in mixed directions and none reached significance.

The strengths are real. The program was delivered in regular classrooms, the groups were randomized and similar at the start, and the authors report effect sizes for every outcome, including those that went the other way. But the evidence comes from seven classrooms, the clearest results depend on teachers who knew which children were in the program, the grades have no starting point to compare with, and the wait-list group didn't receive any alternative activity. As the authors say, larger studies with more classrooms and longer follow-up were needed.

The sharing result fits other studies on this site in which brief practice changed how people treat others, from [two weeks of compassion training](/compassion-alters-altruism/) that increased adults' generosity to a [mindfulness app](/mindfulness-app-compassion/) after which more students gave up their seat to someone in pain. Here the change came in four-year-olds, measured with stickers, and it showed up mainly as the comparison group becoming less generous rather than the curriculum group becoming more so.
