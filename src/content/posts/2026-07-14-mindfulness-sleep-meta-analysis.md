## Background Information

If a busy, circling mind is part of what keeps people awake, could training that mind help them sleep?

Poor sleep is common and costly. The authors cite estimates that sleep disturbance affects 10–25% of the general population, and accumulated sleep loss is linked to mood and anxiety disorders, cognitive problems, heart disease, and obesity. Sleep medications remain the usual first-line treatment, but they carry risks of misuse, drug interactions, and side effects. :term[Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia]{id="cbt_i"} (CBT-I), a structured talk therapy that reshapes sleep habits and unhelpful thoughts about sleep, works well but can be expensive and hard to find.

:term{id="mindfulness"} offers another possible route. Often described as paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and without judgment, it has been shown to reduce rumination (repetitive, stuck thinking) and emotional reactivity, both of which can feed sleeplessness.

Earlier :term[meta-analyses]{id="meta_analysis"}, studies that statistically combine the results of many trials, disagreed, with findings ranging from no effect to a moderate benefit. Some included non-randomized studies. Others lumped :term[waitlist groups]{id="wait_list_control"} together with active comparison programs, which made it hard to separate meditation's own contribution from the general boost of attention, support, and expectation that comes with joining any program.

## What They Did

The team, based at the U.S. National Institutes of Health, searched four research databases for studies published through May 2018. From 3,303 records, they kept 18 :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial" case="lower" plural} published between 2010 and 2018, with 1,654 adult participants in total. Every trial enrolled people with clinically significant sleep problems and taught an in-person, multi-session mindfulness program. One of them was the older-adults trial covered here in [Black et al., 2015](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/).

Half the programs were :term{id="mbsr"} (9 trials), followed by :term[mindfulness-based cognitive therapy]{id="mbct"} (MBCT, which blends mindfulness with cognitive therapy; 3 trials) and mind–body bridging (3 trials). Weekly classes ran 1–2.5 hours for 2–16 weeks, and all trials encouraged home practice.

The key design choice was to leave out waitlist and usual-care comparisons, keeping only trials with an :term[active control group]{id="active_control_group"}, and to split those by what the comparison group did:

- **Established sleep treatments** (7 trials): treatments already known to help sleep, such as CBT-I, sleep medication, exercise, or :term{id="progressive_muscle_relaxation" case="lower"}.
- **Matched programs** (11 trials): classes matched for time and attention but not built to treat sleep, such as health education or :term{id="sleep_hygiene" case="lower"} classes.

Because trials used different questionnaires, mainly the :term[Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index]{id="psqi"} (PSQI), the team converted results to a standardized :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} (Hedges' *g*), which expresses the difference between groups in standard-deviation units. They also graded how much confidence the evidence deserved.

## One Big Result: Mindfulness Outperformed Matched Programs, and the Gap Held at Follow-Up

Across the 11 trials with time- and attention-matched comparison programs, mindfulness improved sleep quality more than the comparison did. Right after the programs ended, the effect size was 0.33 (95% :term{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} 0.17 to 0.48), conventionally considered small. Put another way, the mindfulness groups improved by about a third of a standard deviation more than the comparison groups.

Three of these trials also checked in 5–12 months later. There the effect size was 0.54 (95% confidence interval 0.24 to 0.84), in the medium range. The trials largely agreed with one another, and the team rated this body of evidence as moderate in strength.

This comparison matters because the control programs offered the same class time, group contact, and instructor attention. A benefit beyond them suggests that something about the mindfulness practice itself, and not only the experience of joining a program, may help. The authors call the long-term numbers "preliminary evidence for a long-term effect," a fair caution given that only three trials contributed.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### No Clear Difference From Established Sleep Treatments

Against treatments already known to help sleep, mindfulness showed no meaningful difference either way: an effect size of −0.03 after treatment and −0.14 at follow-up, with wide confidence intervals that crossed zero. The team rated this evidence as low in strength, partly because the trials disagreed sharply.

Much of that disagreement came from one trial comparing MBSR with CBT-I in people with cancer and insomnia, where CBT-I came out well ahead and half the MBSR group dropped out. With that trial set aside, the results became consistent and tilted slightly toward mindfulness (0.15 after treatment), though not significantly. "No difference found" is not the same as "proven equal," so this comparison remains open.

### More Class Hours Didn't Predict Better Sleep

Total class time ranged from 3 to 42 hours, yet the amount showed essentially no relationship with how much sleep improved (Spearman's *r* = 0.1, *p* = 0.70). Home practice told a mixed story: of six trials that checked, three found more practice went with better sleep and three found no link. The authors note that dose is hard to measure in meditation, since minutes on the cushion don't reveal how much of that time was spent attending rather than :term[mind wandering]{id="mind_wandering"}.

### Few Signs of Harm, but Thin Reporting

Only 10 of the 18 trials reported on adverse events, and those that did showed no sign of increased risk. In two trials, sleep worsened for 3% and 7% of meditators, compared with 24% and 12% of people in the comparison groups. The authors still caution that difficult emotions can surface as awareness sharpens, especially early on, and that people with trauma histories may need extra support, a theme explored in [interviews with Buddhist meditators who had struggled in their practice](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/).

## Final Reflection

Taken together, these trials suggest that mindfulness training may offer people with disturbed sleep a modest benefit beyond the support and attention of any structured program, and that the benefit may last for months. How it compares with established treatments like CBT-I is still unclear.

The evidence has real limits. Sleep was almost always self-reported, the populations and programs varied widely, and the long-term picture rests on a handful of trials. Whether mindfulness changes how people sleep, or mainly how they experience their nights, remains an open question.
