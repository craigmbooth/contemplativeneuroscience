## Background Information

Addiction research has changed a great deal in recent decades. Addiction is now widely understood as a cycle of compulsive drug use driven by changes in brain circuits for motivation and pleasure, habit, and self-control. The mainstays of behavioral treatment, cognitive-behavioral therapy and motivational interviewing, were developed decades before that picture emerged.

Over roughly the same period, :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} moved into clinics through structured programs such as :term[Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)]{id="mbsr"} and :term[Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)]{id="mbct"}. Newer programs were then built specifically for addiction. The two most prominent are :term[Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP)]{id="mbrp"} and :term[Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE)]{id="more"}, alongside mindfulness training for smokers. A [2018 meta-analysis of 142 trials](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/), which this review cites, had found mindfulness programs for smoking and other addictions to beat active control conditions and to match other evidence-based treatments.

In this paper, Eric Garland of the University of Utah and Matthew Howard of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill take stock of the field. It is a narrative review, not a systematic one. There is no methods section: the authors chose which studies to discuss and do not describe how they searched for them or judged their quality. Both authors are addiction researchers with their own studies in this area, and Garland developed MORE, one of the programs reviewed. He has received income from the MORE treatment manual and therapist trainings, as the paper's competing-interests statement says.

## What They Did

The authors set themselves three aims: to pin down what mindfulness means in terms of the mental processes that might make it therapeutic, to review the research on mindfulness as a treatment for addiction, and to lay out what the next wave of research should do.

They first describe what these programs look like. Most were modeled on MBSR and MBCT: about 8 weeks of weekly group sessions led by a trained clinician. Each session includes guided practices such as mindful breathing and the body scan, a group discussion of the practice, and new teaching material. Between sessions, participants practice at home and keep track of symptoms such as craving and negative emotions. The programs differ in the practices they teach, how long home practice lasts, and the style of the group discussion. MBRP uses open, non-directive questioning, while MORE is more directive, with a lot of positive reinforcement from the therapist.

The programs also aim mindfulness directly at addiction. In MORE's "chocolate exercise," for example, participants hold a piece of chocolate near their nose and lips without eating it and watch the urge to eat it rise. The urge is compared to craving for a drug. Participants then break the craving down into its sensations, feelings and thoughts, and notice how it fades over time. The idea is to learn to respond to an urge deliberately rather than react to it automatically. The authors note that no one had yet compared such tailored programs with general ones like MBSR in people with substance use disorders, so whether the tailoring adds anything is unknown.

The authors then review evidence on how mindfulness programs might work, summarize the clinical results, and set out a research agenda.

## One Big Result: Trials Point to Less Craving and Drug Use, but Most Were Small

For the clinical results, the review leans mainly on a 2017 :term{id="meta_analysis" case="lower"} by Li and colleagues, the only one at the time focused solely on mindfulness treatment for substance misuse. Both Garland and Howard were among its authors. It pooled 34 :term[randomized controlled trials]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"} of different programs, populations and comparison groups. Compared with the control conditions, mindfulness programs showed:

- **Craving:** a reduction, with an :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} of d = 0.68.
- **Substance use:** a reduction, d = 0.33.
- **Smoking:** better odds of quitting (:term[odds ratio]{id="odds_ratio" case="lower"} 1.76), but this fell just short of statistical significance (p = 0.056).
- **Stress:** a reduction, d = 1.12.
- **Mindfulness:** an increase on a standard mindfulness questionnaire in the eight trials that used it (d = 0.62, p = 0.057, also just short of significance).

The review describes these effects as moderate to large. By the usual rules of thumb, though, d = 0.33 for substance use, the outcome that matters most, is small to moderate, and the smoking result was not statistically significant. The :term[confidence intervals]{id="confidence_interval" case="lower"} printed in the review have inconsistent minus signs, which looks like a typesetting slip, but they are wide: the one for stress runs from 0.01 to 2.24.

The trials themselves had clear weaknesses, and the review lists them:

- Of the 34, ten compared mindfulness with treatment as usual, two with an inert condition, sixteen with another psychological therapy (usually matched for time and format), and six tested brief mindfulness exercises against alternatives in the lab.
- Nearly half had fewer than 50 participants, and many lost a large share of participants by the end of treatment and follow-up.
- All were run at a single site.
- Most relied heavily on participants' own reports of their drug use. Fewer than half checked them objectively, for example with urine tests.
- The most common shortcomings were not using follow-up interviewers who were blind to which group a participant was in, and not checking drug use with people who knew the participants.

The meta-analysis found little sign of :term{id="publication_bias" case="lower"} in its statistical tests, though it did not formally search for unpublished studies. Across the review, the studies cover alcohol, tobacco, opioids, cocaine, marijuana and other drugs, but apart from smoking it does not report results separately by substance.

The paper is not entirely consistent about how strong this evidence is. In its research agenda it says results from "rigorous, full-scale RCTs" show that mindfulness programs can produce short- and long-term reductions in craving and addictive behavior. Its conclusion is more cautious:

> However, with several notable exceptions … few studies of MBIs for addiction have had large enough sample sizes to ensure the robustness and reproducibility of clinical outcomes. Moreover, few long-term follow-ups have been conducted to assess the durability of observed treatment effects.

(MBIs are mindfulness-based interventions.) One of the notable exceptions it cites is [Bowen and colleagues' 2014 trial](/mindfulness-relapse-prevention-trial/), which compared MBRP with standard relapse prevention and treatment as usual. The authors' overall verdict is that mindfulness programs are a promising treatment for addiction, and that further trials with :term[active control groups]{id="active_control_group" case="lower"}, checks that therapists deliver the program as intended, drug testing and blinded assessments are needed before they could rightly be called empirically supported therapies.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### How Might Mindfulness Help? Mostly Hypotheses So Far

The authors' central idea is that chronic drug use weakens the brain's top-down control networks, centered on the prefrontal cortex, and that drug cues and cravings hijack them. Mindfulness practice, on this view, works as mental training that rebuilds that control and strengthens its connections with bottom-up circuits for reward and motivation in the :term{id="striatum" case="lower"} and limbic system, making addictive behavior less automatic. The paper's figures map this out. But the authors are clear about its status:

> Thus little data exists to either support or refute the neural mechanistic models proposed in this section.

They then review six candidate mechanisms. The evidence for each is real but thin, often from small or pilot studies, and much of it from studies of MORE by Garland's own group:

- **Reward.** Garland's "restructuring reward" hypothesis holds that mindfulness shifts value away from drugs and back to natural rewards, such as food, enjoyable activities and relationships. In a trial of MORE for prescription opioid misuse in people with chronic pain, MORE increased heart and brain-wave responses to natural reward cues. Those increases went with lower craving, and a shift toward stronger responses to natural rewards than to opioid cues predicted less opioid misuse three months later. In a pilot :term{id="fmri"} study of smokers, MORE reduced responses in the ventral striatum to cigarette cues and increased activity in the ventral striatum and part of the :term{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} while participants savored pleasant images, compared with a time-matched comparison group.
- **Executive function.** A small non-randomized study and a pilot trial in people who used several drugs found that combined goal-management and mindfulness training improved working memory, response inhibition and decision-making. A small trial found that 2 weeks of mindfulness training reduced smoking and increased resting activity in the anterior cingulate and :term{id="mpfc"}.
- **Trait mindfulness.** In a trial of MBRP, increases in everyday mindfulness (acceptance, awareness, not judging) helped explain, statistically, the program's effect on craving. A large trial of MORE found the same, and in the opioid trial, gains in "nonreactivity" predicted less misuse.
- **Stress.** Several studies found that mindfulness programs raised :term{id="heart_rate_variability" case="lower"}, a marker of the body's capacity to calm itself, during or after stress. Two reported falls in :term{id="cortisol" case="lower"} within the mindfulness group only. Only one study had looked at the brain: in a trial of an 8-week program for quitting smoking, mindfulness was associated with less :term{id="amygdala"} and :term{id="insula"} activity during stress than an active control, and lower activity there went with less smoking at follow-up.
- **Craving and cue reactivity.** MORE reduced :term{id="attentional_bias" case="lower"} toward opioid cues and craving in an opioid :term{id="cue_reactivity" case="lower"} test. In a large smoking trial, the effect of a mindfulness-based treatment on quitting was statistically explained by reduced craving. Mindfulness may also loosen the link between bad moods and using: in [Witkiewitz and Bowen's analysis of an MBRP trial](/mindfulness-depression-craving-relapse/), MBRP weakened the link between depressive symptoms and craving two months after treatment, which in turn predicted less substance use at four months.
- **Thought suppression.** Trying to push urges away can backfire and strengthen craving. In a non-randomized study of incarcerated substance users, reduced thought suppression helped explain why a :term{id="vipassana"} course reduced drinking. In a pilot trial with alcohol-dependent inpatients, MORE reduced thought suppression, which went with a smaller attentional bias toward alcohol cues.

Most of these are :term{id="mediation_analysis" case="lower"} results: they show that a change went along with the treatment effect, which is consistent with a cause but does not prove one. And because MORE also trains reappraisal and savoring, its results may not generalize to mindfulness programs in general. The authors make this point themselves about the reward findings:

> To be clear, MORE provides integrated training in mindfulness, reappraisal, and savoring skills, and therefore other MBIs may or may not exert similar effects on restructuring the relative salience of natural and drug-related reward.

### For Whom Does It Work? Almost Nobody Had Asked

The review found only one study of who benefits most, a secondary analysis of two MBRP trials. It found that people with more severe substance use disorders and more depression and anxiety symptoms gained more from mindfulness training than those with milder problems. In the meta-analysis, trials with only men showed larger effects on craving, stress and mindfulness than trials with only women or with both, a pattern the authors report without explaining. Trials of MORE showed larger effects than trials of other mindfulness programs, a comparison worth reading alongside the competing-interest statement.

### Questions for the Next Wave of Research

The second half of the paper is a research agenda. Its main questions:

- **Brain mechanisms.** Adequately powered, randomized brain-imaging studies to test the top-down and bottom-up ideas above. Other methods could show whether mindfulness affects dopamine and the body's own opioids, or whether it stops attention being drawn to drug cues in the first place or helps people pull away from them afterward.
- **Sequencing.** Whether mindfulness works best alone or as part of a package, and in what order. A few sessions of motivational interviewing first might boost practice, and medication-assisted treatment might ease withdrawal and preoccupation with the next dose enough for people to learn mindfulness skills. Trials that re-randomize people who don't respond (called SMART designs) could test such options.
- **Dose.** How much practice is needed. Several studies, including [Brewer and colleagues' smoking trial](/mindfulness-smoking-cessation-trial/), found that people who did more of the mindfulness homework had bigger changes in their smoking, cannabis or alcohol use. The authors suggest that 8 weeks may not be enough for moderate to severe disorders, and that booster sessions might help. They say mindfulness programs in clinical settings "appear to have few adverse effects," citing a 2014 article. Later research that asked participants directly found unpleasant effects to be common, though mostly brief ([see this study](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/)). The review itself suggests that people whose distress gets worse during meditation may be the ones who drop out.
- **Training therapists.** Becoming a certified MBSR teacher costs over $10,000 and takes about three years, and MBSR teachers need not be licensed clinicians, while most addiction treatment services require licensed staff. The authors contrast this with MORE, which requires a clinical license but a much shorter and cheaper training. Here again, the first author has a financial interest in that training.

### Mindfulness as a Lifestyle, Not Just a Course

The authors' last question is whether mindfulness should be a time-limited treatment or a daily habit for life. They compare it with exercise and diet, which only work if kept up, and with 12-step programs, which encourage people to attend meetings for the rest of their lives. They suggest that the brain changes needed to undo addiction might require long-term practice. This is a proposal, not a finding, and the paper presents it as one. They close by noting that many mindfulness practices were originally meant to extinguish craving, and that mindfulness programs may help free people from "the push and pull of hedonic dysregulation underlying addiction."

## Final Reflection

As a snapshot of a young field, this review is useful and fairly candid. It sets out what mindfulness programs for addiction involve, what the trials up to 2017 found, and which questions remained open, and it states plainly that the proposed brain mechanisms were largely untested. Its abstract is more confident than its body. It says that studies "indicate" mindfulness programs reduce substance misuse and craving by changing processes involved in self-regulation and reward. The body is more tentative: much of that evidence comes from small studies, and the authors concede that little data supports or refutes their brain models.

Two cautions are worth keeping in mind. First, a narrative review lets its authors choose the evidence, and both authors co-wrote the meta-analysis it relies on. Second, one of them developed MORE, which much of the mechanism evidence comes from and which the meta-analysis found to have the largest effects. Neither point means the conclusions are wrong, but both are good reasons to look at the primary studies, including trials by other groups such as [Bowen and colleagues' 2014 trial of MBRP](/mindfulness-relapse-prevention-trial/) and [Brewer and colleagues' smoking trial](/mindfulness-smoking-cessation-trial/). Later work, such as [a full-scale trial of MORE for opioid misuse in chronic pain](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/), postdates this review and is not covered by it.
