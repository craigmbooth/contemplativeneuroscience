## Background Information

Most programs to help people stop smoking begin with smokers who want to stop. This study took a different route. Its authors argued that wanting to quit can backfire: deliberately trying not to think about something, such as a cigarette, often brings it to mind, and trying to resist the urge to smoke comes with :term{id="craving" case="lower"}. Instead of fighting the habit head-on, they asked whether strengthening self-control in general might reduce smoking as a side effect.

Their reason for focusing on self-control comes from brain research on addiction. People with addictions tend to show weaker function in frontal areas that support self-control, including the :term[anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} and the prefrontal cortex, and earlier studies had found lower resting activity in these areas in smokers than in nonsmokers.

The method they tested was integrative body–mind training (IBMT), a form of :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation that combines body relaxation, mental imagery and mindfulness, practiced to music with a coach. In earlier randomized trials, the same group had found that a few hours of IBMT improved attention, mood and the stress response more than the same amount of relaxation training, as in their [five-day study of Chinese undergraduates](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/), and changed activity and white matter around the ACC. Here they asked whether those changes would show up in smoking. The authors describe IBMT's approach this way:

> IBMT does not force participants to resist craving or quit smoking; instead, it focuses on improving self-control capacity to handle craving and smoking behavior, indicating a unique way to treat addiction.

## What They Did

The researchers advertised on a university campus for students who wanted to learn meditation or relaxation to reduce stress and improve their mental performance. People whose goal was to quit smoking were not included. Among the volunteers were 27 smokers, who averaged 10 cigarettes a day and used no other drugs, and 33 nonsmokers, all healthy college students with an average age of about 21 and no previous meditation or relaxation training.

In this :term[randomized controlled trial]{id="randomized_controlled_trial"}, smokers and nonsmokers alike were randomly assigned to one of two programs:

- **IBMT:** body relaxation, mental imagery and mindfulness to background music, guided by an IBMT coach and a CD. The method asks for no effort to control thoughts, aiming instead for a state of "restful alertness."
- **Relaxation training:** with eyes closed, participants relaxed muscle groups one after another, from the face and head down through the body, concentrating on sensations such as warmth and heaviness, guided by a relaxation coach and a CD. This was the :term[active control]{id="active_control_group"}.

Both groups practiced in small groups for 30 minutes every night for 10 consecutive sessions, 5 hours in all over two weeks. The meditation group had 15 smokers (11 men) and the relaxation group 12 (8 men). One relaxation-group smoker didn't finish and was excluded, leaving 26 smokers in the analysis.

Before and after training, the smokers took a breath test for carbon monoxide (CO), a gas from burning tobacco that builds up in the blood and lungs of smokers, which the authors used as an objective measure of how much people smoked. They also filled in the :term[Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence]{id="ftnd"} (FTND), which includes how many cigarettes they smoke a day, rated their craving to smoke on a 5-point scale, and rated how ready they were to quit on a 10-point scale. Everyone had a 6-minute brain scan at rest, lying still and looking at a crosshair, before and after training. This :term{id="resting_state_fmri"} measured how strongly the brain's activity signal fluctuated on its own in each area, a measure called fALFF.

## One Big Result: Breath Carbon Monoxide Fell After Meditation Training but Not After Relaxation

Before training, the smokers in the two groups didn't differ significantly in how much they smoked. After two weeks, the groups had changed differently (a group-by-session interaction, F(1,24) = 9.099, p = .006). Follow-up tests found a significant drop in breath CO in the IBMT group (p < .01) and no significant drop in the relaxation group (p > .05). The FTND questionnaire agreed: self-reported smoking fell significantly after IBMT (p < .05) but not after relaxation training.

The abstract describes this as "a significant reduction in smoking of 60%." The body of the paper never gives that figure or explains how it was calculated. It reports no group averages for CO, cigarettes or anything else, only test statistics and a chart of each smoker's CO readings before and after. So readers can't tell whether the 60% refers to breath CO, to cigarettes, or to something else.

Two details matter for how much weight this can bear. The authors note that the three heaviest smokers were placed in the IBMT group by chance. The groups didn't differ significantly at the start, but with 15 and 11 people that test has little power, and heavier smokers have more room to cut down. A single high reading is also more likely to be followed by a lower one regardless of treatment, a form of :term{id="regression_to_the_mean" case="lower"}, and the analysis didn't adjust for each smoker's starting level. And a breath CO reading reflects recent smoking, so it depends on when the last cigarette was smoked; the paper doesn't say what time of day the tests were taken.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Craving Fell Too, but the Groups Didn't Clearly Differ

Craving ratings dropped over the two weeks overall (F(1,23) = 14.710, p = .001), and within the IBMT group the drop was significant (p < .01), while in the relaxation group it was not. But the test of whether the groups changed differently fell just short of significance (F(1,23) = 3.935, p = .059). The authors conclude that the results "demonstrate that short-term IBMT practice can significantly reduce craving," which is stronger than a marginal group difference in 25 people supports. One IBMT smoker's craving data couldn't be used.

### Resting Brain Activity Shifted, but It Didn't Track the Change in Smoking

Before training, the smokers had lower resting activity than the nonsmokers in the ACC and part of the left lateral prefrontal cortex (corrected for :term{id="multiple_comparisons" case="lower"}). After two weeks of IBMT, resting activity rose in the ACC and :term[medial prefrontal cortex]{id="mpfc" case="lower"} and in the lower outer prefrontal cortex. In the relaxation group, nothing changed significantly. Compared directly after training, the IBMT group had lower activity than the relaxation group in the :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc" case="lower"} and :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"} and in the :term{id="cerebellum" case="lower"}, areas the authors note have shown higher activity in smokers.

The authors read this as training moving smokers toward a more typical pattern, but they are frank about two problems. The areas that changed were "somewhat different" from the ones that were low in smokers to begin with. And:

> Moreover, we did not note significant correlations between the extent of brain changes in ACC and PFC and the amount of reduced smoking or craving.

They still think the frontal changes are "most likely associated with" smoking less, but the data don't show that link. One more caution: the rise in ACC activity was significant within the IBMT group and absent in the relaxation group, but the paper doesn't report a direct test of whether that change differed between the groups. The direct comparison after training turned up only the lower posterior cingulate and cerebellum activity.

### One Smoker Didn't Notice He Had Cut Down

Self-reported smoking didn't match the breath tests well, as a chart in the paper comparing each person's two measures shows. The authors offer one participant's account as a clue. His smoking had fallen from 20 to 10 cigarettes a day, but he said, "I was not aware of my smoking reduction while filling out the self-report questionnaires," and only realized it when he checked his pocket and found he was using half a pack a day. He said it started about a week into training, "but I didn't know why." The authors take this as a sign that the change happened without conscious effort. It is one person's story, and the mismatch also means that self-reports of smoking in this study weren't a reliable guide on their own.

### Wanting to Quit Made No Difference

Although people who wanted to quit weren't recruited, the smokers still rated their readiness to quit. Those who reported some intention to quit didn't cut down more than those who reported none (p > .05). The authors suggest the change may work outside conscious intention. With 26 smokers, though, a real difference could easily go undetected.

### Five Smokers Followed Up for a Month

Five of the IBMT smokers took breath tests and filled in the FTND again two and four weeks after training ended, and the authors report that all five "maintained reduced smoking." There are no numbers, no follow-up of the relaxation group, and no word on the other ten IBMT smokers. The authors acknowledge the problem:

> Because the number was so small, we do not yet know exactly how long the reduction will last; this warrants further investigation.

### What the Design Can't Tell Us

Random assignment and an active control that looked like a credible stress-reduction program are real strengths, and recruiting people for stress reduction rather than quitting makes the result harder to explain by motivation to quit. The paper doesn't say whether participants were told the study was about smoking, but the smokers were screened with a smoking questionnaire and breath-tested before and after, so they knew their smoking was being measured. That applied equally to both groups, which limits but doesn't remove the role of expectations: everyone knew which program they were in, and no one measured whether the two programs seemed equally promising.

Beyond that, the trial was very small: 26 smokers, all college students, averaging 10 cigarettes a day. The paper reports its results briefly, without averages, standard deviations or :term[effect sizes]{id="effect_size" case="lower"} (one result is given as "P = 0.000"). The nonsmokers trained alongside the smokers, but apart from the brain comparison before training, their results aren't reported. Finally, IBMT was developed by the first author, Yi-Yuan Tang, whose own studies and book are the paper's sources for how it is taught; the paper states that the authors declare no conflict of interest.

## Final Reflection

This short paper made an appealing case: smokers who signed up to manage stress, not to quit, smoked less after five hours of meditation training spread over two weeks, while a matched relaxation group didn't change. The idea that you can reduce an addictive habit without targeting it, by training self-control in general, is an interesting one, and the breath test gives the main result some objectivity that self-report lacks.

It sits alongside other smoking research on this site that took the opposite approach, working with smokers who wanted to quit and teaching them to meet cravings directly. In a [trial of 88 smokers](/mindfulness-smoking-cessation-trial/), four weeks of mindfulness training left more people off cigarettes four months later than a standard program, and an [analysis of that trial](/mindfulness-craving-smoking-decoupling/) found that mindfulness training weakened the link between how much people craved and how much they smoked. Across many trials, a [meta-analysis of mindfulness for psychiatric disorders](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/) found that smoking was where mindfulness programs most clearly outperformed standard treatments.

Taken alone, though, this study is a small pilot. It rests on 26 students, reports its main result without averages, leaves the headline 60% unexplained, found no link between the brain changes and the smoking changes, and followed up only five people for a month. It is best read as a promising lead that a larger trial, with smokers of all ages, longer follow-up and full reporting, would need to confirm.
