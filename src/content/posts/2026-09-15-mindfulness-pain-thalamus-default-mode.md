## Background Information

Mindfulness practice is often described as changing a person's relationship to pain rather than the pain itself: noticing sensations as they are, without the layer of judgment and self-concern ("this is happening *to me*, and it's terrible") that can pile on top. This study set out to find a brain signature of that idea.

The research group, led by Fadel Zeidan, had already shown in several studies that a few short lessons in :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} meditation can reduce heat pain in the lab. In earlier work they report that it worked better than a placebo, and through different brain mechanisms from placebo and "sham" meditation. In an [earlier brain-imaging study from the same lab](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/), using a slow kind of scan that tracks blood flow, pain relief during meditation went with more activity in the :term[orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)]{id="orbitofrontal_cortex"}, a frontal area involved in weighing and reappraising experiences, and less activity in the :term{id="thalamus" case="lower"}, the brain's relay station for incoming sensory signals, including pain. That scan was too slow, though, to see how regions worked together from moment to moment.

So the team used standard :term{id="fmri"}, which samples the brain every two seconds, to ask how the connections between regions change when a beginner meditates through pain. They predicted that the OFC would work more closely with the thalamus, gating pain signals before they spread, and that the thalamus would work less closely with the part of the :term[somatosensory cortex]{id="somatosensory_cortex"} that maps the body part being heated. They also looked across the whole brain, including the :term[default mode network]{id="dmn"}, a set of regions most active during self-related thinking and rumination, which has been linked to how people with chronic pain dwell on it.

## What They Did

The study was run at Wake Forest School of Medicine and registered in advance as a clinical trial (NCT03414138). Of 137 people screened, 58 were enrolled and 40 made it into the final analysis: healthy, pain-free, right-handed adults aged 18 to 65 who had never meditated (average age 30; 20 women and 20 men; 22 White, 13 Black, 3 Hispanic, 1 Asian and 1 Native American).

**Painful heat.** A small heat probe on the back of the right calf delivered five-minute series of ten 10-second pulses at 49°C (about 120°F), with harmless 35°C warmth in between. After each series, participants rated how intense the pain had been and, separately, how unpleasant it was, on sliding scales running from 0 to 10. In a first session everyone learned the scales and sat quietly through four heat series. Only then were they randomly assigned, with equal numbers of men and women in each group, and told which group they were in.

**Training.** The mindfulness group had four 20-minute sessions on separate days with experienced instructors. They learned to focus on the changing sensations of the breath, to acknowledge thoughts, feelings and emotions "without judgment or emotional reaction," and to return to the breath when their attention drifted. Unusually, they were told *not* to practice at home, so that everyone had the same amount of practice: 80 minutes in all. In the last sessions they practiced lying down while a recording of scanner noise played, to get used to meditating in an MRI machine. The comparison group spent the same four 20-minute sessions listening to an audiobook of *The Natural History and Antiquities of Selborne*, a book the lab has used before because it doesn't lift mood, and they too heard scanner noise in the last sessions.

**The scan.** In the final session, both groups lay in the scanner with their eyes closed through two heat series. The mindfulness group was then told to begin meditating and continue until the end; the audiobook group was told to keep lying still with eyes closed. After about ten minutes, two more heat series followed. The key question was how much pain changed from the first half of the scan to the second in each group.

To measure how regions worked together, the researchers used :term[psychophysiological interaction (PPI)]{id="psychophysiological_interaction"} analyses, a form of :term{id="functional_connectivity" case="lower"}. These ask how closely one region's activity tracks another's during the hot pulses compared with the warm periods in between, and here how that changed from rest to meditation. When the paper says the thalamus "decoupled" from another region, it means the two rose and fell together less during painful heat while people meditated than while they rested. The team tested four connections it had named in advance: from the thalamus to the rest of the brain, from the OFC to the rest of the brain, from the OFC to the thalamus, and from the thalamus to the leg area of the primary somatosensory cortex. In each case it asked whether these changes went along with how much each meditator's pain fell.

## One Big Result: The Meditators With the Most Relief Showed the Most Thalamus–Precuneus Decoupling

First, the pain itself. Before training, the two groups' ratings did not differ. In the scanner, meditating lowered pain intensity by 32% and unpleasantness by 33% compared with the first, resting half of the scan, and this change differed significantly from the audiobook group's (p < .001 for both). The audiobook group's pain went slightly *up* from the first half of the scan to the second (p = .05). The paper gives these as percentages; the average ratings appear only in a figure.

The brain finding that gives the paper its title came from the connections of the thalamus. Across the 20 meditators, those whose pain intensity dropped most were those whose thalamus became most out of step with the :term{id="precuneus" case="lower"} during the heat (r = .74), along with part of the primary visual cortex. The precuneus sits on the inner surface of the brain toward the back and, with the neighboring :term[posterior cingulate cortex]{id="pcc"}, forms a central hub of the default mode network. The authors read the result this way:

> Thus, the relationship between mindfulness-induced pain relief and weaker thalamic-precuneual connectivity may reflect volitionally mediated disengagement from self-referential appraisals of nociceptive input …

In plainer words: while meditating, the people who got the most relief may have been letting go of the habit of interpreting incoming pain signals in terms of themselves. It is an appealing idea, and it echoes the Buddhist teaching of non-self, which the authors cite. But several things are worth keeping in mind.

This is a :term{id="correlation" case="lower"} across 20 people, not a group difference. The paper reports no significant change in thalamic connectivity for the meditation group as a whole; the finding is that the size of the connectivity change lined up with the size of the relief. A correlation can't show which came first: easing pain could loosen the connection as easily as the other way round, despite the title's "driven by." The r of .74 was also measured in brain areas that were found by searching the whole brain for exactly this relationship, which tends to make such numbers look stronger than they would in a fresh sample. And no one measured self-related thinking itself; the link to the self rests on what the precuneus is known to do in other studies.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### Pain Areas Quieted Down During Meditation

Compared with the change in the audiobook group, meditating reduced the brain's response to the heat in areas that process the sensory side of pain: the posterior :term{id="insula" case="lower"}, the secondary somatosensory cortex and the neighboring parietal operculum, along with the dorsal :term[anterior cingulate cortex]{id="anterior_cingulate_cortex"} and the supplementary motor area. Within the meditation group, compared with its own resting scans, the response in the :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"}, :term{id="hippocampus" case="lower"} and the front and middle of the insula also fell. These are average effects, not correlations, so they are some of the most solid brain results in the paper, though the within-group ones have no comparison with the audiobook group.

### A Self-Related Frontal Area Quieted Most in Those With the Most Relief

The more a meditator's pain intensity fell, the more activity dropped in the :term[ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC)]{id="vmpfc"} (r = .73), another midline region tied to self-related thinking and to judging what matters to oneself. The authors take this, together with the precuneus result, as two separate pieces of evidence that mindfulness eases pain by loosening self-referential processing of it.

### The Frontal Gate Worked the Opposite Way to the Prediction

The team's starting idea was that the OFC would work *more* closely with the thalamus during meditation, damping pain signals at the gate. The opposite turned up: meditators whose OFC–thalamus connection weakened more felt more relief (r = .65). As predicted, weaker coupling between the thalamus and the leg area of the primary somatosensory cortex also went with more relief, more modestly (r = .48, p = .03). And the more the OFC fell out of step with the posterior insula and secondary somatosensory cortex, which help judge where and how strong a pain is, the more relief people felt (r = .80). Together the authors describe this as pain signals being processed less fully as they travel through the brain. The abstract frames the hypothesis as "greater decoupling" between prefrontal and thalamic areas, which fits the result; the paper's introduction and results describe the opposite prediction and call the finding "contrary to our hypotheses."

### The Comparison Group Behaved Oddly

Both groups reported less pain at the start of the scanning session than in the first session, as is common when people get used to repeated testing. But the audiobook group's pain had dropped significantly more than the meditators' had, so the two groups did not start the scan from quite the same place. The authors were candid about not knowing why:

> The pain habituation effect is perplexing but may be associated with relief that the experiment was almost over.

The audiobook group's pain then rose in the second half of the scan, while its visual cortex became more active, which also widened the gap between the groups.

### How Strong Is the Evidence?

The sample of 40 was chosen by a power calculation that expected large effects, based on the lab's earlier work, and the pain reduction was clear. The brain findings are less certain. Each analysis used standard corrections for testing thousands of points in the brain, but there were several analyses (activity, four connectivity tests, and two kinds of pain rating each), with no correction for :term[multiple comparisons]{id="multiple_comparisons"} across them; the thalamus–somatosensory result (p = .03) would likely not survive one. Only pain intensity, not unpleasantness, turned up in the brain correlations. Of the 58 people enrolled, 18 didn't make it into the analysis, among them two who completed their scans but were dropped (one for a procedural error, one for an imaging artifact) and replaced to reach the target of 40.

Most important is what the comparison group did in the scanner: nothing. They had listened to an audiobook in training, but during the scan they simply kept resting, while the meditators actively did something they had been taught as a way of relating to sensations. That makes it hard to say how much of the relief came from mindfulness itself rather than from using any mental strategy, or from expecting relief. The lab's earlier studies with placebo and sham meditation speak to that question, but this study did not include those controls. The paper has no limitations section, and its final sentence reaches well beyond healthy volunteers rating ten-second heat pulses:

> We suggest that mindfulness-based pain relief, after brief mental training, can significantly uncouple self-referential from nociceptive processes, an important finding for the millions of individuals seeking a fast-acting and non-pharmacologic pain treatment.

## Final Reflection

This study adds a clear behavioral result and a provocative brain one. After only 80 minutes of training, beginners who meditated in the scanner rated painful heat about a third less intense and a third less unpleasant than they had earlier in the same scan, while people who had listened to an audiobook and kept resting did not improve. In the meditators, more relief went with the thalamus, the gateway for pain signals, falling out of step with the precuneus, and with quieter activity in the self-related vmPFC.

It is interesting to set this beside [Lutz et al.'s study of expert meditators](/activation-during-anticipation-pain/), who found pain just as intense as novices did but less unpleasant. Here, beginners actively meditating reported lower intensity *and* unpleasantness. The two designs are very different, though: long-term practitioners compared once with novices, versus brief training tested during meditation.

The idea that mindfulness eases suffering by changing the self's relationship to experience runs through other studies on this site, from [stepping back from stressful thoughts](/decentering-imagined-stress-fmri/) to [quieter default mode network activity in experienced meditators](/reduced-dmn-active-task/). This paper ties that idea to pain in an appealing way, but its central evidence is a set of correlations in 20 people, found across several analyses, with a self-related reading inferred from where in the brain they appeared. A larger study with an active comparison during the scan, and some measure of what participants were actually doing with their attention, would be needed to show that loosening the self's grip on pain is what makes it hurt less.
