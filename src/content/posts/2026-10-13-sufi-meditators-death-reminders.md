## Background Information

For decades, psychologists have studied how people cope with knowing they will die through :term{id="terror_management_theory"}. Its central experiment is the :term[mortality salience]{id="mortality_salience"} manipulation: people answer a couple of questions about their own death, are distracted for a few minutes, and are then compared with people who answered questions about something neutral. Such studies have repeatedly found that death reminders make people defend their worldview and favor their own group, and in some situations become more generous, a response known as the "Scrooge effect," after Dickens's miser. On the theory's account, even generosity can be a defense: living up to the values of one's culture shores up a sense of worth against death.

A few studies have suggested that :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} weakens these defenses. People high in trait mindfulness reacted less defensively to death reminders and let more thoughts of death come to mind afterward, and a brief, one-time meditation removed the usual defensive effect in another set of experiments. But those practices don't deal with death directly. This team, from universities in Rome, wanted to study people whose tradition puts death at the center of practice. They chose :term{id="sufism"}, the mystical tradition of Islam:

> Sufi thought encourages individuals to approach death not as a threat to be avoided, but as a reunion with the divine.

Almost every other study in this collection on meditation and death draws on Buddhist practice or belief, from [Tibetan monastics](/no-self-fear-of-death-monastics/) to [Insight meditators](/meditation-death-acceptance-brain/). This one offers a comparison from a different religion, in which death is contemplated as a longed-for union with God rather than as a lesson in impermanence.

The study was funded by the BIAL Foundation, and the authors declare no conflicts of interest. Its data are openly available.

## What They Did

Two Persian-speaking research assistants recruited 67 volunteers in the provinces of Mazandaran and Gilan in northern Iran, through their personal contacts and by asking participants to refer others. Twelve were later excluded, nine for not completing every part and three because of technical problems, leaving 55:

- **24 Sufi practitioners** (18 men), aged 19 to 84. The intended minimum was more than 1,000 hours of lifetime practice, about three years; they reported 1 to 39 years (average 15.6). One had practiced for only a year, an exception the authors made because long-term practitioners were hard to find, and say did not change the main findings.
- **31 people with no meditation experience** (20 women), aged 22 to 56.

The paper doesn't say which Sufi order or orders the practitioners belonged to, or what their practice involved day to day. Participants were told the study was about "their perspectives on life, lifestyle, and personality traits." They first filled in questionnaires on paper about their meaning in life, life satisfaction, psychological distress and self-transcendence (a sense of spiritual connection and self-forgetfulness). Then came a session at a computer, supervised by a research assistant:

1. **An implicit attitude task.** In this version of the :term[affective misattribution procedure]{id="affective_misattribution_procedure"}, a word flashed on screen for 75 milliseconds, followed by a Chinese character, and participants judged whether the character was more or less visually pleasant than average. Half the words were about gaining for oneself (keep, gain, earn, profit, obtain) and half about helping others (share, give, aid, favor, assistance), translated into Persian. The more often a person found a character pleasant after "giving" words than after "gaining" words, the stronger their implicit preference for others' interests over their own, which the authors call prosocial orientation.
2. **The death reminder.** Everyone answered two open-ended questions: "Please briefly describe the emotions that the thought of your own death arouses in you," and "Jot down, as specifically as you can, what you think will happen to you as you physically die and once you are physically dead."
3. **A distraction, which doubled as a measure of feelings.** Participants filled in the :term[State Anxiety Inventory]{id="stai"} and a positive and negative mood questionnaire (the PANAS).
4. **The implicit task again.**
5. **A word-fragment task** measuring :term{id="death_thought_accessibility" case="lower"}. Six of 20 fragments could be completed either as a death-related word or as a neutral one. In the Persian version, for example, "_arg" could become *marg* ("death") or *barg* ("leaf").

Two features of this design matter for everything that follows. Unlike the classic experiment, there was no group that answered questions about a neutral topic: everyone got the death reminder. And anxiety, mood and the word fragments were measured only after the reminder, never before it. Only the implicit task was given both before and after.

## One Big Result: After the Death Reminder, the Sufi Practitioners Were Less Anxious and Wrote More Death Words

After the reminder, the non-meditators reported higher anxiety than the Sufi practitioners. The analysis adjusted for age, gender, distress, life satisfaction, self-transcendence and meaning in life. Its overall test across anxiety, positive mood and negative mood together fell just short of significance (p = 0.07), and a follow-up test of anxiety alone was significant (p < 0.05). Positive mood (p = 0.57) and negative mood (p = 0.50) didn't differ between the groups. The paper gives the groups' anxiety averages only in a chart.

On the word fragments, the Sufi practitioners completed more of them as death words: 0.75 on average against 0.33 for the non-meditators, out of a possible 6 (p < 0.05, adjusting for age, gender, anxiety and mood). Most people in both groups wrote few or no death words. In terror management research, more death completions usually mean that thoughts of death are closer to the surface. The authors read the Sufis' higher count as a sign that they don't push such thoughts away, in line with the earlier findings on mindfulness.

That reading is possible, but the design can't show that the reminder caused either difference. Since anxiety and death words were measured only after the reminder, and no one received a neutral reminder, the Sufis might have been calmer, and thought about death more readily, on any day. People whose tradition asks them to think about death often may simply have it closer to mind. The authors acknowledge this:

> Moreover, because death-thought accessibility was assessed only after the mortality salience manipulation, we cannot rule out the possibility that long-term meditators differ from non-meditators in the baseline accessibility of death-related thoughts.

On anxiety, one detail weighs a little against a general difference: the two groups' psychological distress, measured beforehand, didn't differ significantly, and the analysis adjusted for it. But distress and anxiety after the reminder were closely linked (r = 0.74), and without a measure of anxiety before the reminder, or a group with a neutral reminder, there is no way to tell how much of the gap the reminder produced.

The abstract says that following mortality reminders, non-meditators "reported higher anxiety." That means higher than the Sufi practitioners, not higher than before the reminder, which wasn't measured.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Implicit Task: A Shift in Opposite Directions, of Uncertain Meaning

This was the only measure taken both before and after the reminder. Before it, the two groups differed: the Sufi practitioners found characters pleasant 4.2 percentage points more often after "giving" words than after "gaining" words, while the non-meditators leaned slightly the other way (3.7 points less often), a significant difference. After the reminder, both groups were close to zero (Sufis −1.4, non-meditators −0.2), no longer significantly different. The change over time differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.05).

The authors read the non-meditators' shift toward "giving" as the defensive generosity terror management theory predicts, and the Sufis' shift away from it as a sign that they didn't need that defense. In their discussion, though, they describe the evidence more cautiously than the results section does:

> Although group differences in implicit prosocial orientation did not reach statistical significance, the observed pattern is theoretically informative.

Several things counsel caution. The differences are small, a few percentage points on a task of 100 quick judgments. Two groups that start apart and both end near zero could also reflect :term{id="regression_to_the_mean" case="lower"}, which a group with a neutral reminder could have ruled out. The authors had made two opposite predictions for the Sufis in advance, that they would show no preference after the reminder or a preference for "gaining" words, so the result was always going to match one of them. And in their results, the Sufis' "pleasant" judgments fell after the reminder whichever kind of word came first, more after "giving" words; after the reminder they found characters pleasant 52% of the time, against 66% for the non-meditators. The authors themselves note that participants may not have seen giving versus gaining as relevant to defending their worldview.

### Longer Practice Went With Less Anxiety, Within the Sufi Group

Among the 24 Sufi practitioners, those with more years of practice reported less anxiety (r = −0.44) and less negative mood (r = −0.47) after the reminder, though not more positive mood (r = 0.24). With only 24 people, these estimates are rough. The practitioners' ages ranged from 19 to 84, and the authors note that larger samples are needed to separate age and life experience from years of practice; the paper doesn't report whether these correlations hold once age is taken into account. And because anxiety was measured only after the reminder, longer practice may simply go with less anxiety in general.

### Meaning in Life Mattered, Self-Transcendence Didn't

The Sufi practitioners reported more meaning in life (30.6 against 25.0 on a scale whose maximum is 35) and more self-transcendence (93.9 against 84.0) than the non-meditators. Across everyone, more meaning in life and greater life satisfaction went with less anxiety after the reminder, and greater distress with more. Self-transcendence, despite being higher among the Sufis, wasn't related to any of the feelings measured. The anxiety comparison adjusted for all of these, but only differences that were measured can be adjusted for.

### Belief or Practice?

In this study, a contemplative practice comes bundled with a set of beliefs about death, a religious community and a way of life. Sufi teaching describes death as a reunion with God, and the paper itself suggests that this "theological and emotional orientation toward mortality" may be the resource that lets practitioners face death without defensiveness. Whether any difference comes from meditation, from belief, from belonging to a community, or from whatever led people to become Sufis in the first place, a comparison like this can't say. The non-meditators' own religious beliefs weren't reported.

### What the Study Can't Say

- **No neutral reminder:** everyone got the death reminder, so the study can't show that any difference is about death. The authors note that the changes "might also be elicited by other emotionally salient experimental primes."
- **Measured once:** anxiety, mood and death words were measured only after the reminder.
- **Who took part:** two groups recruited through the research assistants' personal contacts and their referrals, differing in gender balance (the Sufis mostly men, the non-meditators mostly women), meaning in life and self-transcendence. Gender, age and these traits were adjusted for statistically, but other differences may remain.
- **Small and not preregistered:** 24 and 31 people, several outcomes, results near the conventional threshold, and no preregistered plan.
- **Translated measures:** the word-fragment task was adapted into Persian for this study, and so was the self-transcendence scale, which had no validated Persian version.

## Final Reflection

This study brings a new tradition into research on meditation and death, and a sensible question: do people whose spiritual practice centers on death respond to reminders of it differently? On the surface, the answer was yes. After writing about their own death, long-term Sufi practitioners in Iran felt less anxious than non-meditators and let death words come to mind more readily.

But the design asks more of the data than they can give. With no measures before the reminder and no neutral comparison, the results show how two self-selected groups differed after thinking about death, not how thinking about death affected them. The most clearly before-and-after result, on the implicit task, is small and open to more than one reading, and the authors' own discussion calls it non-significant.

The findings echo two others in this collection. [Insight meditators](/meditation-death-acceptance-brain/) showed a brain response the authors read as acceptance rather than avoidance of death, again compared with non-meditators tested separately. And [Tibetan Buddhist monastics](/no-self-fear-of-death-monastics/), who most firmly denied a lasting self, still feared its end the most, a reminder that holding a doctrine about death and feeling at peace with it are not the same thing. Whether a practice built around death, Sufi or Buddhist, actually changes how people face it would take a study that measures people before and after a death reminder, compares them with people given a neutral one, and ideally follows newcomers as they learn the practice. Until then, the gentler reading of this study is that long-term Sufi practitioners and non-meditators from the same region felt differently after contemplating death, for reasons that remain to be untangled.
