## Background Information

Can eight weeks of meditation change the physical structure of your brain? For more than a decade, many people have assumed so. Small studies from the early 2010s reported that people who completed :term{id="mbsr"}, the eight-week course originally developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn, showed more :term[gray matter]{id="gray_matter"} (the brain tissue that holds most nerve cell bodies) in regions such as the hippocampus and the posterior cingulate cortex, a hub of the default mode network (:term{id="dmn"}). Those studies have been cited more than 3,200 times.

The idea was plausible. :term{id="neuroplasticity"}, the brain's capacity to change with experience, is well documented, and aerobic exercise and balance training have both been linked to measurable structural change.

But the earlier MBSR studies had real weaknesses. Groups had 20 or fewer people, participants weren't randomly assigned, and there was no :term[active control group]{id="active_control_group"} (a comparison group doing a different but equally engaging program). Without one, it's hard to tell whether a change comes from meditation itself or from simply taking a class. A team led by Tammi Kral at Richard Davidson's Center for Healthy Minds set out to test the claim more rigorously.

## What They Did

The researchers combined two :term{id="randomized_controlled_trial" case="lower" plural} run about five years apart in Madison, Wisconsin, with the same design. Everyone was a healthy adult with no meditation experience. People who had a psychiatric diagnosis in the past year, or who took psychiatric medication, were excluded.

After a baseline brain scan, participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups:

- **MBSR:** an eight-week course of mindfulness meditation, yoga, and body awareness, taught by certified instructors
- **Health Enhancement Program (HEP):** a matched eight-week course of exercise, music therapy, and nutrition education
- **Waitlist:** no training during the study

Both courses asked for daily home practice, which participants logged. After the eight weeks, everyone was scanned again; 218 people completed both scans.

The team used structural MRI (scans that capture the size and shape of brain tissue rather than its activity) to measure gray matter volume, gray matter density, and cortical thickness (the depth of the brain's outer layer). They looked across the whole brain and in 12 specific regions, including those where earlier studies had reported growth. They then re-ran the analysis with different software to check that the result didn't depend on one method.

## One Big Result: No Structural Changes Compared With Either Control Group

Compared with either the active control or the waitlist, MBSR produced no significant change in gray matter volume, density, or cortical thickness. That held across the whole brain and in every one of the 12 regions examined. It also held with different analysis software, with and without outlying data points, and after accounting for the time between scans.

The differences weren't just statistically insignificant; they were tiny. For the left hippocampus, one of the regions highlighted in earlier work, the :term{id="effect_size" case="lower"} for MBSR versus the active control was a partial η² of 0.01. That means group membership accounted for only about 1% of the variation in change. With more than three times as many MBSR participants as the earlier studies, the authors concluded:

> "MBSR does not differentially affect brain structure."

That conclusion is about eight weeks of MBSR in healthy adults. It doesn't speak directly to longer or more intensive practice.

## Miscellaneous Interesting Takeaways

### The Training Still Had Effects, but Not All of Them Were Specific to Mindfulness

The lack of structural change doesn't seem to come from people skipping practice. On average, the MBSR participants attended 8 of 9 possible classes and logged 32 hours of home practice (range 2–85), compared with about 23 hours in the earlier research. Earlier analyses of these same trials also found changes in brain *function*, such as reduced :term{id="amygdala" case="lower"} reactivity to emotional images.

Self-reported :term{id="mindfulness" case="lower"} rose after MBSR, but it rose after HEP too, and the two groups didn't differ significantly. The authors suggest that some gains may come from what such courses share, like expert teachers, a group, and attention to well-being, rather than from meditation specifically. The mindfulness gain over the waitlist was also moderate (Cohen's d of 0.39 to 0.43), well below the large effects (0.77 to 1.48) reported in the earlier small studies.

### A Possible Practice Effect in the Amygdala Did Not Hold Up

One result hinted at a dose effect. Among MBSR participants, more home practice was associated with a larger reduction in the volume of the right amygdala (an almond-shaped region involved in emotion and threat), compared with the same amount of HEP practice. Participants with less than about 27 hours of practice showed no change, and the authors estimate that fewer than about 22 minutes a day is unlikely to change amygdala structure.

However, the effect was small and didn't survive the team's checks with alternative analysis methods. The authors say it should be treated with caution until it is replicated.

### Who Signs Up May Matter

The earlier studies recruited people who had chosen to take an MBSR course, and some selected participants for high stress. This study advertised a study of "health and well-being," assigned people at random, and ended up with unusually healthy volunteers who had very low baseline anxiety. They may simply have had less room to improve.

The authors acknowledge a trade-off: tighter experimental control can cost some realism, and the act of choosing to enroll may itself be linked to greater benefit. They also note that MBSR mixes many practices, so any structural change might be spread out and hard to detect. Longer training, or training focused on a single practice, may be needed to produce measurable change.

## Final Reflection

For anyone who has read that a few weeks of mindfulness will "grow" their brain, this study is a useful corrective. In a large, carefully controlled trial, eight weeks of MBSR changed brain structure no more than a matched wellness course or no course at all.

That isn't the same as showing meditation does nothing to the brain. The study can't speak to years of practice or intensive retreats, and the same participants showed other changes. What it does suggest is that the benefits of a short course probably don't show up as measurable changes in gray matter, and that striking results from small studies deserve a second look.
