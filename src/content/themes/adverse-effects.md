question: "Can meditation be harmful?"

meta_description: "Research on the adverse effects of meditation, from anxiety to depersonalization, and what the evidence means for practicing safely."
theme_description: |
  While meditation is often portrayed as universally beneficial, a growing body of research acknowledges that contemplative practices can sometimes lead to challenging or adverse effects in certain individuals or contexts. These range from transient psychological discomfort to more severe and lasting difficulties.

  This theme examines research on meditation-related challenges, including experiences like meditation-induced anxiety, depersonalization/derealization unrelated to progress, exacerbation of trauma symptoms, and other potentially difficult outcomes that practitioners may encounter. Understanding these effects is crucial for developing safer implementation of contemplative practices across clinical and non-clinical settings.
research_summary: |
  **Yes: meditation can bring difficult experiences, and they are not limited to beginners, though most are mild and brief, and severe reactions such as psychosis are documented mainly in case reports.** The broadest study here, [Lindahl et al. (2017)](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/), interviewed 60 Western Buddhist practitioners and catalogued 59 kinds of meditation-related experience across seven domains: cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic, conative, sense of self and social. Nearly half (43%) had more than 10,000 hours of lifetime practice, a quarter first ran into difficulties after more than 10 years of practice, and the median symptom lasted 1–3 years. Because everyone in that study was recruited for having had difficulties, it shows how varied these experiences can be rather than how often they occur.

  [Schlosser et al. (2019)](/regular-meditators-unpleasant-experiences/) asked 1,232 regular meditators a single question, and 25.6% said they had had a particularly unpleasant experience, such as anxiety or an altered sense of self, that they thought meditation may have caused. People who had ever attended a retreat, and those who practiced only deconstructive styles such as vipassana, said yes more often, while religious participants said yes less often. The survey recorded nothing about severity or timing, and it can't show cause. An earlier survey of 342 meditators recruited through Spanish-, English- and Portuguese-language websites found almost the same rate with a differently worded question: 25.4% reported an unwanted or adverse reaction, most often anxiety, and most who gave details said it was temporary and kept practicing, though about half of those who said yes gave no details at all ([Cebolla et al., 2017](/meditation-unwanted-effects/)).

  [Britton et al. (2021)](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/) counted them inside an ordinary 8-week mindfulness course. When an independent interviewer asked 96 participants about 44 specific experiences, 58% reported at least one meditation-related effect that felt unpleasant at the time, 37% one that affected daily life or functioning, and 6–14% one with "lasting bad effects", rates the authors liken to those reported for psychotherapy; the benefits of such courses are weighed in [Mental health](/themes/mental-health/). Most effects were brief, but detachment and emotional numbness, though less often felt as unpleasant, flagged a higher risk of lasting problems. A single broad question picked up only about a third of the people who reported effects when asked specifically. Two Oxford studies of an 8-week MBCT-based course for the general population found a similar split: about two-thirds of 84 schoolteachers and 74 students reported unpleasant experiences, mostly not very upsetting, but only 3–7% said the course had harmed them, and those people were almost never the ones whose symptom questionnaires reliably worsened ([Baer et al., 2021](/mindfulness-course-unpleasant-experiences-harm/)).

  [Goldberg et al. (2022)](/meditation-adverse-effects-prevalence/) turns to everyday practitioners. In a survey of 434 US adults who had tried meditation, about half reported at least one adverse effect on a specific checklist, most often anxiety. About 1 in 10 said an effect had interfered with daily life, and nearly 9 in 10 were still glad they had practiced.

  These studies count difficult experiences among people who meditated, without a comparison group doing something else. A randomized trial did that for a single short session: 5,049 US and UK adults were assigned online to a 5- or 10-minute guided meditation or to using the internet as usual, and about 4% in every group said the task was distressing, while a marked rise in negative mood was less common after meditating (6–7%) than after internet use (9.6%), though only one short session was tested, with effects measured right after it ([Simonsson et al., 2026](/brief-meditation-adverse-experiences/)).

  At the severe end, [Charan et al. (2023)](/meditation-psychosis-case-series/) describe five women in India and Nepal who developed psychosis soon after starting or sharply increasing intense, mostly unsupervised concentrative meditation; all improved with antipsychotic treatment after being advised to stop, though one relapsed when she resumed. Five cases with no comparison group can't show that meditation caused the illness or how often this happens.

  [Castillo (1990)](/depersonalization-and-meditation/) adds a nuance: the same experience can be distressing or valued depending on how it is understood. The long-term Transcendental Meditation practitioners he interviewed described depersonalization as calm "witnessing," while clinical patients often fear it as a sign of illness. Changes in the sense of self, welcome and unwelcome, are gathered in [The self](/themes/self/), and the deepest states of practice in [Deep states](/themes/deep-states/).

  [Britton (2019)](/mindfulness-too-much-good-thing/) offers a framework for why some people struggle: in a short review, she argues that mindfulness skills such as body awareness, emotional control and stepping back from experience may follow an inverted U, helping people who lack them but backfiring in excess or in people who already have a lot, a hypothesis built from selected studies rather than a tested finding.

  Together, these papers suggest that challenging experiences are a real part of contemplative practice, and that how they are framed and supported shapes whether they become a problem.
