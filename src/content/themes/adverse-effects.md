theme_description: |
  While meditation is often portrayed as universally beneficial, a growing body of research acknowledges that contemplative practices can sometimes lead to challenging or adverse effects in certain individuals or contexts. These range from transient psychological discomfort to more severe and lasting difficulties.

  This theme examines research on meditation-related challenges, including experiences like meditation-induced anxiety, depersonalization/derealization unrelated to progress, exacerbation of trauma symptoms, and other potentially difficult outcomes that practitioners may encounter. Understanding these effects is crucial for developing safer implementation of contemplative practices across clinical and non-clinical settings.
research_summary: |
  **Yes: meditation can bring difficult experiences, and they are not limited to beginners.** The broadest study here, [Lindahl et al. (2017)](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/), interviewed 60 Western Buddhist practitioners and catalogued 59 types of meditation-related challenges across seven domains: cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic, conative, sense of self and social. Some participants had more than 10,000 hours of practice, and about a quarter reported effects that lasted longer than a month. Because everyone in that study was recruited for having had difficulties, it shows how varied these experiences can be rather than how often they occur.

  [Schlosser et al. (2019)](/regular-meditators-unpleasant-experiences/) asked 1,232 regular meditators a single question, and 25.6% said they had had a particularly unpleasant experience, such as anxiety or an altered sense of self, that they thought meditation may have caused. People who had ever attended a retreat, and those who practiced only deconstructive styles such as vipassana, said yes more often, while religious participants said yes less often. The survey recorded nothing about severity or timing, and it can't show cause.

  [Britton et al. (2021)](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/) counted them inside an ordinary 8-week mindfulness course. When an independent interviewer asked 96 participants about 44 specific experiences, 58% reported at least one meditation-related effect that felt unpleasant at the time, 37% one that affected daily life or functioning, and 6–14% one with "lasting bad effects", rates the authors liken to those reported for psychotherapy. Most effects were brief, but detachment and emotional numbness, though less often felt as unpleasant, flagged a higher risk of lasting problems. A single broad question picked up only about a third of the people who reported effects when asked specifically.

  [Goldberg et al. (2022)](/meditation-adverse-effects-prevalence/) turns to everyday practitioners. In a survey of 434 US adults who had tried meditation, about half reported at least one adverse effect on a specific checklist, most often anxiety. About 1 in 10 said an effect had interfered with daily life, and nearly 9 in 10 were still glad they had practiced.

  [Castillo (1990)](/depersonalization-and-meditation/) adds a nuance: the same experience can be distressing or valued depending on how it is understood. The long-term Transcendental Meditation practitioners he interviewed described depersonalization as calm "witnessing," while clinical patients often fear it as a sign of illness.

  Together, these papers suggest that challenging experiences are a real part of contemplative practice, and that how they are framed and supported shapes whether they become a problem.
