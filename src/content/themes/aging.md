question: "Can meditation slow aging?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and aging: telomeres, telomerase and gray matter in long-term meditators, retreat participants and older adults."
theme_description: |
  Telomeres are the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes, and telomerase is an enzyme that helps maintain them; both are studied as markers of cellular aging. The brain ages too, losing gray matter over adulthood. This theme gathers studies asking whether meditation is linked to slower aging on these measures, from telomeres in long-term meditators and telomerase after retreats to brain volume across adulthood and in a randomized trial in older adults. Research on [stress responses and the body](/themes/biological-health/) and on [inflammation and the immune system](/themes/immunity/) has its own themes.
research_summary: |
  **Some studies link meditation to markers of slower aging, such as longer telomeres, more telomerase and less gray-matter loss with age, but most compare meditators with non-meditators or measured only once, and an 18-month randomized trial in older adults found no significant slowing of brain shrinkage or telomere shortening.** After a three-month intensive retreat, 30 meditators had higher telomerase activity in immune cells than 30 matched people on a wait-list, along with more purpose in life, a greater sense of control and lower neuroticism, but telomerase was measured only after the retreat ([Jacobs et al., 2011](/immune-telemorase-psychological-mediators/)). In a one-time comparison in Sri Lanka, 30 skilled meditators who had practiced regularly for at least three years had white-blood-cell telomeres about 32% longer than 30 matched non-meditators by a qPCR estimate (7.35 against 5.57 kb), and the two telomerase genes were more active ([Dasanayaka et al., 2023](/telomere-dynamics/)). When healthy women were randomly assigned to six days at a resort, on vacation or on a meditation retreat, a telomerase rise in regular meditators on the retreat was only a trend ([Epel et al., 2016](/meditation-retreat-vacation-gene-expression/)). In a pilot trial of 39 stressed dementia caregivers, telomerase in immune cells rose more after eight weeks of a 12-minute daily chanting meditation than after relaxing to music, but this was an exploratory result right at the threshold of significance ([Lavretsky et al., 2013](/kirtan-kriya-dementia-caregivers/)); the trial's depression results are in [Mental health](/themes/mental-health/). The largest test so far found nothing: in the Age-Well trial of adults aged 65 and over, median telomere length in blood immune cells fell by similar amounts over 18 months with meditation, English lessons or no training; within the meditation group only, people who attended more classes kept more telomere length, a correlation the authors note could reflect other differences between them ([Kaliman et al., 2025](/meditation-older-adults-telomeres/)).

  Whether meditation slows the brain's aging is unsettled; the wider evidence on gray matter at any age is in [Brain structure](/themes/brain-structure/). In a one-time comparison of 100 adults aged 24 to 77, older age went with less gray matter in long-term meditators as well as non-meditators, but the link was weaker in meditators, though such a comparison can't separate meditation from lifestyle or who chooses to meditate ([Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/)). In a pilot comparison, six expert Buddhist meditators in their 60s had more gray matter and higher glucose metabolism than 67 peers, mostly in regions that decline most with age, but with so few meditators, each scanned once, it can't show that meditation caused the difference ([Chételat et al., 2017](/older-meditators-brain-aging/)). In a randomized trial of 137 adults aged 65 and over, 18 months of meditation didn't significantly change volume or blood flow in two target brain regions compared with English lessons or no training, though meditators reported larger gains in attention and socioemotional skills ([Chételat et al., 2022](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/)). Beyond these markers, a 2017 review of 22 studies of mindfulness in older adults found mostly positive results for attention, mood and inflammation, but only three randomized trials compared mindfulness with an active program, and in those the comparison group sometimes did as well ([Fountain-Zaragoza and Prakash, 2017](/mindfulness-healthy-aging-review/)).

  This is a small literature. Most of the encouraging results come from comparing people who already meditate with people who don't, which shows an association rather than proving that meditation caused the difference, and no study here has followed whether these markers translate into longer or healthier lives.
