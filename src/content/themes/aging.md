question: "Can meditation slow aging?"

theme_description: |
  Telomeres are the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes, and telomerase is an enzyme that helps maintain them; both are studied as markers of cellular aging. The brain ages too, losing gray matter over adulthood. This theme gathers studies asking whether meditation is linked to slower aging on these measures, from telomeres in long-term meditators and telomerase after retreats to brain volume across adulthood and in a randomized trial in older adults. Research on [stress responses and the body](/themes/biological-health/) and on [inflammation and the immune system](/themes/immunity/) has its own themes.
research_summary: |
  **Some studies link meditation to markers of slower aging, such as longer telomeres, more telomerase and less gray-matter loss with age, but most compare meditators with non-meditators or measured only once, and the one randomized trial in older adults found no significant slowing of brain shrinkage.** After a three-month intensive retreat, 30 meditators had higher telomerase activity in immune cells than 30 matched people on a wait-list, along with more purpose in life, a greater sense of control and lower neuroticism, but telomerase was measured only after the retreat ([Jacobs et al., 2011](/immune-telemorase-psychological-mediators/)). In a one-time comparison, 30 people who had meditated regularly for at least three years had telomeres about 32% longer than 30 matched non-meditators, and genes that maintain them were more active ([Dasanayaka et al., 2023](/telomere-dynamics/)). When healthy women were randomly assigned to six days at a resort, on vacation or on a meditation retreat, a telomerase rise in regular meditators on the retreat was only a trend ([Epel et al., 2016](/meditation-retreat-vacation-gene-expression/)).

  Whether meditation slows the brain's aging is unsettled. In a one-time comparison of 100 adults aged 24 to 77, older age went with less gray matter in long-term meditators as well as non-meditators, but the link was weaker in meditators, though such a comparison can't separate meditation from lifestyle or who chooses to meditate ([Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/)). In a randomized trial of 137 adults aged 65 and over, 18 months of meditation didn't significantly change volume or blood flow in two target brain regions compared with English lessons or no training, though meditators reported larger gains in attention and socioemotional skills ([Chételat et al., 2022](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/)).

  This is a small literature. Most of the encouraging results come from comparing people who already meditate with people who don't, which shows an association rather than proving that meditation caused the difference, and no study here has followed whether these markers translate into longer or healthier lives.
