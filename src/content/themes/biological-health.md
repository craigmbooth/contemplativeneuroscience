question: "Does meditation change how the body handles stress?"

meta_description: "How meditation affects the body's stress responses: cortisol, blood pressure, heart disease, breathing rate and body temperature."
theme_description: |
  Stress is not only a feeling: it raises the hormone cortisol, speeds the heart and lifts blood pressure. This theme gathers research on how contemplative practices affect those responses, from cortisol during a mock job interview to blood pressure, heart attacks and deaths in people with heart disease, breathing rate, skin healing and the body heat raised by Tibetan "inner fire" meditation. Research on [inflammation and the immune system](/themes/immunity/) and on [aging](/themes/aging/) has its own themes.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation may soften the body's response to stress, but the results are mixed: some trials found smaller cortisol rises and others none, matched comparison programs often did as well, and only one trial, in heart disease, has followed hard outcomes such as heart attacks and deaths.** Against a health program matched to MBSR in format, teaching and home practice, an 8-week MBSR course eased distress and the cortisol response to a stress test no more than the health program did, but a nerve-driven skin flare provoked during the test grew less after MBSR. Inflammatory proteins in the skin didn't differ, and the flare difference was gone four months later ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)). Six weeks of compassion meditation likewise didn't change IL-6, cortisol or distress responses to a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group, although students who practiced more had smaller IL-6 responses, a link the design can't show was caused by practice ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)). In 70 adults with generalized anxiety disorder, the stress hormone ACTH rose less during a lab stress test after an 8-week MBSR course and more after a stress management class, though cortisol fell in both groups alike and only course completers were analyzed ([Hoge et al., 2018](/mbsr-anxiety-stress-hormones-inflammation/)).

  The kind of practice may matter. In the ReSource Project, people tested once, after three months of attention and body-awareness training, reported less stress during a mock job interview than untrained people but had no smaller rise in the stress hormone cortisol, while those trained in compassion or perspective-taking had cortisol rises 32–51% smaller; heart rate, alpha-amylase and inflammation markers didn't differ, and the cortisol differences were small by conventional standards ([Engert et al., 2017](/compassion-perspective-training-cortisol-stress/)). Even five days can register: students randomly assigned to 20 minutes a day of integrative body–mind training showed a smaller cortisol rise around a mental arithmetic test, and higher levels of secretory IgA, an antibody in saliva, than students given relaxation training, though these were measured only after training ([Tang et al., 2007](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)).

  Other studies look at the heart, skin, breathing and body temperature. In a small randomized trial of 56 adults with prehypertension, eight weeks of MBSR lowered blood pressure measured in the clinic more than muscle relaxation did (systolic −4.9 against −0.7 mm Hg), but not 24-hour readings taken during daily life, and there was no follow-up ([Hughes et al., 2013](/mbsr-prehypertension-blood-pressure/)). In a rare trial of hard outcomes, 201 African American adults with coronary heart disease were randomly assigned to Transcendental Meditation or a matched health education program; over 5.4 years on average, 20 in the TM group and 32 controls died or had a heart attack or stroke (adjusted hazard ratio 0.52), though the difference wasn't significant without adjustment, and six of the nine authors were at Maharishi University of Management, which coordinated the trial ([Schneider et al., 2012](/transcendental-meditation-heart-disease-events/)). In a much earlier and smaller randomized trial, 37 psoriasis patients who heard guided mindfulness tapes during ultraviolet light treatment reached the halfway and clearing points at about 3.8 times the rate of patients having light treatment alone, though the comparison group had no tape, 14 patients left early, and the nurses who timed the milestones knew who was in which group ([Kabat-Zinn et al., 1998](/mindfulness-psoriasis-skin-clearing/)).

  Breathing practice can shift the body too. Over eight weeks, 20 coached sessions of slow belly breathing (about 3–4 breaths a minute by the end) lowered late-morning cortisol in Beijing office workers, while cortisol didn't change in an untrained control group, though the groups were assigned by order of registration, cortisol was measured at rest rather than during a stress test, and the controls had no sessions of any kind ([Ma et al., 2017](/diaphragmatic-breathing-attention-cortisol/)). In a month-long remote trial, 5 minutes a day of cyclic sighing (a double inhale and a long exhale) lowered people's daily breathing rate more than 5 minutes of mindfulness meditation, though resting heart rate and heart rate variability didn't differ between groups ([Balban et al., 2023](/cyclic-sighing-breathwork-mood/)). Pace may matter too: among 95 students, 15 minutes of breathing at each person's resonance rate (about 6 breaths a minute) lifted positive mood and shifted a heart rhythm ratio (LF/HF) more than sitting quietly, while breathing one breath a minute faster mostly fell in between; overall heart rate variability didn't change, and it was a single session ([Steffen et al., 2017](/resonance-frequency-breathing-hrv-mood/)). Advanced practice can even change body temperature: in eastern Tibet, six experienced practitioners of g-tummo, the "inner fire" meditation, raised their armpit temperature from about 36.5°C to an average 37.6°C in rooms near freezing. A forceful breath-holding technique alone warmed them, while Western volunteers using only the breathing stayed in the normal range, though the samples were tiny and the conditions always ran in the same order ([Kozhevnikov et al., 2013](/g-tummo-body-heat/)).

  The main caveats are size and design. Most of these studies are small, several measured the body only after training rather than before and after, results differ with the comparison program and the kind of practice, and only the heart disease trial followed outcomes such as heart attacks and deaths.
