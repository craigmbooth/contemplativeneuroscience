title: Biological and Health-Related Correlates
theme_key: biological-health
short_title: "Body and health"
question: "Does meditation change the body?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "How meditation affects the body: studies on immune function, inflammation, telomeres and telomerase, and cellular aging."
theme_description: |
  Beyond the mind, meditation can leave measurable traces in the body. This theme gathers research on how contemplative practices affect immune markers, inflammation, telomerase activity, and sleep. Together, they suggest that the health benefits of meditation may be rooted not just in psychology, but in physiology.
research_summary: |
  **The evidence points to measurable effects on the body, but most studies are small, many results haven't been replicated, and matched comparison programs often do as well.** In a randomized trial, [Davidson et al. (2003)](/brain-and-immune-function/) found that employees who took an 8-week MBSR course produced more antibodies after a flu vaccine than a wait-list group. Two studies link meditation to telomere biology, which is tied to cellular aging: [Jacobs et al. (2011)](/immune-telemorase-psychological-mediators/) found higher telomerase activity after a three-month retreat, linked to gains in purpose in life and perceived control, and [Dasanayaka et al. (2023)](/telomere-dynamics/) found that long-term meditators had telomeres about 32% longer than matched non-meditators. A systematic review of 20 randomized trials with 1,602 participants found tentative, repeated signs, counting results that fell short of significance, that mindfulness training lowers NF-κB activity and the inflammation marker CRP and raises telomerase activity and, in people with HIV, CD4+ T-cell counts, but most other immune markers showed no consistent change, and only 8 of the trials used a matched comparison program ([Black and Slavich, 2016](/mindfulness-immune-system-review/)).

  Other studies connect body and mind. [Shields et al. (2020)](/deconstructing-concentration-interference-control/) found that retreat participants with higher inflammation had weaker attention control, and [Lutz et al. (2013)](/activation-during-anticipation-pain/) found that expert meditators rated pain as less unpleasant, though no less intense, than novices. A randomized trial also found that a three-day mindfulness retreat, unlike a matched relaxation retreat, strengthened a resting connection between the default mode network and a region involved in self-regulation, and that change statistically accounted for part of a drop in the inflammation marker IL-6 four months later ([Creswell et al., 2016](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/)). In an earlier small trial with 40 older adults, an 8-week MBSR course eased loneliness and damped inflammation-related gene activity in immune cells compared with a wait-list, though the blood markers CRP and IL-6 didn't change significantly ([Creswell et al., 2012](/mbsr-loneliness-older-adults/)).

  Gene activity in blood cells responds quickly, but not only to meditation. After a single eight-hour day of practice, 19 experienced meditators had lower activity in three HDAC genes and two inflammation-related genes in their white blood cells than 21 non-meditators who spent the day at leisure, though the groups weren't randomized and IL-6 and TNF-α didn't differ ([Kaliman et al., 2014](/meditation-day-gene-expression-inflammation/)). Inflammation-related gene pathways were least active in long-term practitioners of relaxation-response techniques, such as meditation, yoga and repetitive prayer, and dipped further during a single 20-minute session, though the study wasn't randomized and measured gene activity only ([Bhasin et al., 2013](/relaxation-response-gene-expression/)). But when healthy women were randomly assigned to six days at a resort, a vacation shifted blood gene activity, turning down stress- and inflammation-related genes, as much as a meditation retreat did; learning to meditate showed up instead in fewer depressive symptoms ten months later, and a telomerase rise in regular meditators on the retreat was only a trend ([Epel et al., 2016](/meditation-retreat-vacation-gene-expression/)).

  Stress responses are mixed. Against a health program matched to MBSR in format, teaching and home practice, an 8-week MBSR course eased distress and the cortisol response to a stress test no more than the health program did, but a nerve-driven skin flare provoked during the test grew less after MBSR. Inflammatory proteins in the skin didn't differ, and the flare difference was gone four months later ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)). Six weeks of compassion meditation likewise didn't change IL-6, cortisol or distress responses to a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group, although students who practiced more had smaller IL-6 responses, a link the design can't show was caused by practice ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)). The kind of practice may matter. In the ReSource Project, people tested once, after three months of attention and body-awareness training, reported less stress during a mock job interview than untrained people but had no smaller rise in the stress hormone cortisol, while those trained in compassion or perspective-taking had cortisol rises 32–51% smaller; heart rate, alpha-amylase and inflammation markers didn't differ, and the cortisol differences were small by conventional standards ([Engert et al., 2017](/compassion-perspective-training-cortisol-stress/)). Even five days can register: students randomly assigned to 20 minutes a day of integrative body–mind training showed a smaller cortisol rise around a mental arithmetic test, and higher levels of secretory IgA, an antibody in saliva, than students given relaxation training, though these were measured only after training ([Tang et al., 2007](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)).

  Other studies look at the heart, skin, breathing and body temperature. In a small randomized trial of 56 adults with prehypertension, eight weeks of MBSR lowered blood pressure measured in the clinic more than muscle relaxation did (systolic −4.9 against −0.7 mm Hg), but not 24-hour readings taken during daily life, and there was no follow-up ([Hughes et al., 2013](/mbsr-prehypertension-blood-pressure/)). In a rare trial of hard outcomes, 201 African American adults with coronary heart disease were randomly assigned to Transcendental Meditation or a matched health education program; over 5.4 years on average, 20 in the TM group and 32 controls died or had a heart attack or stroke (adjusted hazard ratio 0.52), though the difference wasn't significant without adjustment, and six of the nine authors were at Maharishi University of Management, which coordinated the trial ([Schneider et al., 2012](/transcendental-meditation-heart-disease-events/)). In a much earlier and smaller randomized trial, 37 psoriasis patients who heard guided mindfulness tapes during ultraviolet light treatment reached the halfway and clearing points at about 3.8 times the rate of patients having light treatment alone, though the comparison group had no tape, 14 patients left early, and the nurses who timed the milestones knew who was in which group ([Kabat-Zinn et al., 1998](/mindfulness-psoriasis-skin-clearing/)). Breathing practice can shift the body too: in a month-long remote trial, 5 minutes a day of cyclic sighing (a double inhale and a long exhale) lowered people's daily breathing rate more than 5 minutes of mindfulness meditation, though resting heart rate and heart rate variability didn't differ between groups ([Balban et al., 2023](/cyclic-sighing-breathwork-mood/)). Advanced practice can even change body temperature: in eastern Tibet, six experienced practitioners of g-tummo, the "inner fire" meditation, raised their armpit temperature from about 36.5°C to an average 37.6°C in rooms near freezing. A forceful breath-holding technique alone warmed them, while Western volunteers using only the breathing stayed in the normal range, though the samples were tiny and the conditions always ran in the same order ([Kozhevnikov et al., 2013](/g-tummo-body-heat/)).

  Whether meditation slows the brain's aging is unsettled. In a one-time comparison of 100 adults aged 24 to 77, older age went with less gray matter in long-term meditators as well as non-meditators, but the link was weaker in meditators, though such a comparison can't separate meditation from lifestyle or who chooses to meditate ([Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/)). In a randomized trial of 137 adults aged 65 and over, 18 months of meditation didn't significantly change volume or blood flow in two target brain regions compared with English lessons or no training, though meditators reported larger gains in attention and socioemotional skills ([Chételat et al., 2022](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/)).

  The main caveats are size and design. Several of these studies compare long-term meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time, which shows an association rather than proving that meditation caused the difference; many trials compared meditation with a wait-list rather than a matched program; and only one, the heart disease trial, followed hard outcomes such as heart attacks and deaths.
