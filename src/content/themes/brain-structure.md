theme_description: |
  Can practicing meditation change the brain's physical structure, not just its activity? This theme gathers studies that measure gray matter, cortical thickness and brain volume with MRI: comparisons of long-term meditators with non-meditators, trials of eight-week courses and longer training, and studies of whether meditation slows the brain's aging. For what happens in the brain during meditation, see [Brain](/themes/neurological-correlates/).
research_summary: |
  **One-time comparisons of meditators with non-meditators and small early studies found structural differences, but the two large randomized trials that looked for lasting change, over eight weeks and 18 months, found none beyond their comparison groups.** A 2014 meta-analysis of 21 structural brain-imaging studies, most of them one-time comparisons of long-term meditators with non-meditators, found differences recurring in a handful of regions, including the front of the prefrontal cortex, the anterior cingulate cortex, the insula and the hippocampus, but the authors' adjusted average difference was modest, and the smallest studies reported the largest effects, a sign that negative results may have gone unpublished ([Fox et al., 2014](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/)). A later meta-analysis of 25 such studies, mostly one-time comparisons, found that only one spot, the right anterior insula, came up consistently once a strict statistical correction was applied, and the authors judged the large average effect sizes overestimated, since each came from a study's single strongest result ([Pernet et al., 2021](/meditation-gray-matter-insula/)). In one such comparison, 22 people who had meditated for an average of 24 years had more gray matter than matched non-meditators in one right orbitofrontal region and a larger right hippocampus, but their brains were no larger overall, and gray matter didn't rise with years of practice ([Luders et al., 2009](/long-term-meditators-hippocampus-gray-matter/)). In an exploratory comparison of 50 long-term meditators and 50 matched non-meditators, meditators' cortex was more folded in five areas, most strongly the right anterior insula, where folding also rose with years of practice, but the results weren't corrected for multiple comparisons, and the authors themselves suggest the insula's folding may be set around birth ([Luders et al., 2012](/long-term-meditators-cortical-folding/)). The first such study of loving-kindness meditators found more gray matter in the right angular gyrus and right posterior parahippocampal gyrus of 10 men with at least five years of practice than in 15 beginners, but among the meditators, more practice went with less angular gyrus gray matter, not more ([Leung et al., 2013](/loving-kindness-gray-matter/)). The largest comparison, in 3,742 Dutch adults from a population study, found that the 289 who meditated or did yoga at least an hour a week had a right amygdala about 2% smaller than the rest, even though they reported slightly more stress, and more practice didn't go with a smaller amygdala ([Gotink et al., 2018](/meditation-yoga-smaller-amygdala/)). How the amygdala responds, rather than its size, is followed in [Emotion](/themes/emotion/).

  Training studies tell a more cautious story. An influential early study reported that gray matter density rose in the hippocampus and four other areas after an eight-week MBSR course, but it had only 16 trainees, no random assignment and a wait-list comparison ([Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)). Among 26 stressed adults taking MBSR, with no comparison group, those whose stress fell most showed the largest drops in gray matter density in part of the right amygdala, though the amygdala didn't change on average ([Hölzel et al., 2010](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/)). In the largest randomized trial of its kind, which tested the same regions, eight weeks of MBSR changed gray matter no more than a matched wellness course or a waitlist ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)), even though one of the two trials it combined had found that MBSR changed how the amygdala responds to emotional pictures. A short course may change how the brain responds without measurably changing its structure. Longer, more focused training may be different. Over nine months, 332 adults trained in separate modules of attention, compassion and perspective-taking practice, and each module was linked to cortical thickening in different brain regions, changes that tracked individual gains in the trained skills, though the brain–skill links are correlations ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)). What compassion training does beyond the brain is covered in [Compassion and kindness](/themes/compassion/).

  Whether meditation slows the brain's aging is still open, and [Aging](/themes/aging/) sets these studies beside the research on telomeres. In a one-time comparison of 50 long-term meditators and 50 non-meditators aged 24 to 77, older people had less gray matter in both groups, but the link with age was weaker in meditators; differences in specific regions appeared only without the study's strict statistical correction ([Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/)). Scanning both brain volume and glucose metabolism, a pilot study found that six expert Buddhist meditators in their 60s had more gray matter and higher metabolism than 67 peers, mostly in regions that decline most with age, but with so few meditators, each scanned once, it can't show that meditation caused the difference ([Chételat et al., 2017](/older-meditators-brain-aging/)). When the question was put to a randomized test, 18 months of meditation in 137 healthy adults aged 65 and over left gray matter volume in the anterior cingulate and insula declining no differently than English lessons or no training did, and blood flow there held up slightly better with meditation, but not significantly ([Chételat et al., 2022](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/)).

  Comparing meditators with non-meditators can't separate meditation from everything else that differs between people who choose to meditate and people who don't, and the small early training studies lacked random assignment or matched comparison groups. The randomized trials that addressed both problems found no structural change from meditation beyond what their comparison groups showed.
