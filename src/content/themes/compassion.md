question: "Can meditation make you kinder?"

meta_description: "Research on compassion and loving-kindness meditation: generosity, helping, positive emotions, and how these practices show up in the brain and body."
theme_description: |
  Loving-kindness (*mettā*) and compassion (*karuṇā*) are among the oldest meditation practices in the Buddhist tradition. Practitioners deliberately call up warmth and goodwill, first toward themselves or someone they love, then toward strangers, difficult people and eventually all beings. Today these practices are taught in secular courses and apps, often with the promise that they will make people happier and kinder.

  This theme gathers studies that test that promise: whether compassion training changes how generously people act toward strangers, how practicing kindness affects everyday emotions and well-being, and what these practices look like in the brain and body of beginners and long-term practitioners.
research_summary: |
  **Compassion and loving-kindness practice seem to make people somewhat kinder and warmer, at least in the short term, though most of the studies are small.** In a randomized trial, adults who practiced compassion for 30 minutes a day for two weeks gave nearly twice as much money to help a stranger who had been treated unfairly as adults who practiced a different emotion-regulation technique, and their brains responded differently to images of suffering ([Weng et al., 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)).

  The practices also leave measurable traces in the brain. Long-term Buddhist practitioners produced unusually strong, synchronized gamma waves during compassion meditation ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)), and a single ten-minute session of loving-kindness meditation raised gamma activity in the amygdala and hippocampus of eight people with implanted electrodes, including people new to meditation ([Maher et al., 2025](/neurological-substrates-of-meditation/)). Over nine months of training, a three-month module of loving-kindness and partner exercises was linked to thickening in different parts of the cortex from modules on attention or perspective-taking, changes that tracked individual gains in the trained skills ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)). Long-term loving-kindness meditators also showed hints of a smaller brain bias toward their own face over a friend's, though the direct comparison with non-meditators was only a trend ([Trautwein et al., 2016](/loving-kindness-self-bias/)).

  Effects on the body are less clear: six weeks of compassion meditation didn't change students' hormonal, inflammatory or emotional responses to a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group, although those who practiced more had smaller rises in the inflammation marker IL-6 ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)).

  Most of this evidence comes from small samples, lab measures of kindness and short follow-ups, and several studies compare experienced practitioners with non-meditators at a single point in time, so they show what these practices can do more than how large or lasting the effects are in everyday life.
