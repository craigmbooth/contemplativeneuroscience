question: "Can meditation help with craving and addiction?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on mindfulness for craving and addiction: smoking, alcohol and drugs, opioid misuse and food cravings, and how mindfulness may loosen craving's grip."
theme_description: |
  Buddhist teaching places craving at the root of suffering. The Pali word, *taṇhā*, literally means "thirst": the restless pull toward what we want and away from what we don't. Much meditation practice trains people to notice that pull as it arises, without automatically acting on it. Modern addiction treatment has borrowed the idea. Instead of fighting or obeying a craving, people learn to notice its sensations and let it rise and pass, an approach one early study called "[surfing the urge](/urge-surfing-student-smokers/)."

  This theme gathers studies that test whether mindfulness helps people with addictions and cravings: randomized trials for smoking, for alcohol and drug use after treatment, and for opioid misuse alongside chronic pain; analyses of how mindfulness might work, for example by weakening the link between craving and use; and research on food cravings.
research_summary: |
  **Mindfulness programs for addiction have done as well as or better than standard approaches in several randomized trials, and they may work partly by loosening the link between craving and use, but many trials are small or rely on self-report, and several were led by the programs' own developers.** Two meta-analyses frame the evidence. One, pooling randomized trials across psychiatric conditions, found that mindfulness outperformed evidence-based smoking treatments (d = 0.42), while for other addictions it beat other therapies but not, statistically, no treatment, and those results were fragile ([Goldberg et al., 2018](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/)). A narrative review leaning on a meta-analysis of 34 randomized trials, which its authors co-wrote, reported that mindfulness programs reduced craving (d = 0.68) and substance use (d = 0.33), though the smoking result was not significant, many trials were small, and the first author developed one of the programs ([Garland and Howard, 2018](/mindfulness-addiction-treatment-review/)).

  For smoking, a pilot trial of 88 smokers compared four weeks of group mindfulness training with the American Lung Association's Freedom From Smoking program: 17 weeks after treatment began, 31% of those who started mindfulness training had not smoked for a week, confirmed by a breath test, against 6% in the standard program, which had been squeezed into half its usual length ([Brewer et al., 2011](/mindfulness-smoking-cessation-trial/)). In that trial's mindfulness group, the usual link between how much people craved and how much they smoked almost disappeared by the end of training (a correlation of 0.13, down from 0.58) before returning in the following weeks; there was no comparison with the other group ([Elwafi et al., 2013](/mindfulness-craving-smoking-decoupling/)). An earlier, much smaller test gave 123 college smokers, after a night without cigarettes, a single lab session in which they handled cigarettes while a recording taught them either to "surf" their urges like waves or to cope as they usually would: urges didn't differ between the groups, but over the following week the urge-surfing group smoked 1.55 fewer cigarettes a day than before (26%), while the other group didn't cut back, though smoking was self-reported, the students smoked only about five cigarettes a day, and follow-up lasted just a week ([Bowen and Marlatt, 2009](/urge-surfing-student-smokers/)). A different trial recruited students who wanted to reduce stress rather than quit: among 26 smokers randomized to two weeks (5 hours in all) of integrative body–mind training or relaxation training, breath carbon monoxide fell significantly only after the meditation training, though the trial was tiny, reported no group averages, followed up just five smokers, and was led by the method's developer ([Tang et al., 2013](/brief-meditation-smoking-reduction/)). In the brain scanner, 47 smokers new to meditation looked at smoking pictures either passively or while mindfully noticing their reactions, after brief instruction: mindful attention quieted the subgenual anterior cingulate, an area that smoking pictures had switched on, but craving fell only slightly (2.70 against 2.82 on a 5-point scale, a marginal difference among those scanned), and smoking wasn't followed up ([Westbrook et al., 2013](/mindful-attention-smoking-cues-brain/)). A later trial from Brewer's group gave 80 smokers who wanted to quit a month with either a mindfulness training app, from a company he founded, or the National Cancer Institute's QuitGuide app. Among the 67 scanned twice, both groups cut down by similar, self-reported amounts (11.4 and 8.7 cigarettes a day), and the posterior cingulate's response to smoking pictures fell no more with the mindfulness app. But only with the mindfulness app did people whose response fell more tend to cut down more (a correlation of 0.39), a link measured over the same month and not formally compared between the groups ([Janes et al., 2019](/mindfulness-app-smoking-cue-reactivity/)).

  For alcohol and drugs, the largest early trial randomized 286 adults who had just finished addiction treatment to mindfulness-based relapse prevention (MBRP), matched cognitive-behavioral relapse prevention, or usual 12-step aftercare. Over the following year, the two structured programs together cut the risk of relapse to drug use by 54% and to heavy drinking by 59% compared with usual care. Standard relapse prevention delayed the first drug use longer, but at 12 months the mindfulness group was less likely to drink heavily (8 of 83 against 17 of 72) and those who used drugs did so on fewer days, though few people were still using by then ([Bowen et al., 2014](/mindfulness-relapse-prevention-trial/)). An analysis of an earlier MBRP pilot trial with 168 adults suggests one way it might help: after usual aftercare, more depressive symptoms went with more craving two months later, which predicted more substance use, but after MBRP that chain nearly vanished, even though the two groups were equally depressed ([Witkiewitz and Bowen, 2010](/mindfulness-depression-craving-relapse/)).

  For opioids, a trial in primary care randomized 250 adults with chronic pain who were misusing their prescribed opioids to eight weeks of Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE) or a support group. At 9 months, 45% of those still in the MORE group were no longer misusing opioids, against 24% with support, and pain, craving and opioid doses fell more; over a third dropped out, misuse was largely self-reported, and the lead author wrote MORE's manual and earns royalties from it ([Garland et al., 2022](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/)).

  Craving is not only about drugs. In an uncontrolled pilot of a 28-day mindful eating app, overweight women who finished it reported eating because of a craving in 32% of text check-ins, down from 53%, but their weight barely changed, and one author, who principally developed the program, owned stock in the company that made the app ([Mason et al., 2018](/mindful-eating-app-food-craving/)).

  Most of the trials here compared mindfulness with active treatments, which is a strength, but most measured drug and alcohol use by self-report from people who knew which group they were in, none followed people for more than a year, and several were led by the people who created the programs. The evidence suggests mindfulness is a reasonable option for craving and addiction more than it shows that it is better than established treatments.
