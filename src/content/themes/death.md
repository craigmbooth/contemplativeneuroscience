question: "Can meditation change how we face death?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and death: fear of death, death contemplation, tukdam and meditative near-death experiences."
theme_description: |
  Death has a central place in Buddhist practice. Early texts recommend recollecting death (*maraṇasati*) as a regular meditation, and teachings on impermanence and not-self present clinging to a lasting self as a root of suffering, including the fear of dying. Tibetan traditions go further and treat dying itself as an opportunity for practice: the mind is said to dissolve through a sequence of stages before a "clear light" dawns, and some accomplished practitioners are believed to rest in it after death, in a state called *tukdam*.

  This theme gathers studies that put these ideas to the test: whether contemplative practice changes fear of death, how meditators and more mindful people react when they are reminded of their own mortality, and what happens in their brains, whether believing that the self is an illusion makes death less frightening, and what researchers have found when they studied tukdam and the near-death experiences some meditators say they can enter at will.
research_summary: |
  **Only a handful of studies have looked, and none yet shows that meditation eases the fear of death: denying the self didn't, brief practice eased fear of dying but not of death itself, the most encouraging results come from comparing experienced practitioners with non-meditators or from measuring everyday mindfulness rather than training it, and studies of tukdam have documented unusual cases without explaining them.** The Buddhist claim that seeing through the self removes the fear of death has been tested most directly in a cross-cultural survey. Tibetan Buddhist monastics in India rejected a lasting self more firmly than lay Tibetans, Bhutanese, Hindus or Americans, almost exactly as senior scholars said an ideal Buddhist should, yet they feared the self's destruction the most (5.74 on a 1–7 scale, against 3.04–3.63 for the other groups), and 72% said they would keep 6 extra months of life rather than give a stranger 5 or more years. None were long-term meditators, and the authors suggest that doctrine may remove the belief in a self while leaving the felt sense of one intact ([Nichols et al., 2018](/no-self-fear-of-death-monastics/)).

  Psychology's own theory of death anxiety, terror management theory, holds that reminders of death make people cling harder to their worldview. In seven experiments with 791 students, writing about their own death made those who scored low on a mindfulness questionnaire more defensive (favoring a writer who praised the U.S. over one who criticized it, judging wrongdoers more harshly), but made no significant difference for those who scored high. More mindful students also spent longer writing about their death. But mindfulness was measured, not trained, the students weren't studied as meditators, and some other trait linked to mindfulness could explain the results ([Niemiec et al., 2010](/trait-mindfulness-death-reminders/)).

  Experienced meditators may be different. In 38 Insight meditators, an automatic brain "surprise" response to their own face held up when the face appeared under death-related words; in non-meditators tested in an earlier study it had vanished, which the team had read as the brain shielding the self from death. The authors cautiously read the meditators' pattern as acceptance rather than denial, and those who showed more of it had found their earlier experiences of dissolving the sense of self more pleasant and less anxious. But the non-meditators came from a separate study, the response didn't grow with hours of practice, and fear of death wasn't measured directly ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2025](/meditation-death-acceptance-brain/)).

  A study from outside Buddhism points the same way and has the same weakness. In northern Iran, 24 long-term practitioners of Sufism, the mystical tradition of Islam, in which death is contemplated as a reunion with God, and 31 non-meditators wrote about their own death. Afterwards the Sufis reported less anxiety and completed more word fragments as death words (0.75 against 0.33 of 6), and the longer they had practiced, the less anxious they were. But anxiety and death words were measured only after the reminder, no one received a neutral one, and the groups were self-selected, so the Sufis may simply have been calmer and more used to thinking about death on any day ([Puvia et al., 2026](/sufi-meditators-death-reminders/)).

  The only randomized trial here is small and indirect. Eighty-nine New Zealand psychology students spent six weeks on daily 20-minute recordings of either mindfulness of breathing or reflection based on the Ignatian Examen. Both groups reported less fear of their own dying and of others' deaths, more fear of others' dying, and no change in fear of their own death, with no difference between the practices. For ethical reasons neither practice mentioned death, so the traditional recollection of death went untested, and with no group that didn't practice, the changes can't be credited to either ([Anālayo et al., 2022](/fear-of-death-mindfulness-contemplation/)).

  Tibetan traditions also make claims about what accomplished practitioners can do at death. In *tukdam*, a meditative state said to continue after death, the body is held to stay fresh, and warm at the heart. EEG recordings in seven practitioners believed to be in tukdam, starting 26 hours to 5 days after death, found no detectable brain response to sound, though weaker or earlier activity can't be ruled out ([Lott et al., 2021](/thukdam-eeg-after-death/)). In the same project's longest case, an 86-year-old monk photographed daily by his attendant from a week after death showed no blood pooling and supple skin until visible decay began around day 33; but a thermometer found no warmth at the heart, and the author notes that photographs alone would rarely establish an unusual state after death ([Tidwell, 2025](/tukdam-37-day-case/)). And 12 advanced Buddhist practitioners who said they could deliberately enter a near-death experience scored 24–30 out of 32 on a near-death questionnaire after that practice, against 6–10 after ordinary meditation, in a small, hand-picked group relying entirely on self-report ([Van Gordon et al., 2018](/meditation-near-death-experiences/)).

  This is a young literature. Apart from the survey, every study here is small, or split into small groups, and most compare groups at a single point in time or describe single cases. The practice Buddhist texts actually prescribe, recollecting death, has not yet been tested directly, and no study has followed people as they learn to meditate to see whether their fear of death changes.
