question: "What happens in the deepest states of meditation?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on the deepest states of meditation: the jhanas, nondual and content-free awareness, the self dissolving, and what the brain does in them."
theme_description: |
  Contemplative traditions describe states of meditation far beyond everyday calm. Theravada Buddhism maps eight *jhānas*, absorptions in which thinking quiets, bliss gives way to equanimity, and in the last four, the "formless" jhanas, sensory objects fall away altogether. Tibetan Buddhist traditions point to nondual awareness, in which the felt divide between an observer and what is observed drops away, and many traditions speak of awareness resting with no content at all. Teachers present these states as supports for insight and, ultimately, awakening (*bodhi*), though they are rare, take years of practice to reach and are hard to put into words.

  This theme gathers the small but growing body of research that studies these states directly: brain recordings of expert meditators as they move through the jhanas or rest in awareness without thoughts or a sense of self, experiments in which meditators deliberately dissolve the boundary between self and world, and interviews showing that such experiences can be peaceful, welcome or deeply unsettling.
research_summary: |
  **Science has only begun to study the deepest states of meditation, mostly in single expert meditators or small groups, and the states themselves are known only from what meditators report, but the early findings agree on one thing: these states don't look like drowsiness or sleep, and the brain's usual organization loosens.** The jhanas, the deep absorptions of Theravada Buddhism, have been scanned in two people. As a meditator with about 6,000 hours of practice moved through all eight, activity fell, compared with rest, in regions tied to outward awareness, inner speech and orientation, and rose in regions linked to executive control and subjective happiness ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)). In a meditation teacher recorded across 56 runs, brain networks were less compartmentalized and more interconnected during the jhanas than during two control tasks, most of all in the formless jhanas, with weaker slow rhythms and more complex brain signals ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). Followed moment to moment, the same meditator's brain switched between recurring states more often during jhana than in the control tasks, and a state that sets the default mode network apart from the rest of the brain grew as the jhanas deepened ([Treves et al., 2025](/jhana-dynamic-brain-states/)).

  States in which the mind empties of content point the same way. When 30 experienced meditators from many traditions spent two minutes aiming for "thoughtless emptiness," overall brain-wave power was lower than at rest, with fewer slow delta and theta waves, the opposite of drowsiness ([Hinterberger et al., 2014](/emptiness-meditation-eeg/)). A Tibetan Buddhist teacher with more than 50,000 hours of practice who reported awareness with no thoughts, sensations or sense of self showed more theta and much less alpha activity than at rest, and weaker connections at the back of the default mode network ([Winter et al., 2020](/content-free-awareness-expert-meditator/)). In 22 experienced Tibetan Buddhist meditators, the brain's inward-facing and outward-facing systems, which usually work against each other, were less opposed in nondual awareness than when simply looking at a dot ([Josipovic et al., 2012](/nondual-awareness-brain-networks/)). What such "pure awareness" is like from the inside has been mapped in a survey of 1,403 meditators who each rated one remembered experience: their answers grouped into 12 dimensions, among them peace and bliss, wakeful presence, self-knowing clarity, luminosity and an absence of self, though the model was tentative and fit only moderately ([Gamma and Metzinger, 2021](/pure-awareness-questionnaire/)).

  Some practitioners describe going further still, into *cessation*, a complete gap in consciousness that Buddhist texts call *nirodha*. In the first EEG study of these events, one meditator with over 23,000 hours of practice had 37 reported cessations across 29 sessions; compared with ordinary stretches of the same meditation, alpha brain waves faded from about 40 seconds before each event and were lowest just after it. He is also a co-author, and the pattern may be his alone ([Chowdhury et al., 2023](/meditation-cessation-eeg/)).

  Other studies ask meditators to loosen the ordinary frame of experience on cue. When 12 long-term Vipassana meditators tried to experience the present without being "in the center," beta activity fell in a network of prefrontal and parietal regions ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/)); when the same meditators tried "to be outside time" or "not to be in the center of space," theta activity rose in a shared set of regions the authors tie to the felt sense of the body ([Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2013](/timelessness-meditation-meg/)). One man with about 40 years of practice dissolved his felt boundary between self and world in steps, and beta rhythms faded step by step in right-side parietal areas ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)). In interviews from the same sessions, he described time, location, agency, ownership and any sense of a center fading to nothing as the boundary vanished, while a faint feel of the body and of touch remained ([Ataria et al., 2015](/sense-of-boundaries-case-study/)). Interviews with 46 meditators trained to do the same found that relaxing and letting go, rather than effort, went with the deepest dissolution ([Nave et al., 2021](/self-boundary-dissolution-letting-go/)).

  Deep practice can also bring unusual perceptions and bodily feats. In open-ended interviews about unexpected experiences, 9 of 28 Buddhist practitioners described lights unprompted, from small colored globes and "little stars" to a brightening of the whole visual field, closely matching the *nimitta* and other signs in Buddhist texts and the lights seen in sensory-deprivation studies; the authors' idea that meditation works partly like sensory deprivation is untested ([Lindahl et al., 2014](/meditation-light-experiences/)). In eastern Tibet, six experienced practitioners of g-tummo, the "inner fire" practice, raised their armpit temperature from about 36.5°C to an average 37.6°C in rooms near freezing; a forceful breath-holding technique alone warmed them, and the visualization seemed to push the heat higher, though the conditions always ran in the same order ([Kozhevnikov et al., 2013](/g-tummo-body-heat/)). Twelve advanced Buddhist practitioners who said they could deliberately enter a near-death experience scored 24–30 out of 32 on a near-death questionnaire after that practice, against 6–10 after ordinary meditation, but everything rested on self-report from a small, hand-picked group ([Van Gordon et al., 2018](/meditation-near-death-experiences/)). And when researchers recorded brain responses to sound in seven Tibetan Buddhist practitioners believed to be in *tukdam*, a meditative state said to continue after death, starting 26 hours to 5 days after death, they found no detectable brain activity, although weaker or earlier activity can't be ruled out ([Lott et al., 2021](/thukdam-eeg-after-death/)). A later case report from the same project describes an 86-year-old monk whose body, photographed daily by his attendant from a week after death, showed no blood pooling and little visible decay for about a month; it is a single case, recorded with a phone and a thermometer and with no comparison body ([Tidwell, 2025](/tukdam-37-day-case/)).

  What these states mean is less clear. A review comparing meditation with psychedelic drugs argues that "self-loss" is not one thing, and that a passing state of self-loss is not the same as a lasting change in who one is ([Millière et al., 2018](/meditation-psychedelics-self-loss/)). Nor are these experiences always welcome. Among 59 kinds of difficulty described by 60 Western Buddhist meditators who had struggled with their practice, one of seven broad domains was changes in the sense of self ([Lindahl et al., 2017](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/)), while six long-term Transcendental Meditation practitioners described feeling detached from themselves as calm "witnessing" rather than as a symptom, which the author attributes to how they understood it ([Castillo, 1990](/depersonalization-and-meditation/)).

  Nearly all of this evidence comes from one or a few experienced meditators, usually with no non-meditator comparison, and several of the brain studies come from the same small number of people. So the research describes what deep states can look like in the brain and body more than how they arise, how common they are, or whether they lead to the lasting changes that traditions call awakening, which no study here has followed.
