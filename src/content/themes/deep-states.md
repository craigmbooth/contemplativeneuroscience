question: "What happens in the deepest states of meditation?"

meta_description: "Research on the deepest states of meditation: the jhanas, nondual and content-free awareness, the self dissolving, and what the brain does in them."
theme_description: |
  Contemplative traditions describe states of meditation far beyond everyday calm. Theravada Buddhism maps eight *jhānas*, absorptions in which thinking quiets, bliss gives way to equanimity, and in the last four, the "formless" jhanas, sensory objects fall away altogether. Tibetan Buddhist traditions point to nondual awareness, in which the felt divide between an observer and what is observed drops away, and many traditions speak of awareness resting with no content at all. Teachers present these states as supports for insight and, ultimately, awakening (*bodhi*), though they are rare, take years of practice to reach and are hard to put into words.

  This theme gathers the small but growing body of research that studies these states directly: brain recordings of expert meditators as they move through the jhanas or rest in awareness without thoughts or a sense of self, experiments in which meditators deliberately dissolve the boundary between self and world, and interviews showing that such experiences can be peaceful, welcome or deeply unsettling.
research_summary: |
  **Science has only begun to study the deepest states of meditation, mostly in single expert meditators, but the early findings agree: as experience empties out, the brain's usual divisions loosen.** The jhanas have been scanned twice, each time in one person. As a meditator with about 6,000 hours of practice moved through all eight, activity fell, compared with rest, in brain regions tied to outward awareness, inner speech and orientation, and rose in regions linked to executive control and subjective happiness, matching every one of the team's predictions ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)). A larger study of one meditation teacher, with 56 recorded runs in an ultra-high-field scanner and on EEG, found brain networks that were less compartmentalized and more interconnected during the jhanas than during two control tasks, most of all in the formless jhanas, along with weaker slow brain rhythms and more complex brain signals; bliss, which peaked in the second jhana, had its own signature ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). Many of the scanner differences were clear against counting but weaker against recalling recent events.

  States of awareness without content point the same way. A Tibetan Buddhist teacher with more than 50,000 hours of practice reported reaching awareness with no thoughts, sensations or sense of self in the last minutes of a scanned meditation; compared with rest, those minutes showed more theta and much less alpha activity, and weaker connections at the back of the default mode network ([Winter et al., 2020](/content-free-awareness-expert-meditator/)). In 22 experienced Tibetan Buddhist meditators, the brain's inward-facing and outward-facing systems, which usually work against each other, were less opposed in nondual awareness than when simply looking at a dot, and more opposed in focused attention ([Josipovic et al., 2012](/nondual-awareness-brain-networks/)).

  Some studies ask meditators to dissolve the sense of self on cue. When 12 long-term Vipassana meditators tried to experience the present without being "in the center," slower beta activity fell in a network of prefrontal and parietal regions ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/)). One man with about 40 years of practice dissolved his felt boundary between self and world in steps, and beta rhythms faded step by step, mostly in right-side parietal areas tied to the sense of self and body; ten other meditators showed a similar, smaller pattern ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)). Interviews with 46 meditators trained to alternate between keeping and dissolving that boundary found that their accounts varied mainly along a single scale of depth, and that relaxing and letting go, rather than effort, went with the deepest dissolution ([Nave et al., 2021](/self-boundary-dissolution-letting-go/)).

  What these states mean is less clear. A review comparing meditation with psychedelic drugs argues that "self-loss" is not one thing, since self-related thoughts, the felt ownership of one's body and the sense of where one is can each fade separately, and that a passing state of self-loss is not the same as a lasting change in who one is ([Millière et al., 2018](/meditation-psychedelics-self-loss/)). Nor are these experiences always welcome. Among 59 kinds of difficulty described by 60 Western Buddhist meditators who had struggled with their practice, one of seven broad domains was changes in the sense of self ([Lindahl et al., 2017](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/)), while six long-term Transcendental Meditation practitioners described feeling detached from themselves as calm "witnessing" rather than as a symptom, which the author attributes to how they understood it ([Castillo, 1990](/depersonalization-and-meditation/)).

  Nearly all of this evidence comes from one or a few experienced meditators, usually with no non-meditator comparison, and the states themselves are known only from what the meditators report. So the research describes what deep states can look like in the brain more than how they arise, how common they are, or whether they lead to the lasting changes that traditions call awakening, which no study here has followed.
