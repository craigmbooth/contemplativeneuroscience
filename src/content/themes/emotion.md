title: Meditation and Emotion
theme_key: emotion
short_title: "Emotion"
question: "Can meditation change how we react to emotions?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and emotion: amygdala reactivity, harsh self-beliefs in social anxiety, compassion and emotional sounds, and positive mood."
theme_description: |
  Many contemplative traditions teach that we can relate differently to emotions, noticing them as they arise rather than being swept along by them. This theme gathers studies of how practice changes emotional reactions, both in the brain, especially the amygdala and the insula, and in how people say they feel: how the brain responds to emotional pictures and sounds, how people with social anxiety handle harsh thoughts about themselves, and whether kindness practices and breathing exercises lift everyday mood.
research_summary: |
  **Training can change how the brain responds to emotional material, including a quieter amygdala in two randomized trials, but most studies are small, several have no matched comparison group, and the everyday gains, such as modest rises in positive mood, rest on self-reports.** The amygdala, which flags emotionally important events, is the most studied region. In a small randomized trial, eight weeks of mindful-attention training lowered the right amygdala's response to emotional photos more than a health discussion class did, even though people weren't meditating in the scanner, while compassion training showed no clear change ([Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/)). In a larger trial, MBSR participants' right amygdala responded less to positive pictures than that of people in a matched health program, though not significantly less to negative ones, and long-term meditators showed the same smaller response to positive pictures; after MBSR, the amygdala also worked more closely with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, a region that helps keep it in check ([Kral et al., 2018](/mindfulness-amygdala-reactivity/)). Structure may follow stress: among 26 stressed adults taking MBSR, with no comparison group, those whose stress fell most showed the largest drops in gray matter density in part of the right amygdala, though the amygdala didn't change on average ([Hölzel et al., 2010](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/)).

  Meditation may change how people meet difficult feelings as well as how strongly they react. In 14 adults with social anxiety disorder scanned before and after an MBSR course, turning to the breath while reading harsh beliefs about themselves, such as "People always judge me," left them less upset after the course and engaged parietal and visual attention areas more, though the study had no comparison group ([Goldin and Gross, 2010](/mbsr-social-anxiety-negative-self-beliefs/)). Compassion practice can heighten some emotional responses rather than dampen them: while generating compassion, 15 long-term Tibetan Buddhist practitioners' insula responded more than that of 15 novices to emotional sounds, most of all to a woman in distress, though the groups were compared only once ([Lutz et al., 2008](/compassion-meditation-insula-emotional-sounds/)).

  In everyday life, the clearest changes are in positive mood. In a randomized trial with 139 employees, a 7-week loving-kindness workshop slowly raised daily positive emotions from about the third week compared with a wait-list, while negative emotions didn't change, and those whose positive emotions rose most reported gains in personal resources and life satisfaction, a link that is correlational ([Fredrickson et al., 2008](/loving-kindness-positive-emotions-resources/)). In a month-long remote trial, anxiety and negative mood fell after every session by similar amounts whether people did mindfulness meditation or one of three breathing exercises, but 5 minutes a day of cyclic sighing raised positive mood more than mindfulness did ([Balban et al., 2023](/cyclic-sighing-breathwork-mood/)).

  This is a small literature. The brain studies scanned at most a few dozen people per group, the amygdala findings differ between positive and negative pictures and between kinds of training, and the mood results rest on what people reported about themselves, often against a wait-list rather than a matched program.
