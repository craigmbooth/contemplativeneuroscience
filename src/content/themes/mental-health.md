short_title: "Mental health"
question: "Can meditation ease stress, anxiety and depression?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and mental health: stress, anxiety, low mood and depression, and how meditation compares with other programs."
theme_description: |
  Stress relief is the most common reason people give for starting to meditate, and mindfulness-based programs are now offered for everything from workplace stress to preventing relapse into depression. But how much do they actually help, and for whom?

  This theme gathers studies that measured stress, anxiety, mood and depression before and after meditation training. Some compare meditation with doing nothing; others, more demandingly, compare it with relaxation training, health classes or other programs matched for time and attention. Reading them together shows where the benefits look solid, where a well-run comparison program does just as well, and where the evidence is still thin.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation training can ease stress, anxiety and low mood, but the gains are usually modest, other active programs often do as well, a large trial in schools found no benefit, and the firmest evidence is for preventing relapse into depression.** The broadest reviews agree on this shape. Across 47 randomized trials that compared meditation programs with programs matched for time and attention, mindfulness programs eased anxiety and depression, though only slightly, with weaker evidence for stress and none that meditation did better than exercise, relaxation or therapy ([Goyal et al., 2014](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/)). Across 136 trials in people without a diagnosis, mindfulness courses eased anxiety, depression and distress compared with doing nothing, but were no better than other active practices such as exercise, and every trial was at high risk of bias ([Galante et al., 2021](/mindfulness-programs-mental-health-meta-analysis/)). And across 142 trials in people with psychiatric disorders, mindfulness therapies did better than no treatment and about as well as established treatments such as CBT and antidepressants, though results varied widely and there were signs of publication bias ([Goldberg et al., 2018](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/)). An earlier meta-analysis shows why comparison groups matter: across 39 mostly uncontrolled studies, anxiety and depression fell moderately over a course of mindfulness therapy, and by more in people being treated for anxiety disorders or depression, but in the 16 studies with a comparison group, effects against wait-lists or usual care were smaller, and the authors called the controlled results preliminary ([Hofmann et al., 2010](/mindfulness-therapy-anxiety-depression-meta-analysis/)).

  Individual trials show the same mix. Among 616 Cambridge students, those offered an 8-week mindfulness course were less distressed at exam time than those given usual support, with 37% against 57% scoring in the clinical range ([Galante et al., 2018](/mindful-student-study/)). Employees given six weeks of training after a one-day workshop reported less stress during a company restructuring than colleagues who had the workshop alone ([Chin et al., 2019](/mindfulness-training-workplace-stress/)), and ten 10-minute sessions of a meditation app improved mood and irritability more than an audiobook, in a trial run by the app maker's staff ([Economides et al., 2018](/headspace-app-stress-irritability/)). Five days of integrative body–mind training improved anxiety, depression, anger and vigor scores more than relaxation training ([Tang et al., 2007](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)), and in older adults with sleep problems a six-week mindfulness course reduced depressive symptoms and fatigue, which a matched sleep hygiene course did not ([Black et al., 2015](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/)). Among 39 family dementia caregivers with mild depressive symptoms, more of those who did 12 minutes a day of Kirtan Kriya chanting meditation improved by half or more on depression (65% against 31%) than those who relaxed to music, though average depression scores fell in both groups by a similar amount, and only those who finished were analyzed ([Lavretsky et al., 2013](/kirtan-kriya-dementia-caregivers/)). In 40 older adults, an 8-week MBSR course eased loneliness, which rose slightly in a wait-list group, though a wait-list can't rule out the benefit of simply meeting as a group ([Creswell et al., 2012](/mbsr-loneliness-older-adults/)). A seven-week loving-kindness workshop slowly raised working adults' daily positive emotions compared with a wait-list, though the difference wasn't significant when everyone randomized was counted ([Fredrickson et al., 2008](/loving-kindness-positive-emotions-resources/)).

  But distress and anxiety fell just as much after a health program matched to MBSR as after MBSR itself ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)); six weeks of compassion meditation didn't change distress during a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)); and beginners given four 20-minute mindfulness sessions felt much less anxious straight after each one, but their overall mood and depression scores improved no more than those of students who listened to an audiobook ([Zeidan et al., 2010](/brief-mindfulness-training-cognition/)). In a month-long remote trial of 108 mostly student adults, 5 minutes a day of mindfulness eased anxiety straight after each session about as much as 5 minutes of breathing exercises did, while one exercise, cyclic sighing, lifted positive mood more than mindfulness; the trial was small, exploratory and registered only afterward ([Balban et al., 2023](/cyclic-sighing-breathwork-mood/)). The largest single trial ruled out any important benefit. When 84 UK secondary schools with 8,376 students aged 11–14 were randomly assigned to ten teacher-led mindfulness lessons or their usual social-emotional teaching, risk of depression, social, emotional and behavioral difficulties, and well-being were the same in both groups a year later, and students rated the lessons middling and rarely practiced at home ([Kuyken et al., 2022](/school-mindfulness-myriad-trial/)).

  In anxiety disorders, the picture is similar. In 89 adults with generalized anxiety disorder, an 8-week MBSR course and a stress management class matched minute for minute both lowered clinician-rated anxiety, the trial's main outcome, with no significant difference between them, but MBSR did better on clinicians' overall ratings (66% against 40% much or very much improved), self-rated anxiety and distress during a lab stress test ([Hoge et al., 2013](/mbsr-generalized-anxiety-trial/)). In 38 of the trial's participants, MBSR raised self-reported decentering, seeing thoughts as passing events, more than the class did, and that gain statistically accounted for MBSR's larger drop in anxiety ([Hoge et al., 2015](/mbsr-anxiety-decentering/)). In the same trial, blood drawn during a lab stress test showed a smaller rise in the stress hormone ACTH after MBSR than after the class, though not a smaller cortisol response ([Hoge et al., 2018](/mbsr-anxiety-stress-hormones-inflammation/)). In 16 adults with social anxiety disorder, an MBSR course was followed by less social anxiety, depression and rumination, and by feeling less upset when they turned to the breath while reading harsh beliefs about themselves, though with no comparison group these changes can't be credited to the course itself ([Goldin and Gross, 2010](/mbsr-social-anxiety-negative-self-beliefs/)).

  For people in remission from recurrent depression, the evidence is stronger. Pooling each patient's data from nine trials of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), 38% relapsed within 60 weeks after MBCT against 49% without it, and MBCT did at least as well as maintenance antidepressants, with the most benefit for people who still had some symptoms; most of the trials were run by MBCT's proponents, and the advantage faded over time ([Kuyken et al., 2016](/mbct-depression-relapse-meta-analysis/)). The largest of those trials found that MBCT with support to come off antidepressants was no better than staying on them, with 44% and 47% relapsing over two years at similar cost ([Kuyken et al., 2015](/mbct-vs-antidepressants-relapse/)). In a small trial of antidepressant users with lingering sleep problems, depression scores fell after MBCT and rose in a wait-list group, though the difference was only a trend ([Britton et al., 2012](/mbct-sleep-antidepressant-users/)).

  How meditation might help is less clear. One possible route runs through attention: in a smartphone study of 2,250 adults, people were less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing, whatever they were doing ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/)), though that study didn't test meditation. Another may run through the amygdala, which flags emotionally important events. Among 26 stressed adults who took MBSR, with no comparison group, those whose stress fell most showed the largest drops in gray matter density in a small part of the right amygdala, though the amygdala didn't change on average ([Hölzel et al., 2010](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/)). In a randomized trial against a matched health program, MBSR dampened the amygdala's response to positive pictures more clearly than to negative ones ([Kral et al., 2018](/mindfulness-amygdala-reactivity/)), and in a smaller trial, eight weeks of mindful-attention training lowered its response to emotional photos more than a health class did, but depression and anxiety scores in these healthy volunteers didn't change differently between groups ([Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/)). A review from the Center for Healthy Minds proposes that well-being rests on four trainable skills, awareness, connection, insight and purpose, though its authors say little is known about the best way to train them ([Dahl et al., 2020](/well-being-four-skills/)). Which practice matters is also unclear: when MBCT was split into focused-attention and open-monitoring versions for adults with depression symptoms, focused attention and full MBCT shifted an EEG marker of emotion and open monitoring didn't, but all three eased how upsetting negative pictures felt, with no non-meditating comparison group ([Brown et al., 2022](/focused-attention-open-monitoring-emotion/)).

  Apart from the reviews and the schools trial, most of these studies are small, rely on questionnaires and measure outcomes soon after training, and many recruited people who were not seeking treatment. Meditation can also stir up difficult experiences, anxiety among them: in a study of adults with depression symptoms who took an MBCT course, 6–14% reported an effect with lasting bad consequences ([Britton et al., 2021](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/); see [adverse effects](/themes/adverse-effects/)).
