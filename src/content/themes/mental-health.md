question: "Can meditation ease stress, anxiety and depression?"

meta_description: "Research on meditation and mental health: stress, anxiety, low mood and depression, and how meditation compares with other programs."
theme_description: |
  Stress relief is the most common reason people give for starting to meditate, and mindfulness-based programs are now offered for everything from workplace stress to preventing relapse into depression. But how much do they actually help, and for whom?

  This theme gathers studies that measured stress, anxiety, mood and depression before and after meditation training. Some compare meditation with doing nothing; others, more demandingly, compare it with relaxation training, health classes or other programs matched for time and attention. Reading them together shows where the benefits look solid, where a well-run comparison program does just as well, and where the evidence is still thin.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation training can ease stress, anxiety and low mood, but the gains are usually modest, well-matched comparison programs sometimes do as well, and the firmest evidence is for preventing relapse into depression.** The broadest review here, of 47 randomized trials that compared meditation programs with programs matched for time and attention, found moderate-strength evidence that mindfulness programs ease anxiety and depression, though only slightly, weaker evidence for stress, and no evidence that meditation did better than exercise, relaxation or therapy ([Goyal et al., 2014](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/)). Individual trials show the same mix. Five days of 20-minute integrative body–mind training improved anxiety, depression, anger and vigor scores more than relaxation training ([Tang et al., 2007](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)), and in older adults with sleep problems a six-week mindfulness course reduced depressive symptoms and fatigue, which a matched sleep hygiene course did not ([Black et al., 2015](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/)). But distress and anxiety fell just as much after a health program matched to MBSR in format, teaching and home practice as after MBSR itself ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)); six weeks of compassion meditation didn't change distress during a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)); and beginners given four 20-minute mindfulness sessions felt much less anxious straight after each one, but their overall mood and depression scores improved no more than those of students who listened to an audiobook ([Zeidan et al., 2010](/brief-mindfulness-training-cognition/)).

  For people in remission from recurrent depression, the evidence is stronger. Pooling each patient's data from nine trials of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), 38% relapsed within 60 weeks after MBCT against 49% without it, and MBCT did at least as well as maintenance antidepressants, with the most benefit for people who still had some symptoms; most of the trials were run by MBCT's proponents, and the advantage faded over time ([Kuyken et al., 2016](/mbct-depression-relapse-meta-analysis/)). The largest of those trials found that MBCT with support to come off antidepressants was no better than staying on them, with 44% and 47% relapsing over two years at similar cost ([Kuyken et al., 2015](/mbct-vs-antidepressants-relapse/)). In a small trial of antidepressant users with lingering sleep problems, depression scores fell after MBCT and rose in a wait-list group, though the difference was only a trend ([Britton et al., 2012](/mbct-sleep-antidepressant-users/)).

  How meditation might help is less clear. One possible route runs through attention: in a smartphone study of 2,250 adults, people were less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing, whatever they were doing ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/)), though that study didn't test meditation. Another may run through the brain's threat system: among 26 stressed adults who took MBSR, with no comparison group, those whose stress fell most showed the largest drops in gray matter density in a small part of the right amygdala, though the amygdala didn't change on average ([Hölzel et al., 2010](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/)).

  Apart from the reviews, most of these studies are small, rely on questionnaires, measure outcomes soon after training and recruited people who were not seeking treatment. Meditation can also stir up difficult experiences, anxiety among them: in a study of adults with depression symptoms who took an MBCT course, 6–14% reported an effect with lasting bad consequences ([Britton et al., 2021](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/); see [adverse effects](/themes/adverse-effects/)).
