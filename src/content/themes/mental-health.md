question: "Can meditation ease stress, anxiety and depression?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and mental health: stress, anxiety, low mood and depression, and how meditation compares with other programs."
theme_description: |
  Stress relief is the most common reason people give for starting to meditate, and mindfulness-based programs are now offered for everything from workplace stress to preventing relapse into depression. But how much do they actually help, and for whom?

  This theme gathers studies that measured stress, anxiety, mood and depression before and after meditation training. Some compare meditation with doing nothing; others, more demandingly, compare it with relaxation training, health classes or other programs matched for time and attention. Reading them together shows where the benefits look solid, where a well-run comparison program does just as well, and where the evidence is still thin.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation training often eases stress, anxiety and low mood, but the gains in these studies are modest, and well-matched comparison programs sometimes do as well.** In a randomized trial, five days of 20-minute integrative body–mind training improved anxiety, depression, anger and vigor scores more than relaxation training ([Tang et al., 2007](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)). In older adults with sleep problems, a six-week mindfulness course reduced depressive symptoms and fatigue, which a matched sleep hygiene course did not ([Black et al., 2015](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/)). Among antidepressant users in remission from recurrent depression, depression scores fell after an 8-week MBCT course and rose in a wait-list group, though in this small trial the difference was only a trend ([Britton et al., 2012](/mbct-sleep-antidepressant-users/)).

  The comparison matters. Distress and anxiety fell just as much after a health program matched to MBSR in format, teaching and home practice as after MBSR itself, and depression scores didn't change in either group ([Rosenkranz et al., 2013](/mbsr-active-control-skin-inflammation/)). Six weeks of compassion meditation didn't change distress during a lab stress test compared with a health discussion group ([Pace et al., 2009](/compassion-meditation-practice-stress-inflammation/)). And beginners given four 20-minute mindfulness sessions felt much less anxious straight after each one, but their overall mood and depression scores improved no more than those of students who listened to an audiobook ([Zeidan et al., 2010](/brief-mindfulness-training-cognition/)).

  One possible route runs through attention: in a smartphone study of 2,250 adults, people were less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing, whatever they were doing ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/)), though that study didn't test meditation.

  Most of these trials are small, rely on questionnaires, measure outcomes soon after training and recruited people who were not seeking treatment. Meditation can also stir up difficult experiences, anxiety among them: in a study of adults with depression symptoms who took an MBCT course, 6–14% reported an effect with lasting bad consequences ([Britton et al., 2021](/mindfulness-course-adverse-effects/); see [adverse effects](/themes/adverse-effects/)).
