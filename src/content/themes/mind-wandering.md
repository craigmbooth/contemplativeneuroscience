theme_description: |
  Meditation is often praised for improving focus, but what exactly changes in the mind? This theme explores how contemplative practice influences attention regulation, reduces mind-wandering, and strengthens executive control. These studies illuminate the cognitive mechanisms behind staying present—and the costs of failing to do so.
research_summary: |
  **Minds wander a lot, it costs us, and mindfulness training appears to help.** [Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010)](/wandering-mind-unhappy-mind/) sampled 2,250 people through a smartphone app and found their minds wandered about 47% of the time, and that people were less happy while mind wandering, whatever they were doing; whether meditation lifts mood is the subject of [Mental health](/themes/mental-health/). [McVay et al. (2009)](/mind-wandering-contexts/) found that students whose minds wandered more during a lab attention task also reported more mind wandering over a week of daily life, which suggests a stable trait, though their accuracy and response-time variability on that task didn't predict it.

  On the meditation side, a meta-analysis of 111 randomized trials ([Zainal and Newman, 2023](/mindfulness-cognitive-function-survey/)) found that, compared with active control activities, mindfulness training brought small-to-moderate gains in executive and sustained attention and in accuracy on working memory, inhibition and task-switching tests, but no gains in speed. Gains can appear quickly: in an early randomized trial, undergraduates who did five days of 20-minute integrative body–mind training improved more at resolving conflicting cues on an attention test, and in mood, than students given relaxation training ([Tang et al., 2007](/five-days-meditation-attention-stress/)). Even less practice may shift some scores: beginners who had four 20-minute sessions of breath-focused mindfulness improved more than students listening to an audiobook on three of six cognitive measures, though they were tested straight after meditating and the groups were assigned by sign-up week ([Zeidan et al., 2010](/brief-mindfulness-training-cognition/)). Even a single session can shift alertness for a while: ten beginners responded faster on a reaction-time test after 40 minutes of breath-focused meditation, while nearly all slowed after a 40-minute nap or ordinary activity, though many were back to baseline an hour later ([Kaul et al., 2010](/meditation-reaction-time-sleep-need/)); the study's findings on sleep are in [Sleep](/themes/sleep/). Slow breathing alone gave less: after eight weeks of coached belly breathing, office workers improved on a sustained attention test only somewhat more than untrained controls, who also improved ([Ma et al., 2017](/diaphragmatic-breathing-attention-cortisol/)), though their cortisol fell (see [Stress and the body](/themes/biological-health/)). And in older adults, a review found the evidence on attention mixed: MBSR's gain on a mental-flexibility test against a wait-list had faded by follow-up, and MBSR did no better than a reading and relaxation group ([Fountain-Zaragoza and Prakash, 2017](/mindfulness-healthy-aging-review/)). In a randomized trial of 48 undergraduates, eight mindfulness classes over two weeks improved scores on a reading test drawn from the GRE and on a working memory task more than a nutrition class did, and reduced self-reported mind wandering during them; a statistical analysis tied the gains to less mind wandering only among the most distractible students ([Mrazek et al., 2013](/mindfulness-gre-working-memory/)). Not every group shows such gains: in 68 preschoolers, a 12-week mindfulness-based Kindness Curriculum didn't significantly change delay of gratification, cognitive flexibility or inhibitory control compared with a wait-list ([Flook et al., 2015](/kindness-curriculum-preschool/)). Over a three-month retreat, 17 practitioners got better at catching a second target that quickly followed a first (their "attentional blink" shrank), while novices given a one-hour class and brief daily practice changed less, though the groups weren't randomized ([Slagter et al., 2007](/meditation-retreat-attentional-blink/)). In a randomized wait-list trial of a similar three-month Shamatha retreat, experienced meditators got better at telling apart lines of slightly different length, a gain the wait-list group matched during its own later retreat and that lasted about five months; attention held up better over a 32-minute task only when the target stayed fixed, which the authors attribute to sharper perception rather than a direct boost to sustained attention ([MacLean et al., 2010](/shamatha-retreat-perception-vigilance/)). [Shields et al. (2020)](/deconstructing-concentration-interference-control/) found that a three-month retreat strengthened controlled attention without reducing automatic reactions to distractions, and that participants with higher inflammation had weaker attention control (see [Inflammation and immunity](/themes/immunity/)).

  Brain studies point to the default mode network, which is associated with mind wandering. [Garrison et al. (2015)](/reduced-dmn-active-task/) found that in experienced meditators, but not non-meditators, two of its hubs were less active during meditation than during an active word task. In an early fMRI study, single words briefly interrupted breath focus, seeding a short train of thought; in 12 Zen meditators the default mode response to a word's meaning ended sooner than in 12 non-meditators, a hint, from a small one-time comparison, that practice helps people let go of thoughts ([Pagnoni et al., 2008](/zen-meditation-default-mode-words/)). Even in 23 non-meditators, silently repeating "one" quieted default mode regions compared with rest, and a separate group reported fewer thoughts while repeating, especially about themselves and the future, though their sense of bodily and sensory experience dropped too ([Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2015](/silent-word-repetition-brain-deactivation/)). Looser attention may have its uses as well: when 19 experienced meditators did single sessions of each style, they listed more, and more original, uses for everyday objects after open monitoring than after focused attention or a visualization exercise, while focused attention didn't improve word-puzzle solving; the participants knew which practice they were doing ([Colzato et al., 2012](/open-monitoring-meditation-creativity/)). Practices also differ in what they do to thinking: over nine months of training, 229 adults rated themselves less distracted by thoughts after each of four meditations, while observing thoughts raised awareness of thinking the most, though these were self-ratings with no non-meditating comparison ([Kok and Singer, 2017](/four-meditations-fingerprints/)). The default mode network's link with self-related thinking is followed in [The self](/themes/self/).

  The pattern across these studies is that meditation seems to strengthen deliberate, goal-directed attention rather than making people immune to distraction, though most of the studies are small and the gains haven't appeared in every group tested.
