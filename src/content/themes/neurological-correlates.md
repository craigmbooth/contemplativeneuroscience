theme_description: |
  What happens in the brain during meditation? This theme collects studies that use neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques to map how contemplative states modulate neural activity. From changes in activation during pain, to shifts in attention-related networks and salience processing, these findings highlight the diverse ways meditation reshapes brain function. Importantly, the observed changes are often specific to the kind of meditative practice used—whether focused attention, open presence, or compassion training—suggesting that different intentions may sculpt the brain in different ways. This research offers a window into the elusive terrain of inner experience by grounding it in measurable neural signatures.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation produces distinct, measurable brain states, and they depend on the kind of practice.** Long-term practitioners of loving-kindness and compassion meditation generated unusually high-amplitude gamma waves, which rose with years of practice and stayed elevated at rest ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)). A later study from the same group found more gamma activity in meditators even during sleep, linked to how much they practiced each day ([Ferrarelli et al., 2013](/meditators-sleep-gamma/)). In the same meditators, a single intensive day of practice raised slower, low-frequency activity in that night's sleep, a short-term trace alongside the long-term gamma difference ([Dentico et al., 2016](/meditation-day-sleep-brain-waves/)). Even a single session of loving-kindness meditation raised gamma activity in the amygdala and hippocampus of people with implanted electrodes ([Maher et al., 2025](/neurological-substrates-of-meditation/)).

  Other practices look different. In a case study of jhana meditation, an experienced meditator's brain showed reduced activity in areas tied to outward awareness and increased activity in reward areas ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)), a pattern the authors note differs from loving-kindness and Transcendental Meditation studies. A more detailed case study, which scanned another expert dozens of times as he moved through all eight jhanas, linked these states to less compartmentalized, more interconnected brain networks ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). Practice can also change behavior: two weeks of compassion training increased altruistic giving, and the change was linked to shifts in brain responses to suffering ([Weng et al., 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)). Even a three-day mindfulness retreat strengthened the resting link between the default mode network and a self-regulation region, a change tied to lower inflammation four months later ([Creswell et al., 2016](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/)). Over a three-month retreat, practitioners' brain response to the first of two quickly following targets (the P3b) shrank, and those whose response shrank most generally got best at catching the second ([Slagter et al., 2007](/meditation-retreat-attentional-blink/)).

  Changes to brain structure are a different question. A 2014 meta-analysis of 21 structural brain-imaging studies, most of them one-time comparisons of long-term meditators with non-meditators, found differences recurring in a handful of regions, including the front of the prefrontal cortex, the anterior cingulate cortex, the insula and the hippocampus, but the authors' adjusted average difference was modest, and the smallest studies reported the largest effects, a sign that negative results may have gone unpublished ([Fox et al., 2014](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/)). An influential early study reported that gray matter density rose in the hippocampus and four other areas after an eight-week MBSR course, but it had only 16 trainees, no random assignment and a wait-list comparison ([Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)). In the largest randomized trial of its kind, which tested the same regions, eight weeks of MBSR changed gray matter no more than a matched wellness course or a waitlist ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)), even though earlier analyses of the same participants found changes in brain function. A short course may change how the brain responds without measurably changing its structure. Longer, more focused training may be different. Over nine months, separate modules of attention, compassion and perspective-taking practice were each linked to cortical thickening in different brain regions, and the changes tracked individual gains in the trained skills ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)).

  Most of these studies are small, and several compare experts with novices at a single point in time, so they map what meditation looks like in the brain more than they prove how it changes it.
