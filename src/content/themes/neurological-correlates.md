question: "What does meditation do to the brain?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "How meditation changes brain activity: fMRI, EEG, and intracranial studies of the networks, regions, and rhythms of meditative states."
theme_description: |
  What happens in the brain during meditation? This theme collects studies that use neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques to map how contemplative states modulate neural activity. From changes in activation during pain, to shifts in attention-related networks and salience processing, these findings highlight the diverse ways meditation reshapes brain function. Importantly, the observed changes are often specific to the kind of meditative practice used—whether focused attention, open presence, or compassion training—suggesting that different intentions may sculpt the brain in different ways. This research offers a window into the elusive terrain of inner experience by grounding it in measurable neural signatures.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation produces distinct, measurable brain states that depend partly on the kind of practice, but the largest randomized trials so far haven't found that it changes brain structure.** Long-term practitioners of loving-kindness and compassion meditation generated unusually high-amplitude gamma waves, which rose with years of practice and stayed elevated at rest ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)). A later study from the same group found more gamma activity in meditators even during sleep, linked to how much they practiced each day ([Ferrarelli et al., 2013](/meditators-sleep-gamma/)). In the same meditators, a single intensive day of practice raised slower, low-frequency activity in that night's sleep, a short-term trace alongside the long-term gamma difference ([Dentico et al., 2016](/meditation-day-sleep-brain-waves/)). Extra gamma isn't confined to one tradition: experienced practitioners of Vipassana, Himalayan Yoga mantra meditation and Isha Shoonya (16 in each group) all had more 60–110 Hz gamma over the back of the head than 16 non-meditators, rising modestly with hours of practice, a difference the authors read as a lasting trait rather than something switched on only during meditation, though the groups were compared only once ([Braboszcz et al., 2017](/gamma-three-meditation-traditions/)). Even a single session of loving-kindness meditation raised gamma activity in the amygdala and hippocampus of people with implanted electrodes ([Maher et al., 2025](/neurological-substrates-of-meditation/)).

  Other practices look different. In a case study of jhana meditation, an experienced meditator's brain showed reduced activity in areas tied to outward awareness and increased activity in reward areas ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)), a pattern the authors note differs from loving-kindness and Transcendental Meditation studies. A more detailed case study, which scanned another expert dozens of times as he moved through all eight jhanas, linked these states to less compartmentalized, more interconnected brain networks ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). Letting go of the sense of self has its own signature in experienced meditators: weaker beta rhythms in prefrontal and parietal regions ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/); [2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)). And in experienced meditators, the brain's inward-facing and outward-facing systems, which usually work against each other, were less opposed during nondual awareness than during focused attention ([Josipovic et al., 2012](/nondual-awareness-brain-networks/)). Followed moment to moment, the jhana meditator's brain switched between recurring states more often than in control tasks, rather than settling into stillness ([Treves et al., 2025](/jhana-dynamic-brain-states/)). Not every deep state raises brain rhythms: when 30 experienced meditators aimed for "thoughtless emptiness," overall EEG power fell below rest, including fewer slow waves, the opposite of drowsiness ([Hinterberger et al., 2014](/emptiness-meditation-eeg/)), and in one advanced meditator, alpha waves faded in the 40 seconds before each reported cessation, a brief gap in consciousness ([Chowdhury et al., 2023](/meditation-cessation-eeg/)). The meditators who let go of the sense of self in Dor-Ziderman's study showed a different rhythm when they tried to step outside time and space: more theta in regions the authors tie to the sense of the body ([Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2013](/timelessness-meditation-meg/)). A later MEG study from the same lab found that in 38 experienced meditators, death-related words didn't silence the brain's automatic response to their own face, as they had in non-meditators tested earlier, though the comparison group came from that earlier study ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2025](/meditation-death-acceptance-brain/)).

  The default mode network, a set of regions most active during mind wandering and self-related thinking, comes up again and again. When a word briefly interrupted breath meditation in the scanner, the default mode response to its meaning faded sooner in 12 Zen meditators than in 12 non-meditators, though both groups judged the words equally well ([Pagnoni et al., 2008](/zen-meditation-default-mode-words/)). Even without meditation training, silently repeating a single word, the core of mantra meditation, lowered activity across much of the cortex, mainly in the default mode network, with no area becoming significantly more active, in 23 non-meditators repeating the word in 21-second blocks ([Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2015](/silent-word-repetition-brain-deactivation/)). After an 8-week MBSR course, people focusing on their present experience rather than on what personality words said about them showed less midline prefrontal activity and more in the insula and lateral prefrontal cortex than people scanned before the course, and the insula no longer moved in step with the midline, though each group was scanned once and the groups weren't directly compared ([Farb et al., 2007](/narrative-experiential-self-focus/)). And a three-day mindfulness retreat strengthened the resting link between the default mode network and a self-regulation region, a change tied to lower inflammation four months later ([Creswell et al., 2016](/mindfulness-connectivity-inflammation/)).

  Brief training can change how the brain handles pain: after four 20-minute mindfulness lessons, meditating during painful heat reduced activity in the part of the brain where the pain signal arrives, and greater relief went with more activity in the anterior insula, anterior cingulate and orbitofrontal cortex, in a small study with no control group ([Zeidan et al., 2011](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/)); a randomized study comparing mindfulness with sham meditation and a placebo cream found that meditating lowered activity in the thalamus and periaqueductal gray, a midbrain pain-control area, more than either did, and that relief went with orbitofrontal and subgenual cingulate activity, while placebo relief went with quieter sensory cortex and sham relief only with slower breathing ([Zeidan et al., 2015](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/)); in a later randomized study, meditators whose pain fell most showed the thalamus falling most out of step with the precuneus ([Riegner et al., 2023](/mindfulness-pain-thalamus-default-mode/)). A brief instruction can shift craving-related activity too: when smokers new to meditation mindfully attended to smoking pictures rather than just looking at them, a craving-linked area at the base of the frontal lobes (the subgenual anterior cingulate) quieted and fell out of step with the insula and striatum, though their craving eased only slightly ([Westbrook et al., 2013](/mindful-attention-smoking-cues-brain/)). In 26 smokers, two weeks of integrative body–mind training raised resting activity in the anterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex, though these changes didn't track how much less people smoked ([Tang et al., 2013](/brief-meditation-smoking-reduction/)). In a randomized trial of a month-long mindfulness app for smokers, the posterior cingulate's response to smoking pictures fell no more than with a standard quit app, but among the 33 mindfulness users, those whose response fell more cut down more ([Janes et al., 2019](/mindfulness-app-smoking-cue-reactivity/)). Over a three-month retreat, practitioners' brain response to the first of two quickly following targets (the P3b) shrank, and those whose response shrank most generally got best at catching the second ([Slagter et al., 2007](/meditation-retreat-attentional-blink/)).

  Meditation also changes how the brain responds to emotional material. While generating compassion, 15 long-term Tibetan Buddhist practitioners' insula responded more than that of 15 novices to emotional sounds, most of all to a woman in distress, and regions tied to understanding others' minds, such as the right temporoparietal junction, responded more too, though the groups were compared only once ([Lutz et al., 2008](/compassion-meditation-insula-emotional-sounds/)). Practice can also change behavior: two weeks of compassion training increased altruistic giving, and the change was linked to shifts in brain responses to suffering ([Weng et al., 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)). Training can change the amygdala, which flags emotionally important events, even when people aren't meditating. In a small randomized trial, eight weeks of mindful-attention training lowered the right amygdala's response to emotional photos more than a health discussion class did, while compassion training showed no clear change ([Desbordes et al., 2012](/mindful-attention-compassion-amygdala/)). In a larger trial, MBSR participants' right amygdala responded less to positive pictures than that of people in a matched health program, though not significantly less to negative ones, and long-term meditators showed the same smaller response to positive pictures; after MBSR, the amygdala also worked more closely with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, a region that helps keep it in check ([Kral et al., 2018](/mindfulness-amygdala-reactivity/)). And in 14 adults with social anxiety disorder scanned before and after an MBSR course, turning to the breath while reading harsh beliefs about themselves engaged parietal and visual attention areas more after the course, though the study had no comparison group ([Goldin and Gross, 2010](/mbsr-social-anxiety-negative-self-beliefs/)).

  Changes to brain structure are a different question. A 2014 meta-analysis of 21 structural brain-imaging studies, most of them one-time comparisons of long-term meditators with non-meditators, found differences recurring in a handful of regions, including the front of the prefrontal cortex, the anterior cingulate cortex, the insula and the hippocampus, but the authors' adjusted average difference was modest, and the smallest studies reported the largest effects, a sign that negative results may have gone unpublished ([Fox et al., 2014](/meditation-brain-structure-meta-analysis/)). In one such comparison, 22 people who had meditated for an average of 24 years had more gray matter than matched non-meditators in one right orbitofrontal region and a larger right hippocampus, but their brains were no larger overall, and gray matter didn't rise with years of practice ([Luders et al., 2009](/long-term-meditators-hippocampus-gray-matter/)). The first such study of loving-kindness meditators found more gray matter in the right angular gyrus and right posterior parahippocampal gyrus of 10 men with at least five years of practice than in 15 beginners, but among the meditators, more practice went with less angular gyrus gray matter, not more ([Leung et al., 2013](/loving-kindness-gray-matter/)). Among 26 stressed adults taking MBSR, with no comparison group, those whose stress fell most showed the largest drops in gray matter density in part of the right amygdala, though the amygdala didn't change on average ([Hölzel et al., 2010](/stress-reduction-amygdala-gray-matter/)). An influential early study reported that gray matter density rose in the hippocampus and four other areas after an eight-week MBSR course, but it had only 16 trainees, no random assignment and a wait-list comparison ([Hölzel et al., 2011](/mindfulness-gray-matter-density/)). In the largest randomized trial of its kind, which tested the same regions, eight weeks of MBSR changed gray matter no more than a matched wellness course or a waitlist ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)), even though one of the two trials it combined had found the changes in amygdala function described above. A short course may change how the brain responds without measurably changing its structure. Longer, more focused training may be different. Over nine months, separate modules of attention, compassion and perspective-taking practice were each linked to cortical thickening in different brain regions, and the changes tracked individual gains in the trained skills ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)).

  Whether meditation slows the brain's aging is still open. In a one-time comparison of 50 long-term meditators and 50 non-meditators aged 24 to 77, older people had less gray matter in both groups, but the link with age was weaker in meditators; differences in specific regions appeared only without the study's strict statistical correction ([Luders et al., 2015](/meditation-age-gray-matter-decline/)). When the question was put to a randomized test, 18 months of meditation in 137 healthy adults aged 65 and over left gray matter volume in the anterior cingulate and insula declining no differently than English lessons or no training did, and blood flow there held up slightly better with meditation, but not significantly ([Chételat et al., 2022](/meditation-older-adults-brain-volume/)).

  Most of these studies are small, and many compare experienced meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time, so they map what meditation looks like in the brain more than they prove how it changes it. The two large randomized trials that looked for lasting structural change, one over eight weeks and one over 18 months, found none beyond what their comparison groups showed.
