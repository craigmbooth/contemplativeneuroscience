theme_description: |
  What happens in the brain during meditation? This theme collects studies that use neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques to map how contemplative states modulate neural activity. From changes in activation during pain, to shifts in attention-related networks and salience processing, these findings highlight the diverse ways meditation reshapes brain function. Importantly, the observed changes are often specific to the kind of meditative practice used—whether focused attention, open presence, or compassion training—suggesting that different intentions may sculpt the brain in different ways. This research offers a window into the elusive terrain of inner experience by grounding it in measurable neural signatures.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation produces distinct, measurable brain states, and they depend on the kind of practice.** Long-term practitioners of loving-kindness and compassion meditation generated unusually high-amplitude gamma waves, which rose with years of practice and stayed elevated at rest ([Lutz et al., 2004](/high-amplitude-gamma-synchrony/)). Even a single session of loving-kindness meditation raised gamma activity in the amygdala and hippocampus of people with implanted electrodes ([Maher et al., 2025](/neurological-substrates-of-meditation/)).

  Other practices look different. In a case study of jhana meditation, an experienced meditator's brain showed reduced activity in areas tied to outward awareness and increased activity in reward areas ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)), a pattern the authors note differs from loving-kindness and Transcendental Meditation studies. Practice can also change behavior: two weeks of compassion training increased altruistic giving, and the change was linked to shifts in brain responses to suffering ([Weng et al., 2013](/compassion-alters-altruism/)).

  Changes to brain structure are a different question. In the largest randomized trial of its kind, eight weeks of MBSR changed gray matter no more than a matched wellness course or a waitlist ([Kral et al., 2022](/mindfulness-brain-structure/)), even though earlier analyses of the same participants found changes in brain function. A short course may change how the brain responds without measurably changing its structure. Longer, more focused training may be different. Over nine months, separate modules of attention, compassion and perspective-taking practice were each linked to cortical thickening in different brain regions, and the changes tracked individual gains in the trained skills ([Valk et al., 2017](/social-brain-plasticity/)).

  Most of these studies are small, and several compare experts with novices at a single point in time, so they map what meditation looks like in the brain more than they prove how it changes it.
