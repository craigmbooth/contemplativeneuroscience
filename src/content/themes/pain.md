question: "Can meditation change how we feel pain?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and pain: how expert meditators experience pain in the brain, and trials of mindfulness programs for chronic pain."
theme_description: |
  Pain is more than a signal from the body. How much something hurts depends partly on attention, expectation and emotion, and pain researchers distinguish how *intense* a pain feels from how *unpleasant* it is. A Buddhist teaching, the Sallatha Sutta, makes a similar distinction: a person in pain is struck by a first arrow, the sensation itself, and then by a second, their distress about it, and practice is said to remove the second arrow rather than the first.

  This theme gathers studies that test whether meditation changes pain: brain scans of experienced meditators receiving painful heat, and randomized trials and meta-analyses of mindfulness programs for people living with chronic pain, including those who also misuse prescribed opioids.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation may change how much pain bothers people more than how strongly they feel it, and mindfulness programs ease chronic pain modestly, but the evidence here is still thin: one brain study of expert meditators and a few trials and meta-analyses, nearly all relying on pain that people rated themselves.** In the lab, 14 long-term Buddhist practitioners with over 10,000 hours of practice rated painful heat as just as intense as 14 novices did, but less unpleasant. Before the heat arrived, the experts showed less anticipatory activity in the brain's salience network, and their amygdala responses faded faster over repeated trials. The study compared two groups at one point in time, so it can't show that practice caused the differences ([Lutz et al., 2013](/activation-during-anticipation-pain/)).

  For chronic pain, the evidence comes mostly from programs such as mindfulness-based stress reduction. A review that kept only trials comparing meditation with programs matched for time and attention found that mindfulness programs reduced pain slightly more (effect size 0.33), but that result came from only four trials, and the benefit was uneven: pain from irritable bowel syndrome improved significantly, while musculoskeletal pain did not ([Goyal et al., 2014](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/)). A larger meta-analysis of randomized trials across psychiatric and pain conditions found mindfulness better than no treatment for pain and no different from other therapies, and its authors concluded that the evidence supports using mindfulness for pain conditions ([Goldberg et al., 2018](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/)).

  One trial looked at people with long-term chronic pain who were also misusing their prescribed opioids. Among 250 adults in primary care, eight weeks of Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE), which combines mindfulness with reappraisal and savoring, eased pain severity by 0.49 points more than a support group did, and pain's interference with daily life by 1.07 points more, on 0–10 scales, while also cutting opioid misuse. Over a third of participants dropped out, and the lead author wrote MORE's manual ([Garland et al., 2022](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/)).

  The effects on chronic pain are small to moderate, and against established treatments mindfulness has not been shown to do better. No study here has yet tested, in a trial, whether training lowers how unpleasant pain feels in the way the expert meditators' ratings suggest.
