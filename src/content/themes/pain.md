question: "Can meditation change how we feel pain?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and pain: how expert meditators experience pain in the brain, and trials of mindfulness programs for chronic pain."
theme_description: |
  Pain is more than a signal from the body. How much something hurts depends partly on attention, expectation and emotion, and pain researchers distinguish how *intense* a pain feels from how *unpleasant* it is. A Buddhist teaching, the Sallatha Sutta, makes a similar distinction: a person in pain is struck by a first arrow, the sensation itself, and then by a second, their distress about it, and practice is said to remove the second arrow rather than the first.

  This theme gathers studies that test whether meditation changes pain: brain scans of beginners and of long-term meditators receiving painful heat in the lab, and randomized trials and meta-analyses of mindfulness programs for people living with chronic pain, from back pain in younger and older adults to pain alongside opioid misuse.
research_summary: |
  **Meditation can lower pain in the lab, even after a few short lessons, and mindfulness courses ease chronic pain modestly, about as much as cognitive behavioral therapy, but the clinical effects are small, the evidence for them is rated low quality, and only one lab study has compared meditation with a sham meditation and a placebo.** In the lab, brief training is enough to change pain while people meditate. Fifteen healthy beginners who had four 20-minute lessons in breath-focused meditation rated painful heat as 40% less intense and 57% less unpleasant while meditating than while resting, with less activity where the pain signal arrives in the brain; there was no control group ([Zeidan et al., 2011](/mindfulness-pain-brain-mechanisms/)). A larger randomized study from the same lab tested the placebo objection directly: among 75 beginners, four 20-minute mindfulness sessions cut heat pain intensity by 27% and unpleasantness by 44% while meditating, more than a "sham" meditation that participants believed was real (8% and 27%) or a placebo cream presented as a new anesthetic (11% and 13%), with a different brain pattern for each; the edges over the sham were statistically modest, in groups of 17 to 20 ([Zeidan et al., 2015](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/)). In a later randomized study with 40 beginners, the same training cut ratings of painful heat by about a third while people meditated, against no improvement in a group that had listened to an audiobook and simply rested in the scanner. The meditators with the most relief were those whose thalamus, the gateway for pain signals, fell most out of step with the precuneus, a hub of the self-related default mode network, though that was a correlation across 20 people ([Riegner et al., 2023](/mindfulness-pain-thalamus-default-mode/)).

  Long-term meditators point to a distinction between how intense pain feels and how much it bothers people. During Open Presence, an open, non-reactive awareness, 9 expert Tibetan Buddhist meditators rated painful heat as less unpleasant than 10 novices did, but focusing on a visual cross made no difference, and the experts' intensity ratings changed less than their unpleasantness ([Perlman et al., 2010](/open-monitoring-pain-unpleasantness/)). In a larger group from the same lab, 14 experts with over 10,000 hours of practice rated heat as just as intense as 14 novices did but less unpleasant, showed less anticipatory activity in the brain's salience network before it arrived, and their amygdala responses faded faster over repeated trials ([Lutz et al., 2013](/activation-during-anticipation-pain/)). Both studies compared experts with novices at one point in time, so they can't show that practice caused the differences.

  For chronic pain, the largest trials tested 8-week courses based on mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR). Among 342 adults with chronic low back pain, 61% of those offered MBSR and 58% of those offered group cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) had a meaningful improvement in daily function at 26 weeks, against 44% with usual care, and MBSR's advantage over usual care held at a year; MBSR did no better than CBT, only about half attended most sessions, and the comparison was usual care rather than a matched course ([Cherkin et al., 2016](/mbsr-cbt-chronic-low-back-pain/)). In 282 adults aged 65 and over, a similar program improved disability from back pain only slightly more than a health education course matched for time and attention (1.1 points on a 24-point scale at 8 weeks), and the difference was gone 6 months later; current pain was lower with mindfulness at 6 months, partly because it rose in the control group, whose course never mentioned pain ([Morone et al., 2016](/mindfulness-older-adults-low-back-pain/)).

  Reviews put these trials in context. The broadest review of mindfulness for chronic pain pooled 30 randomized trials with 2,292 adults and found that mindfulness reduced pain slightly more than usual care, waitlists or education groups (effect size 0.32), and eased depression a little (0.15, rated high-quality evidence); but results varied widely between trials, the pain effect shrank to 0.19 in the better-run ones, and the authors rated the pain evidence low quality ([Hilton et al., 2017](/mindfulness-chronic-pain-meta-analysis/)). A review limited to trials with comparison programs matched for time and attention found a similar effect on pain (0.33), but from only four trials, with clear benefits for irritable bowel syndrome and not for musculoskeletal pain ([Goyal et al., 2014](/meditation-stress-meta-analysis/)). A larger meta-analysis across psychiatric and pain conditions found mindfulness better than no treatment for pain and no different from other therapies ([Goldberg et al., 2018](/mindfulness-psychiatric-disorders-meta-analysis/)).

  One trial looked at people with long-term chronic pain who were also misusing their prescribed opioids. Among 250 adults in primary care, eight weeks of Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE), which combines mindfulness with reappraisal and savoring, eased pain severity by 0.49 points more than a support group did, and pain's interference with daily life by 1.07 points more, on 0–10 scales, while also cutting opioid misuse. Over a third of participants dropped out, and the lead author wrote MORE's manual ([Garland et al., 2022](/more-opioid-misuse-chronic-pain/)).

  Nearly all of this evidence rests on pain that people rated themselves, usually knowing which group they were in. The lab studies used healthy volunteers and a few minutes of heat, and only one compared meditation with a sham meditation and a placebo cream, finding mindfulness ahead of both ([Zeidan et al., 2015](/mindfulness-pain-placebo-sham/)). In people with chronic pain, mindfulness courses help about as much as CBT and a little more than usual care or education, but whether that comes from mindfulness itself, rather than from joining a supportive weekly group, is still unclear.
