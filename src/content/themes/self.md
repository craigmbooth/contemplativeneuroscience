title: Meditation and the Sense of Self
theme_key: self
short_title: "The self"
question: "Does meditation change your sense of self?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and the sense of self: the default mode network, absorption states, depersonalization, and the Buddhist idea of non-self."
theme_description: |
  Buddhist traditions teach that there is no fixed, separate self (*anattā*, or non-self), and much contemplative practice aims to see through the feeling that one exists. Other traditions speak instead of a quiet, observing "witness." Either way, many meditators report that, with practice, the ordinary sense of being a bounded "me" can soften, shift or, at times, seem to fall away.

  This theme brings together studies that approach these reports scientifically: brain activity in networks tied to self-related thinking, case studies of deep absorption states in which practitioners describe the boundaries of the self dissolving, and interviews with meditators for whom changes in the sense of self were calm, welcome or deeply unsettling.
research_summary: |
  **The evidence is still thin, but meditation does seem able to loosen the everyday sense of self, in ways that can be welcome or distressing.** Brain studies point to the default mode network, which is most active during self-related thinking and mind wandering. In experienced Insight meditators, its activity was lower during three kinds of meditation than during an active thinking task, a contrast not seen in non-meditators, though the groups were compared only once ([Garrison et al., 2015](/reduced-dmn-active-task/)). Recordings of the brain's magnetic fields add detail. When 12 long-term Vipassana meditators moved from thinking about themselves to simply experiencing the present, fast gamma activity fell over the front of the brain, and when they then tried to experience the present without being "in the center," slower beta activity fell across a network of prefrontal and parietal regions, most of all in the four who described the sense of ownership dropping away ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/)). A follow-up centered on one meditator with about 40 years of practice, who dissolved his felt boundary between self and world in steps; as it faded, beta rhythms weakened in parietal regions tied to the sense of self and body, and a smaller version of the pattern appeared when the team reanalyzed 10 meditators from the earlier study ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)). In a small one-time comparison, a brain response called the P300 was larger for one's own face than for a close friend's in non-meditators, but this self-bias looked smaller in 11 long-term loving-kindness meditators, especially those who had practiced longest, though the direct group comparison was only a trend ([Trautwein et al., 2016](/loving-kindness-self-bias/)).

  Case studies of jhana, the deep absorption states of Buddhist practice, go further. As one experienced meditator moved through the eight jhanas, activity fell, compared with rest, in regions tied to outward awareness, inner speech and orientation, which the authors link to the sense of personal boundaries ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)). In another expert, brain networks became less compartmentalized and more interconnected, most of all in the formless jhanas that tradition describes as the self falling away, with the default mode network becoming more connected with the rest of the brain ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). Each of these jhana studies followed a single person.

  The same loosening can be unsettling. Among 59 kinds of difficulty described by 60 Western Buddhist meditators who had struggled with their practice, one of seven broad domains was changes in the sense of self, including depersonalization, a feeling of being detached from oneself ([Lindahl et al., 2017](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/)). Yet all six long-term Transcendental Meditation practitioners interviewed in an older study had experienced depersonalization, often in daily life, generally without the anxiety seen in clinical cases; the author argues that whether people read it as calm "witnessing" or as a sign of going mad shapes whether it distresses them ([Castillo, 1990](/depersonalization-and-meditation/)).

  A review that treats well-being as a set of trainable skills counts insight, seeing how beliefs and thoughts shape one's sense of self, as one of four, though its authors note that research on the meditation practices meant to train it is limited ([Dahl et al., 2020](/well-being-four-skills/)).

  Most of this evidence comes from single cases, small one-session experiments, one-time comparisons and interviews, and none of these studies tracked a person's sense of self before and after training, so they describe what these changes can look like more than they show how often meditation brings them about.
