title: Meditation and the Sense of Self
theme_key: self
short_title: "The self"
question: "Does meditation change your sense of self?"
image_format: webp
meta_description: "Research on meditation and the sense of self: the default mode network, absorption states, depersonalization, and the Buddhist idea of non-self."
theme_description: |
  Buddhist traditions teach that there is no fixed, separate self (*anattā*, or non-self), and much contemplative practice aims to see through the feeling that one exists. Other traditions speak instead of a quiet, observing "witness." Either way, many meditators report that, with practice, the ordinary sense of being a bounded "me" can soften, shift or, at times, seem to fall away.

  This theme brings together studies that approach these reports scientifically: brain activity in networks tied to self-related thinking, case studies of deep absorption states in which practitioners describe the boundaries of the self dissolving, and interviews with meditators for whom changes in the sense of self were calm, welcome or deeply unsettling.
research_summary: |
  **The evidence is still thin, but meditation does seem able to loosen the everyday sense of self, in ways that can be welcome or distressing.** Two reviews give the question shape. One proposes that mindfulness builds self-awareness, self-regulation and self-transcendence by changing how brain networks for the narrative self and the present-moment self work together, while noting that key parts were still untested ([Vago and Silbersweig, 2012](/s-art-mindfulness-framework/)). Another, comparing meditation with psychedelic drugs, argues that "self-loss" is not one thing: self-related thoughts, the felt ownership of one's body and the sense of where one is can each fade separately, and a passing state of self-loss is not the same as a lasting change in who one is ([Millière et al., 2018](/meditation-psychedelics-self-loss/)).

  Brain studies of experienced meditators point to the default mode network, which is most active during self-related thinking and mind wandering. In experienced Insight meditators, its activity was lower during three kinds of meditation than during an active thinking task, a contrast not seen in non-meditators, though the groups were compared only once ([Garrison et al., 2015](/reduced-dmn-active-task/)). In 22 experienced meditators, the brain's inward-facing and outward-facing systems, which usually work against each other, were less opposed during nondual awareness, a practice that aims to hold inner and outer experience together, than during focused attention ([Josipovic et al., 2012](/nondual-awareness-brain-networks/)). Recordings of the brain's magnetic fields add detail. When 12 long-term Vipassana meditators moved from thinking about themselves to simply experiencing the present, fast gamma activity fell over the front of the brain, and when they then tried to experience the present without being "in the center," slower beta activity fell across a network of prefrontal and parietal regions ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2013](/mindfulness-induced-selflessness/)). A follow-up centered on one meditator with about 40 years of practice, who dissolved his felt boundary between self and world in steps; as it faded, beta rhythms weakened in parietal regions tied to the sense of self and body ([Dor-Ziderman et al., 2016](/self-boundaries-beta-waves/)). In a small one-time comparison, a brain response called the P300 was larger for one's own face than for a close friend's in non-meditators, but this self-bias looked smaller in 11 long-term loving-kindness meditators, though the direct group comparison was only a trend ([Trautwein et al., 2016](/loving-kindness-self-bias/)). The default mode network may matter for pain, too: in a randomized brain-imaging study of 40 beginners, those whose pain eased most while meditating after four short lessons showed the thalamus decoupling most from the precuneus, a default mode network hub, and the most quieting of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, which the authors read as letting go of self-related appraisal of pain, though no one measured self-related thinking directly ([Riegner et al., 2023](/mindfulness-pain-thalamus-default-mode/)).

  What dissolution feels like has been studied directly too. Interviews with 46 meditators trained to alternate between keeping and dissolving the boundary between self and world found that their accounts varied mainly along one scale of depth, on which agency, attention, sense of location and the sense of being an observer faded together, and that relaxing and letting go, rather than effort, went with the deepest states ([Nave et al., 2021](/self-boundary-dissolution-letting-go/)).

  Case studies of single experts go furthest. As one experienced meditator moved through the eight jhanas, the deep absorption states of Buddhist practice, activity fell, compared with rest, in regions tied to outward awareness, inner speech and orientation ([Hagerty et al., 2013](/ecstatic-meditation-reward-system/)). In another expert, brain networks became less compartmentalized and more interconnected, most of all in the formless jhanas that tradition describes as the self falling away ([Chowdhury et al., 2025](/jhana-brain-networks/)). A Tibetan Buddhist teacher with more than 50,000 hours of practice who reported awareness with no thoughts, sensations or sense of self in the last minutes of a scanned meditation showed more frontal theta activity and weaker connections at the back of the default mode network in those minutes ([Winter et al., 2020](/content-free-awareness-expert-meditator/)). Each of these studies followed a single person.

  In beginners, the shift is more modest. Decentering, seeing thoughts and feelings as passing events rather than as part of who one is, can be taught in a single session: when 30 non-meditators viewed imagined stressful events this way rather than immersing themselves, the brain maps suggested less engagement of self-related regions and more of control regions, though direct comparisons of the two strategies found few differences and stress ratings barely changed ([Lebois et al., 2015](/decentering-imagined-stress-fmri/)). In 38 adults with generalized anxiety disorder, an 8-week MBSR course raised self-reported decentering more than a stress-management class, and that gain statistically accounted for MBSR's larger drop in anxiety ([Hoge et al., 2015](/mbsr-anxiety-decentering/)). But when 229 beginners rated their thoughts before and after daily practice during nine months of training, each of four meditations shifted experience in its own way, and none changed how much people thought about themselves ([Kok and Singer, 2017](/four-meditations-fingerprints/)).

  The loosening can also be unsettling. Among 59 kinds of difficulty described by 60 Western Buddhist meditators who had struggled with their practice, one of seven broad domains was changes in the sense of self, including depersonalization, a feeling of being detached from oneself ([Lindahl et al., 2017](/meditation-adverse-effects-survey/)). Yet all six long-term Transcendental Meditation practitioners interviewed in an older study had experienced depersonalization, often in daily life, generally without the anxiety seen in clinical cases; the author argues that whether people read it as calm "witnessing" or as a sign of going mad shapes whether it distresses them ([Castillo, 1990](/depersonalization-and-meditation/)). A review that treats well-being as a set of trainable skills counts insight, seeing how beliefs and thoughts shape one's sense of self, as one of four, though its authors note that research on the meditation practices meant to train it is limited ([Dahl et al., 2020](/well-being-four-skills/)).

  Most of this evidence comes from experienced meditators compared at a single point in time, single cases and interviews. The few studies that followed people through training measured narrower things, such as decentering or how often people thought about themselves, so the research describes what changes in the sense of self can look like more than it shows how often, or how lastingly, meditation brings them about.
