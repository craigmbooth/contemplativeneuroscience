theme_description: |
  Meditation has long been linked to better rest, but how exactly does it affect the sleeping brain and body? This theme brings together studies exploring how contemplative practice influences sleep quality, architecture, and related physiological processes. From improving subjective sleep satisfaction to altering inflammatory markers and cognitive control mechanisms, these findings show how meditation might help us fall asleep faster, sleep more deeply, and wake more refreshed. They also hint at something deeper: that our waking practices shape our sleeping mind.
research_summary: |
  **Mindfulness meditation improves sleep in randomized trials, especially how people experience their sleep.** A meta-analysis of 18 randomized trials in adults with sleep problems found that mindfulness programs improved self-reported sleep quality more than time-matched classes, with a small effect after the programs and a medium one 5–12 months later, but showed no clear difference from established treatments such as CBT-I ([Rusch et al., 2019](/mindfulness-sleep-meta-analysis/)). An earlier meta-analysis of six small trials (330 participants) found improvements in sleep quality ratings and total time awake; against a wait-list or education classes, mindfulness also shortened the time to fall asleep and improved sleep efficiency, though each of those comparisons rested on two trials, and the two trials against sleep medication or CBT-I tended to favor those treatments ([Gong et al., 2016](/mindfulness-insomnia-meta-analysis/)). In older adults with moderate sleep problems, a six-week mindfulness course improved sleep quality more than a matched sleep hygiene course, with a large effect size, and also reduced fatigue and depression ([Black et al., 2015](/meditation-sleep-older-adults/)). In adults with chronic insomnia, both MBSR and a mindfulness program combined with behavioral sleep techniques (MBTI) cut time spent awake at night by about 44 minutes, and MBTI produced the strongest long-term reduction in insomnia severity ([Ong et al., 2014](/mindfulness-chronic-insomnia/)). In a larger trial of 127 adults aged 50–80 with poor sleep, MBTI reduced insomnia severity more than a matched sleep education and exercise program, but self-rated sleep quality improved equally in both groups, and wrist-worn and home sleep recordings showed no larger change in the mindfulness group ([Perini et al., 2023](/mbti-insomnia-older-adults/)). A meditation app helped too, modestly: in 263 adults with insomnia symptoms, eight weeks on the Calm app reduced bedtime arousal, daytime sleepiness and, slightly, fatigue more than a wait-list, though every measure was self-reported, sleep diaries were kept only by the app group, and the lead author was Calm's Director of Science ([Huberty et al., 2021](/calm-app-sleep-disturbance/)).

  Objective measures are more modest: in the insomnia trial, sleep recordings changed less than people's own reports. In a small randomized trial of antidepressant users with lingering sleep complaints, an 8-week MBCT course reduced total time awake at night compared with a wait-list, in both sleep-lab recordings and diaries, but didn't change deep or light sleep, and in the lab part of the difference came from the wait-list group sleeping worse ([Britton et al., 2012](/mbct-sleep-antidepressant-users/)). Studies of long-term practitioners hint at deeper effects, though. Vipassana meditators had more slow wave and REM sleep than non-meditators across age groups, and kept more deep sleep into their 50s ([Pattanashetty et al., 2010](/vipassana-enhanced-sleep/)), but that comparison can't rule out other lifestyle differences. A high-density EEG study found that long-term meditators had about 35% more gamma activity over the back of the head during non-REM sleep, rising with their hours of daily practice, though their sleep structure looked the same as non-meditators' ([Ferrarelli et al., 2013](/meditators-sleep-gamma/)). A follow-up in 24 of the same meditators found that after a single eight-hour day of mindfulness or compassion meditation, slower brain rhythms rose over the front and left side of the head during that night's non-REM sleep, while sleep length and stages didn't change; a direct comparison with non-meditators narrowly missed significance ([Dentico et al., 2016](/meditation-day-sleep-brain-waves/)). Whether meditation can stand in for sleep is far less settled: in a small one-time comparison, seven long-term yogis slept about 5.2 hours a day against 7.8 in 23 non-meditators, but when kept awake all night the yogis were generally too tired to meditate, which the authors take as evidence against meditation largely replacing sleep ([Kaul et al., 2010](/meditation-reaction-time-sleep-need/)).
